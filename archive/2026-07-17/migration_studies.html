Journal of Refugee Studies

From survival to sustainability: a spatial implementation timeline for socially sustainable refugee camps

Eman Ismail, Aysen Ciravoglu

Full text
Refugee camps are planned as temporary humanitarian spaces; however, many evolve into complex and long-term settlements. This paper explores how camp transformations occur and how this transformation can be guided towards achieving socially sustainable communities. The paper shows that refugees’ everyday practices and spatial actions to meet their spatial needs contribute to the informal urbanization of camps. These acts challenge the existing assumption of temporality in humanitarian governance. Therefore, a shift from reactive management by humanitarian and governmental actors to more inclusive, long-term planning is required. Accordingly, the study develops a timeline framework based on expert interviews and document analysis that traces camp development through three overlapping phases: survival, liveable, and socially sustainable communities. The proposed framework offers both a conceptual and practical tool for aligning emergency response with community development, which places dignity, participation, and social resilience at the centre of refugee camp design and policy.

Evolving Pathways of Departure and Return in Rural Thailand

Jonathan Rigg, Wasana La‐orngplew, Monchai Phongsiri, Albert Salamanca, Mattara Sripun

Full text
This paper draws on a forty‐year longitudinal (1982–2022) village study of Thailand to reflect on how circulation persists, but in new ways. We trace evolving migration flows over a period during which Thailand progressed from low‐income to upper‐middle‐income status, and Non Tae and Tha Song Korn from semi‐subsistence rice‐growing communities to increasingly prosperous settlements with multi‐stranded livelihoods. Villagers accumulated considerable educational capital and left the villages in growing numbers to find work in other places and sectors. They also, however, in large part also returned, raising questions about linear and one‐directional theories of agrarian and mobility transition. It is argued that departure and return continue to be co‐produced in response to conditions in the villages and the wider Thai economy. Circulation is a central element in understanding Thailand's truncated agrarian transition, and vice vera.

Changing Spatial Patterns of Internal Migration in Germany: Behavioural Shifts or Population Ageing Effect?

Markus Dörflinger, Tamilwai Kolowa, Nico Stawarz, Heiko Rüger

Full text
A key characteristic of developed societies is population ageing, driven by low fertility and rising life expectancy. In this context, migration has become an increasingly important driver of population change and its spatial distribution. While several studies have examined the impact of population ageing on overall migration intensities, far fewer have investigated how regional population ageing affects spatial patterns of internal migration. Using data for 400 German districts, we employ a decomposition analysis to disentangle how shifts in age composition and age‐specific migration behaviour have shaped migration flows across the urban–rural continuum from 1991 to 2024. Our findings indicate that changing spatial patterns of internal migration have been driven by behavioural shifts, particularly among young adults. However, shifts in age composition have become more important in recent years. In particular, the decrease in migration to large cities since 2011 is largely due to population ageing in urban, intermediate and rural counties.

Displacement as a Cultural Repertoire for Resilience in Chronic Crisis, in Burundi

Lidewyde H. Berckmoes

Full text
Prior to a new outbreak of violence in Burundi in 2015, more than 10000 Burundians had already crossed borders in search of refuge. The anticipatory displacement signalled the need to rethink displacement as contextualised in the country's long history of conflict and decline. In this article I take a life course perspective based on longitudinal ethnographic research. I show that displacement has become deeply entangled with other forms of mobility, beyond dualities of forced versus voluntary migration or coping versus adapting. I propose that past experiences have realised and transformed the collective meaning of displacement in Burundian culture. Today, displacement should be understood as a sedimented, cultural resource and framework. This conceptualisation enables situating displacement practices in lives and communities in chronic crisis. Rather than displacement being central to life projects, it is a rather ordinary cultural repertoire of action employed for resilience.

Disruption and Adjustment. Labour Market Performance of Ukrainian War Refugees in Poland in the Context of Pre‐War Migration

Agata Górny, Paweł Kaczmarczyk, Jeremy Caldeira

Full text
In the context of the exceptionally high labour market activity of Ukrainian war refugees in Poland, this article examines its drivers in mid‐2022, shortly after the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Adapting the aspiration‐capabilities framework to economic integration outcomes, and using data from a Poland‐wide survey of Ukrainian nationals, it evaluates the role of war refugees' earlier migration experience and positions their labour market outcomes alongside those of pre‐war migrants (including recent migrants). Our analyses reveal that, in addition to lower employment rates, war refugees had less stable jobs and a more polarised occupational structure. Both capabilities—social, financial and human capital—and aspirations, related to skills usage and migratory plans (but not prior migration experience) influenced war refugees' labour market outcomes. Based on our results, we point to the importance of structural factors in forced migrants' labour market integration, and discuss the role of the temporary protection framework against this background.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Policing, Race, and the Re-Making of the Diverse City

Shana Almeida, Al Cunningham Rogers, Danillo Onetto

Full text
Police reform has been a cornerstone of activism in Toronto for decades. Activists, organizations, and community groups have applied pressure to politicians, government officials, and other decision-making bodies specifically for them to intervene in a pattern of racialized violence on the part of the Toronto Police force. At the same time, external interventions of any kind have been vehemently resisted by Toronto Police and its Board of Commissioners. This paper will address the question: How does Toronto’s municipal government respond to demands to address racialized police violence in a city that is defined by its diversity and racially harmonious sensibilities? Through a detailed genealogy of Toronto government records and centering the killing of Albert Johnson as an “event,” this paper reveals how Toronto government policies and practices addressing racism and racialized violence in policing become the mechanisms through which to continuously obscure and protect such violence against public accountability and to recirculate Toronto as the city without racism. The paper concludes by drawing parallels to and thus complicating the idea of civilian oversight bodies as a potential solution to police racism, as advocated by politicians, government officials, activists, organizations, and community groups across Toronto and beyond.

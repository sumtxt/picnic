International Organization

Collective Deterrence

Scott Wolford, Joshua Landry, Kevin Galambos

Full text
We analyze a model of collective deterrence in which (1) one state may challenge a status quo defended by a great power and an arbitrary number of partners and (2) defender war costs are private information. We show first that the great power’s intra-coalition dominance shapes which cooperative failure, free riding, or uncertainty over others’ willingness to fight may weaken deterrence. Second, increasing the great power’s war payoffs increases both deterrence and partner free riding. Third, the extent of great power dominance determines whether a larger or more capable coalition enhances or weakens deterrence and ensures that size and capabilities work in opposite directions. Finally, increasing uncertainty over a great power’s war costs can decrease or increase deterrence, depending on the prior strength of its commitment. We also use the model to illuminate differing responses of American allies in Europe and East Asia to the 2016 election of Donald Trump.

Journal of Peace Research

Participation yielding stigmatization? Demobilized rebels' protest involvement and public opinion

Christoph D Sponsel

Full text
While significant literature exists on the political participation of demobilized rebels in party politics, research on their participation in protests is scarce. However, protests are likely in societies transitioning from conflict, and demobilized rebels are likely participants. If the former rebels carry a negative image, for instance, due to wartime violence and atrocities, their participation may reduce protest support. Based on a survey experiment conducted in Colombia, this article examines whether demobilized rebels' participation in protests alters citizens' attitudes toward protests. Results show that former rebels' participation, particularly during protests involving vandalism, significantly reduces citizens' support for protests, whereas a name change of the rebel successor organization may mitigate this effect. The analysis contributes to our understanding of the extent to which former rebels' political participation is accepted, the effectiveness of stigmatization tactics employed by protest opponents, and how protesting outgroups may mitigate potential stigmatization.

Political consequences of ethnic rebel governance: evidence from the Myanmar conflict

Jiwon L Kim

Full text
Is trust for different political actors in ethnic conflict settings competitive in nature? How does rebel governance provision influence the perceived legitimacy of the rebel and other actors in a conflict? This study investigates the political consequences of reported exposure to ethnic rebel governance across Myanmar's ethnic minority areas through an original online survey. It finds that more exposure to legitimacy-enhancing rebel governance, such as education and medical services, is associated with higher trust in the rebels and lower trust in an emerging non-ethnic resistance movement, the National Unity Government (NUG). This is true when the ethnic rebel is not seen as explicitly supporting the NUG. However, exposure to legitimacy-eroding rebel governance, such as violence against civilians or forcible recruitment, has a more nuanced relationship. Although associated with lower trust in the ethnic rebels, it is not associated with higher trust in the NUG. Only the positive records of rebel governance are counted against a competitive political actor, possibly suggesting people's preferential affinity for coethnic rebels.

Of monsters and martyrs: the mutability of public perceptions of international human rights conditions

Brittnee Carter, Clayton Webb

Full text
Can presidents use human rights rhetoric to manipulate public perceptions of foreign countries and build support for foreign policies? Previous research shows that public perceptions of human rights conditions in foreign countries affect public support for military intervention. This makes public perceptions of human rights a potential political tool. Political leaders can weave narratives about human rights conditions in foreign countries to persuade their publics that monsters should be punished and/or martyrs should be aided. Because most people are poorly informed about international affairs, public perceptions of international human rights conditions are ripe for manipulation. Worse, the tendency of the public to focus on negative information makes it easier to shift their perspectives in a negative direction; leaders will find it especially easy to generate public consent for conflictual foreign policies. We use a novel, nationally representative, between subject, pre–post experimental design to test our theory and compare our findings to previous research. The results show that public beliefs about human rights conditions are easy to change and that people are more likely to adjust their beliefs about human rights conditions when they are provided with negative information. Additional analyses show that these changes in perceptions affect public support for changes in trade policy and diplomatic relations with targeted states.

How portraying women's wartime victimization shapes perceptions of their leadership potential

Alexis Ye Eun Jang

Full text
Does portraying women as wartime victims shape citizens’ perceptions of their leadership potential? Conflict-related media coverage disproportionately frames women as victims of war, yet little is known about whether these portrayals shape public attitudes toward women's political authority in security and foreign policy—domains in which women remain significantly underrepresented. Highlighting gendered victimization may increase support for women's representation by casting women as especially attuned to humanitarian suffering and the needs of vulnerable populations. At the same time, such portrayals may activate paternalistic protectionism that undercuts confidence in women's political authority. Analysis of over 15,000 Facebook posts from 17 major news outlets covering conflicts in Afghanistan, Israel–Palestine, and Ukraine–Russia shows that women's wartime suffering is a persistent feature of conflict coverage, consistently outnumbering references to men's victimization and frequently linked to children, sexual violence, and social vulnerability. An original preregistered survey experiment in the United States (N = 995) provides causal evidence that exposure to narratives emphasizing women's wartime suffering increases support for women's leadership in peacebuilding, including conflict resolution and postconflict negotiations. These effects, however, do not extend to support for women's leadership in militarized security domains such as national defense or warfare, revealing an asymmetry in how victimhood shapes gendered leadership evaluations. Exploratory analyses further indicate that although victimization narratives heighten support for women's descriptive and substantive representation, they also strengthen benevolent sexism, which activates stereotypes that women lack the competence associated with hard security roles. Together, these findings suggest that highlighting women's wartime experiences can broaden demand for their inclusion in peacebuilding, yet expanding their authority in militarized security roles will require long-term shifts toward gender-egalitarian norms.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Blueprints and Blind Spots: Sixty Years of Mediation Scholarship and the Design of Peace Processes

Allard Duursma, Govinda Clayton, David Lanz, Marc Werner

Full text
This article reviews 60 years of academic research on peace mediation. Drawing on 395 academic articles published between 1966 and 2025, we assess the field through six core mediation process design questions: why, when, who, how, what, and where . We show that scholarship has generated important insights but has often addressed design questions in isolation, with limited attention to how they interact in practice. The literature on peace mediation is therefore marked by blind spots and conceptual fragmentation, not fully capturing the integrated nature of mediation processes. Organizing existing research through a process design framework allows us to identify key gaps and connections across strands of scholarship. We argue that research should more explicitly address the interdependence of design factors. This perspective clarifies priorities for future mediation research by identifying analytically promising questions and highlighting opportunities for more systematic and comparative inquiry.

International Studies Review

International Relations, Quantum Social Theory, and Scientific Reductionism

Fred Chernoff

Full text
Alex Wendt’s Quantum Mind and IR has sparked a new debate among scholars of international relations about the nature of the objects studied. Wendt points out that other disciplines’ adoption of a quantum framework has enabled them to gain explanatory power. He describes two assumptions that he admits are far from obvious but defends them by detailing the significant intellectual gains and explanatory power they add. Wendt thus argues for the quantum framework by means of an intellectual cost-benefit calculation. Quantum Mind details the benefits but attends less to the costs; the latter are the focus of this paper.

Reconnecting Cities and Nations: Forging Divergent Paths in Global Technology Governance

Efe Sevin, Di Wu

Full text
As cities play increasingly visible roles in international relations and global governance, scholars have proposed multiple explanations for variation in their engagement and influence. This study examines a relatively understudied yet crucial factor: vertical coordination between cities and national governments. Through a structured-focus case comparison of four cities (San Francisco, Austin, Shanghai, and Shenzhen), it traces each city’s representation in governance networks, reputation-building efforts, and mobilization of its tangible and intangible resources across two contrasting approaches to coordination by the United States and China. The findings reveal that coordination functions as a pathway-structuring variable that determines the sequence and logic through which cities establish their presence and argue for their legitimacy. Strongly coordinated cases convert national legitimacy into flagship representational opportunities and sustained reputational gains; less coordinated cases generate visibility through local ecosystems but lack the structural capacity to translate reputational capital into norm-setting roles. These insights contribute to polycentric governance studies, advance scholarship on city diplomacy, and offer practical insights for the design of coordination frameworks in an era of growing urban involvement in global affairs.

International Theory

Ritual, emotion, and change in world politics

Emma Hutchison, Roland Bleiker

Full text
The question of political change – how the nature and the distinguishing features of orders persist or change – is an enduring puzzle in international theory. We engage ensuing debates through the burgeoning literature on the politics of rituals. We proceed in two steps. First, we introduce and build on existing scholarship that presents emotions as central to how rituals create a sense of collective unity. Second and primarily, we then show that emotions are also key to understanding how rituals can disrupt and transform order. To think so appears counterintuitive at first, for it is natural to think of rituals as being associated with tradition, with time-honoured processes and patterns. Drawing on historical and contemporary examples, we show how the very power of rituals to enact and entrench political orders also provides disenfranchised individuals and groups with opportunities to challenge existing power relations. We argue that discomfort – being unsettled – is important for understanding how ritual and political change take place. We do so by linking rituals with shifts in collective feelings. The ensuing affective agency can explain how rituals – and the political orders they manage and sustain – are enacted and change over time.

International Studies Quarterly

Buying Hearts and Minds, Securing Blue Helmets: UN Quick Impact Projects and Violence against Peacekeepers

Jori Breslawski, Jacob D Kathman

Full text
United Nations peacekeeping operations have come under threat, with peacekeepers facing attacks by armed groups. One way that the UN has sought to generate support for its missions is through Quick Impact Projects (QIPs)—small-scale, low-cost, visible projects, carried out in consultation with local communities. Do QIPs increase, decrease, or have no effect on violence against peacekeepers? We argue that QIPs decrease violence against peacekeepers by building support from the civilian population, increasing civilians’ willingness to provide intelligence to peacekeepers and allowing blue helmets to more effectively avoid or thwart subsequent attacks. We test the relationship between QIPs and violence against peacekeepers using geo-located data from the Central African Republic, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mali, and South Sudan. We find that as QIPs in a given district increase, armed group violence against peacekeepers subsequently decreases.

“The many metaphors of silence: Conceptualizing silence as a collaborative structure”

Jenna Norosky

Full text
Abstracts Silence is a powerful metaphor, but functions as a black box within which many distinct social phenomena coexist. Consequently, metaphorical slippage makes it difficult to operationalize silence in empirical research. First, I show the need for greater metaphorical specification in the study of silence through the example of silence as a collaborative structure: a wide-spanning silence appearing as a unitary object which, behind its opacity, is sustained by various intersecting but distinct trajectories of silence and silencing. Empirically understanding these collaborative structures thus requires attentiveness to the interplay between the modalities of silence represented through different metaphors. Next, I demonstrate the utility of this concept for the study of IR through a brief discussion of the global silence on conflict-related sexual violence against men and boys. Considering the issue’s increased recognition, I conclude with a cautionary explication of how the framing of CRSV against men and boys as a “silenced” issue limits approaches to addressing the problem and adversely impacts intra-network advocacy relationships. This underscores the significance of the unique methodological problems with studying silence involving the imposition of the “horizon of expectation”.

Slave Empire: Ottoman Islamic Slavery and State Formation

J C Sharman

Full text
Islamic slavery provided a distinctive, inherently internationalized model of successful state formation that illustrates important variations in institutional diffusion. Focusing on the Ottoman Empire, this paper shows how slaves constituted the military-administrative institutions conventionally associated with the modern state before their European counterparts, in particular, a professional standing army. Reproducing Islamic slavery necessitated enslaving outsiders, and hence the external and internal faces of this institution were co-constitutive. Confounding expectations of the diffusion of superior institutional innovations, Europeans failed to emulate military-administrative slavery, illuminating deep-seated normative barriers to functional adaptation in the West and, more generally, highlighting cultural preconditions for mechanisms of diffusion. Later, an abolitionist “standard of civilization” legitimated European coercive institutional diffusion imposed on the Ottomans and other Muslim powers, ending Islamic slavery and hence a distinctive Islamic political model.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

Centralisation without Independence: The functional scope of informal intergovernmental organisations

David Hagebölling

Full text
Informal intergovernmental organisations (IIGOs) like the G20, the G7, and BRICS have become key pillars of the post-Cold War multilateral system. IIGOs lack a legal foundation and a permanent secretariat or staff—yet states now use them to govern global issues ranging from artificial intelligence to nuclear non-proliferation. This raises a critical question: are IIGOs viable vehicles for supporting sustained and structured state interactions? I address this question by theorising IIGOs’ functional scope. Building on the institutional design literature, I disaggregate the notion of formality into organisational properties (P)—centralisation and independence—and relate these to organisational functions (F). Because IIGOs lack independence from member states but may achieve centralisation, the P-F framework suggests that IIGOs can enhance negotiation stability, information dissemination, and issue linkage. In turn, they struggle to support cooperation through initiation and enforcement. A case probe of the G20’s organisational structure and operations lends plausibility to these conjectures.

Immigration, business, and the state: UK immigration policy from New Labour to Brexit

James Hampshire

Full text
Since the early 2000s, immigration to the UK has become increasingly salient, anti-immigration sentiment has been mobilised, and successive governments have promised to reduce numbers. Yet over this period, immigration policies have enabled net migration to increase to historic highs. This paper examines the role of business in driving expansive immigration policies. Contrary to theories that depict business as an interest group whose main channel of influence is lobbying, the paper argues that in a capitalist democracy such as the UK, business enjoys structural power, generated by mutual dependencies between capital and the state. The paper examines three moments of policy change to trace how electoral logics and business interests interact to shape immigration policies: the Labour Government’s liberalisation of the early 2000s; the Coalition Government’s net migration target; and the Conservative’s post-Brexit immigration regime. The analysis shows how immigration policies are forged by both the noisy politics of elections and a quieter politics of economic interests, which unfolds through technocratic institutions and collaborative relations between political and business elites. The empirical analysis is based on original interviews with politicians, officials, and business representatives, as well as analysis of policy documents, speeches, parliamentary debates, and official minutes.

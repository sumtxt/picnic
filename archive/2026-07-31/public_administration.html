Institutionalized Enforcement of Administrative Compliance: State Regulator Use of Noncompliance Burdens

Mingxu (Emma) Li, Daniel L Fay

Full text
Administrative burden scholarship typically examines rules and regulations affecting access to public benefits during initial citizen-state interactions. Scholars agree that individual burdens function as “hidden politics” because policy changes during implementation through bureaucratic discretion. Less is known about bureaucratic behavior or hidden politics enforced on third-party service providers maintaining compliance. In this article we address this by examining noncompliance burdens imposed on abortion providers to understand if regulators emulate peers’ behavior. Using an institutional theoretical framework and administrative records from the Florida Agency for Health Care Administration (FAHCA) on abortion provider oversight, we construct a directed dyadic event history model using 1540 dyad-year observations to predict isomorphism or divergence of sanction activities across regulatory field offices. We find that regulatory field offices emulate behavior of others based on peer’s previous sanction behavior and oversight workload. We also find that regulators diverge from peers based on the regulatory conditions of their own jurisdiction.

Linking Decision Roles to Knowledge Utilization in US State Governments: A Mixed‐Method Study

Shuping Wang, Yuan (Daniel) Cheng, Chengxin Xu

Full text
The current movement for evidence‐based policymaking advocates the use of scientific knowledge in government. However, in practice, civil servants often prioritize different types of knowledge depending on their decision‐making roles and the context. Using survey responses from 259 civil servants across three US state governments and 36 semistructured interviews, we employ a mixed‐method approach to understand how civil servants strategically utilize various types of knowledge to meet different role requirements and decision‐making responsibilities. Consistent with our hypotheses, our findings indicate that civil servants involved in policy and legislative roles value political knowledge more, while those in contracting roles place greater importance on experiential knowledge. Contrary to our hypothesis, while civil servants in budgeting roles value political knowledge more than their counterparts in other decision roles, they show no such preference for scientific knowledge. Our qualitative interviews provide possible mechanisms for this unexpected finding.

The Matthew Effect in American Generosity? Philanthropic Disparities and the Role of Government Support

Viviana Chiu Sik Wu, Ji Ma, Chao Guo

Full text
This study examines how economic, racial, and ecological dimensions of community disadvantage shape philanthropic donations to local human services nonprofit sectors, and whether government support mitigates these place‐based disparities. Using a novel county‐level panel dataset spanning 2000–2019, we combine descriptive analyses with mixed‐effects regression models to estimate both direct and moderating relationships. We find that per capita private donations are significantly lower in economically deprived communities and significantly higher in rural communities, while minority prominence shows no statistically significant association with private giving. While government funding crowds in private donations overall, its moderating role is context specific. Government support does not attenuate philanthropic disparities linked to economic deprivation or minority prominence, but it crowds in private donations more strongly in rural counties than in urban counties. These findings highlight the contingent role of government support and underscore the need for targeted policy strategies to address philanthropic disparities across communities.

The Rise of Midocracy: How NPM and Post‐ NPM Reform Ideas Recombine in Contemporary Public Organizations

Gustaf Kastberg Weichselberger, Johan Alvehus

Full text
This paper introduces and conceptualizes midocracy as an emergent organizational form in the public sector, characterized by the growth and dominance of a professionalized administrative middle layer. The study argues that midocracy draws on elements associated with both New Public Management (NPM) and post‐NPM reform agendas. While administration is essential for coordination, we show how its unchecked expansion can lead to self‐reinforcing dynamics that distance governance from core operations. Midocracy, we contend, reflects a shift from service delivery to symbolic value signaling, strategic abstraction, and polycentric goal‐setting. Our contribution is both conceptual and diagnostic, offering midocracy as a framework for understanding contemporary administrative expansion and its implications for public sector legitimacy and performance. Finally, we relate and critically examine different explanatory accounts of administrative expansion, highlighting how they interact within the dynamics of midocracy.

Corporate Quasi‐Sovereignty: Big Tech and the Politics of Sovereign Authority in the Digital Age

Chee Hae Chung, Bryce J. Dietrich

Full text
As private technology firms expand their control over critical infrastructures, questions of sovereignty and governance are increasingly displaced from states to corporate actors. Existing scholarship on sovereignty assumes that authority is either monopolized by states or shared through institutionalized public–private arrangements. Yet, recent events, including SpaceX's control of wartime communications in Ukraine, Meta's confrontation with the Australian government, and OpenAI's attempts to shape global AI regulation, demonstrate that firms can exercise discretionary authority without formal delegation, legal accountability, or democratic oversight. This study introduces the concept of Corporate Quasi‐Sovereignty (CQS) to theorize how certain technology firms function as de facto sovereign actors. CQS is defined by the convergence of three enabling dimensions: infrastructural command, executive autonomy, and normative or geopolitical assertion. Drawing on Kingdon's Multiple Streams Framework (MSF), this paper theorizes ‘sovereignty windows’ as the mechanism through which firms internally collapse the problem, policy, and politics streams, enabling unilateral interventions with geopolitical consequences. ‘Stream collapse,’ the internalization of all three MSF streams within a single corporate hierarchy, is operationalized as a boundary condition of MSF, distinct from conventional stream coupling. The ‘conditional policy entrepreneur’ is introduced as the corporate analogue to Kingdon's entrepreneur: a founder‐CEO who exercises this role only when all three CQS enabling conditions simultaneously converge. Drawing on comparative case analyses of Meta, SpaceX, and OpenAI, the article shows how CQS captures both reactive and anticipatory modes of corporate authority, showing that firms now operate as both market actors and quasi‐sovereign agents capable of substituting, bypassing, or preempting state power.

Legislative Pork‐Barrel Funding and Public Policy Outcomes: The Role of Local‐Level Factors

Luna Viana, Alketa Peci

Full text
The growing legislative influence over budget allocations through pork‐barrel funding raises concerns that this type of distributive policy may undermine effectiveness. We examine Brazil's mandatory pork‐barrel system to assess whether funding availability affects health outcomes and how local conditions shape these effects. We focus on municipalities' abilities to attract resources (“funding concentration”) and their autonomy over investment decisions (“funding flexibility”), with expectations conditioned by administrative capacity. Using panel data on 4114 Brazilian municipalities (2015–2021) and controlling for endogeneity and selection bias, we find that pork‐barrel funding is associated with higher short‐term rates of preventable hospitalizations. These effects dissipate over time but reveal how political‐electoral dynamics shape implementation. Such dynamics undermine the expected benefits of administrative capacity, suggesting that political oversight patterns condition policy outcomes. Evidence on funding concentration indicates that, in contexts of politically allocated funding, administrative capacity may facilitate the rapid execution of concentrated, electoral‐driven allocations, which can amplify inefficiencies. These findings highlight the importance of aligning legislative budget governance with local administrative capacity to mitigate the risks of funding fragmentation and ensure more effective governance of distributive policies.

Mapping Organized Crime as a Stakeholder: A Risk‐Based and Responsive Governance Perspective

Giovanni Scirè, Enzo Bivona

Full text
Organized crime (OC) can influence firms through coercion, collusion, financial ties, and supply‐chain relationships, yet these interactions remain underexamined in stakeholder theory. This article conceptualizes organized crime as an analytically relevant but non‐normatively legitimate stakeholder and develops a risk‐based framework combining stakeholder salience, interface channel, interest level, and influence strategy. The framework specifies when OC is likely to command managerial attention and which configurations are expected to pose the greatest risks of value destruction and escalation. Three illustrative Italian cases involving preventive anti‐mafia measures show variation from latent relational exposure, through indirect supply‐chain contamination, to embedded ownership and financial control. By linking these configurations to graduated governance responses, the framework helps managers and regulators calibrate proportionate, revisable, and auditable interventions. It extends stakeholder theory to adversarial actors while clarifying institutional conditions that shape the credibility of prevention and remediation.

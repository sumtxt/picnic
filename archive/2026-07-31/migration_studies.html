“We are not criminals!” Stuckness and existential tactics among residents at a Danish deportation center

Martin Lindhardt, Freja Lindberg Birkelund, Annika Vistisen Lidegaard

Full text
Based on qualitative research in a Danish deportation center, Kærshovedgaard, this article explores residents’ experiences of stuckness while also shedding light on their existential struggles to transform the world into which they were thrown into a world they had a hand in making. In pursuing an analysis of stuckness as a multifaceted experience comprising temporal, spatial, legal, and categorical dimensions, we engage with scholarly literature on liminality and waiting. We further draw on the existential anthropology of Michael Jackson, coupled with Michel de Certeau’s concept of tactical agency in our exploration of how residents, while being faced with overwhelming circumstances, struggled to salvage some semblance of comprehension and control in governing their own lives. We show how the agency of residents was in large part related to negotiations of social positions and statuses other than detainee, or of not being reduced to ascribed identities.

Suspended solidarity: the experience of precarity and the chronopolitics of support in Australia’s asylum regime

Alison Reid

Full text
This article examines how the Australian asylum regime’s chronopolitics, the strategic use of indefinite delays, actively constructs a state of indeterminate liminality for both people seeking asylum and the associated support sector. Drawing on ethnographic data, it is argued that bureaucratic processes function as systems of suffering, where weaponized time serves as a tool of affective violence. The support sector faces a fundamental chronopolitical tension as it navigates a punitive state apparatus. The system’s design co-opts supporters’ sustained commitments, to be consumed by the protracted limbo of the asylum process. This shared temporal condition is characterized by an exhaustion of potentiality, where state-sanctioned stalling prevents those seeking refuge from constructing meaningful futures while simultaneously consuming the agency of the supporter in a cycle of permanent crisis management. The regime’s designed precarity perpetuates this bureaucratic stasis, resulting in suspended solidarity and constituting an embedded form of bureaucratic violence.

Spatial Econometric Analysis of Regional Aging: Evidence From German Counties

Andree Ehlert

Full text
Regional demographic change is widely acknowledged to contribute to economic decline, making an understanding of the determinants of regional population aging crucial for designing effective policy interventions. However, research on these determinants remains limited, particularly at the sub‐national level. This gap is further compounded by challenges related to data availability and endogeneity in econometric studies. To address these issues, we analyse a county‐level panel dataset covering the period 2002–2022 for Germany. Our methodology incorporates spatial econometric techniques to account for unobserved heterogeneity and spatial spillover thereby mitigating potential bias. We carefully address remaining endogeneity concerns and conduct extensive robustness checks. Our findings indicate that new housing construction and educational attainment exert significant influences on the share of the population aged 65 and over, primarily through local, own‐county effects, whereas commuting flows operate mainly as cross‐county spatial spillovers. This empirical evidence not only deepens our understanding of the spatial determinants of regional aging but also highlights new avenues for targeted policy interventions, which we discuss in detail.

No Destination? Unveiling the Traces of the Future Among Young Asylum Seekers in “Waithood”

Paolo Boccagni

Full text
What is the future like for young men who feel spatially and temporally stuck, like dozens of thousands who fled West Africa and reached Europe over the last decade, only to end up in the “waithood” of asylum reception? Is there any meaningful destination ahead of them? On the surface, their social and legal precarity is hard to reconcile with future aspirations, let alone anticipations. However, the alluring vision of a future “good life”, which informed their migration pathways, may survive the clash between the linear, accelerated time of migration and the circular, static time of asylum. As my ethnography in a reception centre of Northern Italy shows, meaningful traces of the future emerge across the residents' fragmented narratives, practices, and material cultures. These are multi‐sensory signals about the possibility of a certain future, and assertions about its moral worth. Unveiling them casts light on the subjective understanding of the future on multiple scales, as an aspired gendered transition to autonomy and adulthood. It also shows that anticipation matters, even when it only draws on an implicit register of emotions, atmospheres, and daily routines. It is a way of preparing oneself for an unpredictable future – and of coping and making sense of the suspended temporality of asylum.

Analysing Modelling Errors in Migration Models Using a Simulation Approach

Jianfa Shen

Full text
A major challenge of migration modelling is that the actual migration process is unknown to researchers. Previous migration modelling has been done in a black‐box manner. Only the overall performance of a migration model is evaluated, while the contributions of specific errors remain unclear. There are at least four modelling errors: errors caused by missing any significant factors of migration, incorrect model form, the wrong assumption of a random process and the random process. These four errors are mixed, and it is hard to identify each error clearly in empirical models. They affect accurate analyses of the migration process with biased parameter estimates and large modelling errors, reducing the applicability of migration models. This research uses a known migration model to generate simulated migration data to assess the first, third and fourth modelling errors. Hundred sets of simulations are conducted. If a migration model misses an explanatory variable (model LMb) or a model with a wrong random process (model PM) is used, it is found that the model performance is significantly different from that of the correct model. The impact on estimated parameters is more complex. The results confirm concerns about modelling errors in previous studies, such as missing an important variable and using the wrong random process. But the findings indicate that the impacts of modelling errors are not the same for all origin and destination variables and random processes. Migration researchers need to pay attention to such differential impacts of modelling errors.

Residential Mobility in Mid‐ to Late‐Life: A Longitudinal Analysis From the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging

Somayeh Ghazalbash, K. Bruce Newbold, Manaf Zargoush

Full text
Residential mobility, the local relocation of individuals and households, plays an important role in shaping population dynamics, housing markets, service demand, and individual well‐being. This study aimed to investigate the factors associated with residential mobility in mid‐ to later life among Canadians, examining how needs, capabilities/resources, and opportunity structures together influence the decision to relocate. Using longitudinal data from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging, our findings showed the multidimensional nature of residential mobility in mid‐ to later life. Needs‐related factors, including poorer oral health, nutritional vulnerability, indicators of psychological and cognitive strain, greater reliance on non‐professional support, and dissatisfaction with home, were associated with higher odds of moving. By contrast, capabilities and social resources, such as higher income, social engagement, having a partner, and strong ethnocultural ties, were associated with greater residential stability. Opportunity structures and contextual constraints, including dwelling type, non‐urban residence, and province of residence, also played an important role, highlighting the relevance of broader place‐based factors. In addition, prior relocation and becoming unpartnered were associated with greater odds of subsequent mobility. To support residential stability where desired and ensure that relocations are driven by choice rather than necessity, policy efforts should prioritize early preventive supports, community‐based home adaptations, strengthened home and community care, and improved housing affordability and access to services.

Hubs, Flows and Hierarchies: Mapping European Student Mobility

Vladimír Baláž, Ivan Lichner, Tomáš Jeck

Full text
This study examines the structural evolution of intra‐European student degree mobility networks for non‐PhD and PhD students across two periods (2013–2019 and 2020–2023) marked by Brexit and the COVID‐19 pandemic. Employing network analysis and Poisson Pseudo Maximum Likelihood (PPML) gravity models, we identify persistent core‐periphery hierarchies, with Western and Northern European nations acting as dominant hubs. The analysis identifies key mobility hubs, discerns the primary corridors of movement and quantifies the economic, institutional, cultural and geographical factors associated with these patterns. Four theoretical lenses—world‐systems, human capital, acculturation and aspirational—frame the interpretation. The results indicate that economic disparities, educational quality and trade and knowledge cooperation are associated with stronger student flows. Cultural‐linguistic proximity and existing diasporas are also linked to mobility patterns, while symbolic prestige and institutional reputation accompany the attractiveness of core destinations. The overall network topology remains broadly stable across both periods, with geopolitical shocks absorbed through a reweighting of bilateral flows rather than structural change. A UK‐specific event‐study nevertheless identifies substantial inbound declines aligned with the 2016 referendum for PhD flows and with the 2021 EU/EEA fee‐status change for non‐PhD flows, indicating that aggregate stability coexists with significant country‐level adjustment.

Geographic Immobility in the United States: Assessing the Prevalence and Characteristics of Those Who Never Migrate Across State Lines Using Linked Federal Tax Microdata

Mark Ellis, Lee Fiorio, Thomas B. Foster

Full text
This paper explores the prevalence and characteristics of those who never migrate at the state scale in the United States. Studying people who never migrate requires regular and frequent observation of their residential location for a lifetime, or at least for many years. A novel US population‐sized longitudinal dataset that links individual‐level Internal Revenue Service records across time supplies this information annually. Additional linkages to the Social Security Administration and other data sources provide information on sociodemographic characteristics. We use these administrative microdata to follow a cohort aged between 15 and 50 in 2001 from 2001 to 2016, differentiating those who lived in the same state every year during this period (i.e., never made an interstate move) from those who lived in more than one state (i.e., made at least one interstate move). We find that those who never made an interstate move comprised 75% of the total population of this age cohort. This percentage varies by year of age but never falls below 62%, even for those who were teenagers or young adults in 2001. There are also variations in these percentages by gender, race, nativity, and income. Our findings suggest a need for more research on geographically immobile populations in the United States.

Eastward Bound: The Rise of Singapore as a Preferred Study Destination for Chinese College Graduates Amid Geopolitical Shifts

Qian Huang, Wenqin Shen

Full text
Amid pandemic disruption and intensifying geopolitical tensions, Chinese college graduates are increasingly selecting Singapore as a study destination. This paper examines how and why this shift occurs by conceptualising place not as a container of destination attributes, but as an evaluative and relational construct assembled under conditions of uncertainty. Drawing on qualitative interviews with Chinese postgraduate students and faculty at Singaporean public universities, we develop a five‐dimensional geographical framework comprising physical, cultural, economic, linguistic, and symbolic dimensions to analyse how students bundle, rank, and recalibrate criteria such as safety, proximity, prestige, and future mobility. The findings show that certain dimensions, particularly geopolitical predictability and visa feasibility, function as hard constraints that delimit viable destinations. Within this narrowed field, Singapore is interpreted as simultaneously proximate and globally connected, culturally resonant yet English‐medium, and politically stable without demanding alignment. Its structured neutrality and governance predictability emerge as key mobility resources in students’ risk calculations. By foregrounding students as active geographical interpreters, the study extends place‐based approaches to international student mobility beyond descriptive push–pull models. It demonstrates how ‘good place’ is relationally produced and recalibrated across life trajectories and shifting geopolitical landscapes.

‘Age Brings Experience’: Challenging Ageism in an Online Community

Hope Mimbs, Anne E. Barrett

Full text
Digital spaces, including online communities, are increasingly vital components of the social networks in which individuals age. These communities can prevent isolation and strengthen social connections during a life stage when networks often shrink. Studies, however, have given limited attention to examining how these communities challenge ageism—a pervasive, but often overlooked, form of inequality. To address this issue, we use data from Gransnet, a popular social networking site for older adults that was created in the United Kingdom in 2011. We conducted thematic and discourse analyses of 110 thread posts and more than 5000 comments made in the site's ‘Everyday Ageism’ discussion forum. Analyses revealed that participants challenged ageism in three ways. First, they legitimized older adults' knowledge through the exchange of support among age peers. Second, they asserted older adults' value by identifying ways they contribute to broader society. Third, they promoted a critique of ageism and shared strategies for navigating it. Our study underscores the importance of considering the broader system of age inequality in which online communities for older adults are located. It shapes the social relationships that emerge in these communities and the content of the interactions that sustain them—which, in turn, can challenge ageism both in and outside digital spaces.

Green Spaces and Migrants' Social Integration in Guangzhou's Urban Villages

Yutian Zhuang, Felicia F. Tian, Jiang Chang, Tingting Lu, Yang Xiao, Dixiang Xie

Full text
As China's urbanization enters a quality‐oriented phase, urban villages serve as critical sites for examining how neighborhood environmental resources intersect with everyday social life under institutional inequality. This study investigates how no‐cost green spaces in Guangzhou's urban villages are associated with residents' perceived neighborhood‐level social integration. Using survey and geospatial data, we conceptualize green space through physical characteristics, use patterns, and subjective perceptions, and investigate whether these associations differ by hukou status. Results show that migrants report lower perceived social integration than locals. Vegetation coverage, green space exposure duration, social activities, perceived importance, and perceived safety are positively associated with perceived social integration, whereas green vision rate, physical activities, and perceived proximity are not. The association between social activities in green spaces and perceived social integration is stronger among migrants than among locals. These findings suggest that, in resource‐constrained urban villages, perceived neighborhood‐level integration is associated not simply with the presence or physical quality of green space, but with socially meaningful forms of green space use. No‐cost green spaces may therefore serve as low‐threshold everyday settings where neighborhood sociability is practiced under hukou ‐based inequality.

Beyond Divided Loyalties: How Transnational Family Ties Enhance Political Trust in High‐Emigration Countries

Jing Zhang, Ruiyuan Ding, Nan Zhang

Full text
Existing literature on candidates' transnational background generally associates cross‐border ties with weakened political trustworthiness, emphasizing concerns over divided loyalty and reduced commitment to domestic development. This perspective, however, overlooks contexts in which mass emigration has become increasingly common. We argue that in societies characterized by widespread emigration, transnational family ties can instead function as political assets by fostering in‐group identification and signalling shared interests with voters who have similar experiences. To test these hypotheses, we conduct a survey experiment among 1782 Venezuelan adults in July 2024. Results confirm that candidates with transnational family ties receive significantly higher trust ratings than those without such connections, and this effect is particularly salient for female candidates. These findings challenge the prevailing ‘political curse’ narrative and lead to a more comprehensive understanding of political trust in societies with high emigration rates.

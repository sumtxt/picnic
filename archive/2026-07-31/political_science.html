Contested Governance and Civilian Prosocial Behavior: Evidence from Lab-in-the-Field Experiments in India

BRANDON BOLTE, VINEETA YADAV, BUMBA MUKHERJEE

Full text
How does contested governance by states and competing armed actors affect the behavior of the governed? During civil conflicts, rebel organizations frequently provide public goods, judicial arbitration, and security, but these forms of governance often operate alongside local state institutions. Beyond avoiding violence, therefore, noncombatants must navigate the daily uncertainty of local governance by states and armed groups vying for territorial control. This, we argue, shapes their quotidian interactions with one another. Using a series of lab-in-the-field experiments and surveys from Naxal-affected Bihar, India, we find that individuals living under such contested governance exhibit greater interpersonal trust and trustworthiness and less opportunistic behavior. We propose two mechanisms to explain these patterns—fearing intra-community retaliation or substituting for the state with interpersonal reliance. Most evidence is only consistent with the latter. By connecting governance, rebellion, and social capital, this work makes important contributions toward understanding the micro-politics of local order during longstanding armed conflicts.

Still Instrumentally Inclusive

STUART J. TURNBULL-DUGARTE, ALBERTO LÓPEZ ORTEGA

Full text
Do individuals in Western democracies shift their views on LGBT+ inclusion when exposed to opposition from Muslim out-groups? And is this “homonationalist” responsiveness stronger among anti-immigration individuals? Our article “Instrumentally inclusive […]” addressed these questions using representative data from Britain (Study 1) and crowd-sourced data from Spain (Study 2). We revisit Study 2 in response to concerns about post-stratification weighting a convenience sample and erroneously inconsistent use of robust standard errors. Varying population reference parameters, weight caps, variance estimators, subgroup definitions, and covariate adjustments, we find strong support for the original findings. Neither reasonable alternative weighting schemes nor corrected variance estimators substantively change the results. In fact, the originally published point estimates are equal to or smaller than the median across alternative specifications. Consistent with the Article, we find that treatment increases support for LGBT+ inclusion in Spain, without consistent identifiable variation by immigration attitudes.

A Democratic Theory of Labor Unions

STEVEN KLEIN

Full text
Recently, political theorists have been increasingly concerned with the implications of democratic values for work. This article contributes to these debates by examining the main vehicle through which workers, historically, have sought democracy at work: the labor union. The article defends a vision of economic democracy that arises from the practice of labor unions. It then emphasizes the distinctive features of labor unions that make them potential vehicles for democracy in other domains. Labor unions are particularly important means of advancing democratic values for three reasons: (1) their ability to appeal to the short- and mid-term self-interest of their members; (2) their anchor in the material reproduction of society; and (3) that they rely on attitudes of cooperation and solidarity among their members. Yet these three features also point to potential limitations of labor unions as laboratories of democracy.

Redistricting Reforms Reduce Gerrymandering by Constraining Partisan Actors

CORY McCARTAN, CHRISTOPHER T. KENNY, TYLER SIMKO, EMMA EBOWE, MICHAEL Y. ZHAO, KOSUKE IMAI

Full text
Political actors often manipulate redistricting plans to gain electoral advantages, a process known as gerrymandering. Several states have implemented institutional reforms to address this problem, such as establishing map-drawing commissions. Estimating the impact of these reforms is challenging because each state structures its processes and rules differently. We model redistricting as a sequential game whose equilibrium solution summarizes multistep institutional interactions as a univariate score. We argue that this score measures the leeway political actors have over the partisan lean of the final plan. Our differences-in-differences analysis demonstrates that reforms reduce partisan bias and increase competitiveness by constraining partisan actors. We perform a counterfactual policy analysis to estimate the effects of enacting recent reforms nationwide. Though commissions are likely to reduce bias, reforms that restrict partisan actors in multiple ways, such as removing veto points (Michigan), are more effective than commissions where parties retain some control (Ohio).

Is Blood Thicker Than Water(-and-Earth)? Partisanship Geography in Imperial China and the Limits of Quantitative History

YAO LIN

Full text
Using Wang Anshi’s Reform as a case study, Yuhua Wang’s “Blood is Thicker Than Water” ( American Political Science Review , 2022) argues that geographically dispersed kinship networks incentivize political elites to support the building of a strong state, whereas geographically concentrated kinship networks undermine state building. In this article, I improve the sample with the help of additional historical records and show that the reported correlation fails to replicate when either the control issue or the outlier issue is considered. I then propose two alternative hypotheses on the role of kinship network dispersal in Wang Anshi’s Reform, based on two common explanations of its partisanship geography in Chinese historical scholarship, that is, status competition and policy adaptiveness. After examining those hypotheses respectively, I reflect on the limits of quantitative history that this case illustrates.

British Journal of Political Science

Democratic Satisfaction and the Public Sphere

Agustín Goenaga

Full text
Public debate is central to most theories of democracy, yet few cross-national studies have examined its association with democratic satisfaction. This article identifies three features of national public spheres – pluralism, information, and respect – and presents annual measures of these features for 175 countries from 2000 to 2020. Among electoral democracies, pluralism remained stable, but the quality of information and the respectfulness of debate tended to decline during that period. Using data from the European Social Survey and the Comparative Study of Electoral Systems, we find that higher levels of pluralism, information, and respect correlate with greater democratic satisfaction, even after controlling for other democratic institutions, various socio-economic factors, and past levels of satisfaction with democracy. Notably, respect remains associated with higher democratic satisfaction when controlling for the other features of the public sphere. These findings call for further research into the relationship between non-electoral components of democracy and political attitudes.

The Equality Positions and Policies of Governments, 1970–2020: How Egalitarian Parties Evoke Equality While Evading Real Redistribution

Alexander Horn, Leo Ahrens

Full text
Despite egalitarian rhetoric, governments often fail to implement redistributive policies. We argue that selective egalitarianism helps to understand this puzzle. Analysing a new dataset of 850,000 manifesto statements and equality policies in 12 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries (1970–2020), we show that even parties committed to equality avoid taxing the rich. Instead of engaging in complex top-end taxation, which is perceived as electorally risky, they pursue pre-distribution and welfare policies with less concentrated costs and complexity. Even these policies are primarily adopted if the party’s constituency is not too educated or rich to benefit from them. Our findings suggest that equality-oriented governments adopt an approach that insures the middle, buffers the poor, but spares the rich. The result is a rhetorical commitment to equality that eschews its most redistributive instrument(s). The patterns we reveal help to explain the consequential disconnect between redistributive demand and actual policy, even under ostensibly economically egalitarian governments.

Disproportionality in Geographic Representation

Orit Kedar, Yair Amitai, Gilad Hurvitz

Full text
We theoretically develop and empirically study the concept of geographic representation. We identify an overlooked aspect of (mis)representation in electoral systems: the degree to which within parties, voters residing in some districts are overrepresented at the expense of their co-partisans residing elsewhere. Drawing on district-level data of 113 parties from twelve districted democracies, we find a substantial degree of geographic representational discrepancies within parties. Importantly, we show that the discrepancy is often accompanied by a difference in policy positions between under and overrepresented districts, making it particularly significant politically. We derive a general measure for the discrepancy between the votes a party gains and the seats it holds in different districts, namely, geographic disproportionality (GeoDisp). Unlike malapportionment, GeoDisp is party specific and draws on actual votes. Utilizing geocoded data, we find a substantial degree of geographic disproportionality, which, per our expectations, varies systematically by party.

Repression as Aesthetic Ordering: How UK Universities Make Palestine Solidarity Illegible in a Time of Genocide

Nicola Pratt, Joss Wozencroft

Full text
This paper analyzes the repression of Palestine solidarity activism on UK university campuses following October 7, 2023. To explain why institutions whose mission centers on critical inquiry and open debate have enacted far-reaching restrictions on Palestine solidarity, we compile an original dataset of 114 incidents across 70 universities (October 2023–March 2025). Drawing on Jacques Rancière’s concept of the “distribution of the sensible,” we conceptualize repression not simply as deterrence or punishment but as a mode of aesthetic ordering through which universities govern political visibility and legitimacy. Repression is multilayered, operating through coercive enforcement and legal actions, procedural and bureaucratic controls, punitive disciplinary processes, and the symbolic erasure of visual and affective expressions of dissent. These practices are enabled by—and in turn reinforce—neoliberal managerialism, racialized securitization, and imperial governance logics that produce racialized hierarchies of threat and safety and recode solidarity as risk and disruption. By rendering Palestine solidarity illegible and illegitimate as politics, UK universities contribute to conditions through which genocidal violence becomes tolerable.

European Journal of Political Research

Beyond the ideal type: Investigating ‘mixed preferences’ for who should govern in nine European countries

Sebastien Rojon, Jean-Benoit Pilet, Paulina Pankowska, Davide Vittori, Emilien Paulis

Full text
Noting widespread disenchantment with representative actors and institutions, a growing field of research has investigated public support for decision-making by alternative actors, such as ordinary citizens and independent experts. However, previous research has mainly focused on ideal-type models of government centred on a single actor, rather than considering that citizens may hold ‘mixed preferences’ for multiple actors. Through a latent profile analysis of survey responses to an established battery measuring preferences for who should govern, we identify six preference types that exist across nine European countries, though their prevalence varies from country to country. The results show that only a small minority of respondents support an ideal-type model, i.e., the representative democrats, while most favour a combination of actors. A sizeable share of respondents continues to support politicians but would like to see greater involvement from either citizens or experts. Finally, we identify three profiles that lack strong theoretical or empirical grounding in previous research but emerge across multiple countries and therefore merit further attention: stealth populists, responsible democrats, and moderates.

Threat perception and partisan animosity: Evidence from Argentina and Brazil

Raul Gomez, Jose Javier Olivas Osuna, Luis Ramiro

Full text
This study examines antagonism between opposing political camps in Argentina and Brazil, two Latin American countries that have experienced heightened polarization and growing support for contentious right-wing populist leaders. Using original survey data, we analyze how specific perceived threats relate to social distance toward supporters of rival presidential candidates, which we use as a proxy for outgroup animosity. We examine multiple threat domains, including perceived threats from rival political camps, other social groups (racists and immigrants) and threats to symbolic values (equality and the nation). Our findings show that threat perception is a significant correlate of outgroup distance in both countries, but the forms of perceived threat linked to outgroup distancing differ across political camps. We also find partial support for the hypothesis that threat perception is more strongly associated with outgroup distance among supporters of the populist right, particularly once potential confounders are taken into account. While this pattern is evident in Brazil, in Argentina, the left and right camps do not differ significantly in how strongly threat perception predicts outgroup distance.

Constructing representation: Political actors’ representative style on Facebook during European and national election campaigns

Márton Bene, Gábor Illés, Uta Russmann, Paweł Baranowski, Jörg Haßler, Simon Kruschinski, Linda Bos, Jakob Linaa Jensen, Anders Olof Larsson, Alena Macková, ... (+1 authors)

Full text
This study investigates contemporary forms of political representation by analyzing the Facebook communication of 110 political leaders from eight European countries during both national and 2024 European Parliament (EP) election campaigns. Based on constructivist theories of representation, we introduce the concept of ‘representational style’, defined as the specific combination of six communicative elements: policy focus, personalized communication, direct voter connection, appeals to social groups, references to a homogeneous people, and antagonism toward opponents. Using manual content analysis of 7,854 posts, the study identifies three distinct representational styles through inductive cluster analysis: People’s Policy Warriors, Technocratic-Pluralist Heroes, and (Wo)man of the People. The findings reveal substantial variation across political and national contexts. A clear East–West divide emerges with Western European leaders tending to adopt a Technocratic-Pluralist approach, while Central and Eastern European politicians more often represent themselves as responsive figures closely connected to the people.

Nonpolitical Identity Signals as Information Shortcuts: Evidence from Three Experiments

Levi G Allen, Wayde Z C Marsh

Full text
A rich literature has documented how voters navigate the complex information landscape by using heuristics, or “information shortcuts,” to help them make sense of the political world. Additionally, a budding line of research argues that our current polarization crisis has extended beyond just the political and now even affects the nonpolitical realm. We submit that voters may be using these nonpolitical identity signals (e.g., a candidate driving a Toyota Prius or eating at Chick-fil-A) as heuristics to make sense of the contemporary political era. Using three original survey experiments, we find that these nonpolitical identity cues are indeed used by voters as partisan heuristics, but only in the absence of political (partisan and issue) informational cues. Furthermore, we also suppress the effect of partisan identification when we provide orthogonal nonpolitical information. These findings show that nonpolitical identity items can be used by citizens as information shortcuts to help citizens navigate the complicated political waters that surround them, but that their effect in light of other pieces of information is minimal.

More than a partisan pandemic: Worldview and COVID ‐19 response in the United States

Marc J. Hetherington, Andrew M. Engelhardt, Isaac D. Mehlhaff

Full text
As the COVID‐19 pandemic unfolded, public responses increasingly divided along political lines: Democrats tended to support policies and behaviors recommended by public health experts and Republicans tended to oppose them. We argue that exclusively party‐based explanations for these patterns are incomplete. Instead, we call attention to people's philosophy about life—their “worldview”—as an important force behind their pandemic responses. We posit that citizens use beliefs derived from interactions with their everyday life to inform their affinities and preferences when analogous matters enter the political realm. Using original cross‐sectional and panel survey data gathered over the course of the pandemic, we theorize and empirically validate a multidimensional measure of worldview. We show that worldview is a coherent, belief‐system‐like entity that remained stable during the pandemic, suggesting its centrality in mass responses to uncertain and novel stimuli. We then show that even before strong party cues emerged, worldview structured Americans' reactions to COVID‐19 and constrained the positions parties could take. Worldview's influence was both direct and indirect, channeled through its relationship with partisan preference. While partisanship is a potent force in political belief systems, much of its effect, we suggest, comes from its association with people's general philosophy about life.

Beyond prejudice reduction: Personal and civic effects of intergroup contact among marginalized youth in Northern Ireland

Danielle L. Blaylock, Patrick F. Kotzur, Alexander W. O'Donnell, Rhiannon N. Turner, Stephanie Burns, Laura K. Taylor, Linda R. Tropp

Full text
Intergroup contact reduces prejudice, but its potential to foster broader personal and societal change remains understudied. We address these gaps by examining a large‐scale peacebuilding intervention (PEACE4Youth) among marginalized youth in post‐conflict Northern Ireland. Using latent growth curve modeling across three waves, we demonstrate that increased intergroup contact is associated with improvements not only in youth's outgroup attitudes but also in their self‐esteem, leadership skills, and civic engagement. Youth with steeper contact gains showed the strongest growth across all domains, supporting intergroup contact as a mechanism for multidimensional change. While effects emerged across Catholic and Protestant youth, baseline differences underscore the role of pre‐existing disparities. These findings extend contact theory by empirically linking contact to personal and societal development, redefining it as a tool not only for harmony but also for empowering individuals to transform divided societies.

Measuring affective polarization: Matching concepts to measures

James N. Druckman, Samara Klar, Yanna Krupnikov, Matthew Levendusky, John Barry Ryan

Full text
Affective polarization is the extent to which partisans like their own party relative to their dislike of opposing parties. High levels of affective polarization in the United States and many other countries have prompted widespread scholarly interest and practical concern. Yet, despite nearly 15 years of scholarship, significant uncertainty remains about how best to measure affective polarization. We offer a comprehensive review focused on the measurement of affective polarization, underscoring how seemingly innocuous choices can yield substantially different results. These include the target of evaluation (e.g., engaged ideologues, ordinary voters), the dimensions of evaluation (e.g., liking/disliking, moralization), the measurement context (e.g., whether respondents are interviewed by an interviewer or complete surveys independently) and the political context (e.g., the power relationship between parties). We conclude that the next generation of inquiry will need to more carefully align measures of affective polarization with specific research questions.

Research & Politics

War propaganda without misinformation? Normative framing and side-taking in war

Kolby Hanson, Angelina Kunitskaia

Full text
How do citizens of third-party states form sympathies in foreign wars? Most prior work on wartime public opinion focuses on the effects of information: material costs, elite assessments, and characteristics of combatants. Yet wartime coverage must also make choices about how to frame a conflict, separate from endorsements or facts. For example, a military intervention might be introduced in the context of one state’s sovereignty or the other state’s quest to restore democracy, even holding the facts constant. We argue that these normative frames have a large impact on public sympathy and support, especially in third-party publics, for whom material costs may be secondary considerations and loyalties may be open to debate. We demonstrate these effects with two vignette experiments among a representative sample of 1200 American adults. Framing decisions swayed respondents’ moral judgments and sympathy by 40–65 percentage points, and support for military aid by 20–30 percentage points. These effects were large and consistent regardless of the characteristics of warring parties, respondents’ foreign policy views, or their attitudes on current events such as the wars in Gaza or Ukraine.

Journal of Experimental Political Science

Beyond Single-Shot Designs: Repeated Treatments, Affective Polarization, and Democratic Norms

Libby Jenke

Full text
Although theory and observational work suggest that affective polarization should shape citizens’ support for democratic norms, experimental tests of this causal claim largely find null effects. Most studies assume any impact would be immediate and rely on one-shot manipulations. Yet democratic norms are sticky; brief shifts in partisan feelings may be too weak or fleeting to move them. I therefore test whether repeated treatments over several waves of a survey shift affective polarization and, in turn, support for democratic norms. I find no significant first-stage effects: the treatments do not change respondents’ affective polarization, so repeated exposures do not cumulatively move it and downstream effects on norms cannot be identified. I conclude by considering why my findings differ from studies that report significant effects for similar treatments. Many experiments measure affective polarization as hostility toward the outparty, whereas this study and observational work conceptualize it as the gap between inparty and outparty evaluations.

European Political Science Review

“Nothing else matters?” Salience differences between young and old

Reto Mitteregger

Full text
Younger generations’ attitudes and behavior increasingly diverge from those of older individuals. To better understand these political differences, it is relevant to analyze whether younger citizens also disagree about “what matters” in politics. However, this assumption of younger voters’ different salience perceptions has rarely been tested for a large range of issues. Thus, this paper argues that younger and older citizens differ in issue salience and specifically suggests that younger generations perceive cultural issues as more salient. To test this, I use novel survey data collected in Germany ( N = 2,509) and Switzerland ( N = 2,494) and apply rating tasks and a pairwise choice experiment, looking at young and older respondents’ salience regarding six political issue areas and a selection of 42 political issues derived from widely used voting advice applications in the respective countries. The results show that issues related to gender equality and certain age-related socioeconomic policies, such as rent prices, are more salient for younger than older respondents, whereas immigration is more important for older generations. This points to both value change and self-interest that divides salience assessments. Overall, these findings can help understand diverging political perceptions and the resulting electoral differences between younger and older generations.

Clarity of Responsibility and the Performance of Government Parties in European Second-Order Elections

Jakub Wondreys

Full text
European Parliament elections (EPEs) are widely regarded as second-order contests. Their perceived lower importance is expected to produce a distinct set of electoral outcomes, chief among them the proposition that governing parties lose support in EPEs relative to preceding national first-order elections (FOEs). This article examines explanatory mechanisms behind this proposition, focusing on the previously unaddressed effects of clarity of responsibility. Particular emphasis is thus given to whether the expected losses vary with government parties’ size and cabinet role, and with other factors that shape responsibility attribution, including coalition size, ministerial portfolio shares and prior government experience. In addition, I also assess whether parties providing formal external support to governments face electoral penalties in EPEs. Covering all EPEs and corresponding FOEs from 1979 to 2024, the study also examines whether the second-order model and its key mechanisms, such as election timing, have shifted over time in response to broader transformations in European politics.

Rival or Villain? The Evolution of Language Depicting the Opposition under Authoritarianisation

Alper H. Yagci, Cemal Yılmaz, Uzay Çetin

Full text
Discursive attacks towards the opposition are often a harbinger of polarisation, democratic backsliding and authoritarianism. We study the evolution of negative language about the opposition in Turkey under Erdoğan, a pioneering case of democratic backsliding. We distinguish between two types of negative language: a valid competitor – ‘rival’ – versus an enemy that needs to be destroyed – ‘villain’. We leverage machine learning to examine every opposition-related sentence in two major newspapers from 2009 to 2022. Our data allow us to compare the depictions of the main opposition party as a rival or villain between media outlets, before and after media ownership change, during election times and beyond. We also test how these depictions respond to currency shocks and military casualties, as the incumbents could try to contain the fallout from these adverse events by discursively attacking the opposition. We find that vilification of the opposition is driven by security challenges rather than economic troubles, which are associated with increases in rival discourse. Results also suggest that political competition can appear unrepresentatively civil precisely during election periods, as an already polarising incumbent may try to present himself in a more reconciliatory light during the campaign period.

Conceptualizing Democratic Embodiment: Feminist Movements Advancing Democracy Amid Backsliding

Anastasia Barone, Giada Bonu Rosenkranz, Anna Lavizzari

Full text
Feminist movements in times of democratic backsliding are primarily framed as defensive, resilient, or resistant. However, such an approach overlooks the agency underpinning the enactment of democratic practices. In this article, we introduce the concept of democratic embodiment to shift the focus from what democracy achieves to how democratic agency is materially, affectively, and relationally constituted in contexts of vulnerability. Drawing on feminist and democratic theory, we conceptualize democratic embodiment as a constitutive mode of political agency through which feminist actors defend, enact, and expand democracy by centering embodied presence, affective solidarities, and infrastructures of care. Rethinking the relationship between democratic resilience and democratic innovation, we argue, helps better highlight how feminist actors enhance democracy, simultaneously resisting the erosion of gender equality while also embodying alternative visions of democracy.

When the party is over: How parties respond to electoral decline

Felix Lehmann

Full text
Over the past decades, we have witnessed the severe electoral decline of formerly successful parties. Yet, we know surprisingly little about how these parties have responded to their losses. I examine the consequences of severe and persistent electoral losses through the lens of intra-party dynamics. I argue that parties react to severe decline by shifting attention to core issues and adopting more extreme positions. I theorize that this is because office-seekers are the first to abandon the sinking ship, tilting the internal power balance towards policy-motivated actors. Testing the theory with manifesto data from 18 Western European countries between 1970 and 2025, I find evidence that parties devote more attention to their core issues following their electoral decline. Parties do not, however, adopt more extreme positions on these issues. I find limited evidence that shifts in the intra-party balance are behind these changes.

Allocative claims and pork barrel politics

Marius Sältzer, Hauke Peters, Charlotte Keyßler

Full text
Political strategies are shaped by electoral institutions and incentives. There is a large literature on pork barrel politics in single-member districts (SMD), but understanding the institutional effects that shape the incentives for pork requires comparison within the same system. The German Mixed-Member Electoral system offers an ideal institutional setup, but official data on pork is not available. Instead, we focus on the use of pork references as an electoral strategy, adapting credit-claiming theory to textual analysis of candidates’ social media posts. Using supervised machine learning on Facebook posts by candidates in the 2017 and 2021 federal election campaign period, we fine-tune a BERT classifier on 1000 manually coded posts to identify pork references (F1: 0.82). Our results demonstrate that incumbents, particularly district incumbents and those who outperform their national party’s vote share, are significantly more likely to emphasize pork in their communication. Moreover, candidates are more likely to use pork references if their competitors within the district employ similar strategies, and when electoral competition in the district is high. Our findings imply that particularistic logic exists beyond SMDs and can be shaped by the internal specificities of more complex incentive systems.

Party responsiveness to party supporters, the electorate, and interest groups in a multidimensional policy space

Vibeke Wøien Hansen, Mads Thau

Full text
This article sheds new light on party responsiveness by considering the role of interest group pressure in parties’ responsiveness to their supporters and the electorate on multiple policy dimensions in 12 countries. Following recent work, we expect that parties are as responsive to shifts in their supporters’ preferences as to the wider electorate. Yet, parties also attend to interest group pressure. We argue that parties’ responsiveness to voters is enhanced by leadership ties with interest groups but that ties with only sectional groups are associated with more stable party positions. Combining data on voters, interest groups, and parties, our analysis shows that parties are responsive to preference change among their supporters and, further, that their policy positions are more stable when they only have ties with sectional groups. However, parties’ responsiveness on specific dimensions is not necessarily enhanced by leadership ties with the interest groups particularly active on those dimensions.

Technocracy or not, this is the question: can party system predict the number of technocrats in governments?

Alessandro Di Biagio

Full text
In the last three decades, Europe has witnessed an increase in the number of technocrats – non-partisan, non-elected experts appointed on the basis of technical competence – in government. This paper examines whether structural characteristics of party systems predict the share of technocrats in cabinets across 30 European countries between 2000 and 2024. Building on theories of party competition, delegation, and representation, it investigates how systemic features of party democracy shape technocratic appointments. Using fractional regression models, the study tests the relationship between the share of technocrats and three key party-system variables: ideological polarization, party system fragmentation, and party institutionalization. The findings reveal that technocrats are significantly more prevalent where ideological distance between parties complicates bargaining and creates demand for neutral mediators who can bridge incompatible policy positions. Conversely, when more parties compete for portfolios, partisan actors crowd out non-partisan experts rather than delegating to them. Most importantly, in systems where parties possess stable organizations, coherent programmatic profiles, and deep societal roots, technocratic inclusion is significantly lower.

A dynamic seat product model

Abelardo Gómez Díaz

Full text
The Seat Product Model uses just two institutional parameters (average district magnitude and assembly size) to predict legislative fragmentation with remarkable accuracy. However, it is best suited for simple, single-tier systems. Subsequent extensions to complex, two-tier systems rely on an upper-tier (multiplier-style) correction that accounts for cross-tier inflationary effects but estimate it as a single constant from pooled cross-national data. Applied to Mexico’s mixed-member lower house elections (1997–2021), both specifications systematically mispredict legislative fragmentation. This is because cross-tier inflationary effects are not a fixed system property but an evolving one, shaped by institutional features interacting with a changing party system. This study proposes a Dynamic Seat Product Model that retains the original institutional baseline while allowing the correction to update automatically via exponential smoothing. The DSPM tracks Mexico’s long-run rise in legislative fragmentation more closely than fixed alternatives, clarifies the mechanism behind it, and offers a principled path toward making the SPM applicable to virtually any single election.

Full text
In honor of the creation of the Congressional Oversight Research Database (CORD), we explore some of the selection effects that observable measures of oversight may be subject to.

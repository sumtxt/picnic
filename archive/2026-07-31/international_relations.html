Executive constraints and incumbent takeovers

Vilde Lunnan Djuve

Full text
This paper provides a systematic study of how judicial and legislative constraints on the executive safeguard against incumbent-driven regime transitions and argues that the relationship, across the regime-type spectrum, should take the shape of an inverted U. The basic argument is based on opportunity and incentive. In regimes with high levels of constraints, incumbents are inhibited by their institutions from taking over, whereas incumbents in regimes with very low levels of constraints provide no incentive for such moves. However, in regimes with medium levels of constraints, the constraining institutions restrict the incumbent enough to incentivize takeover whilst remaining insufficient to curtail them. This relationship is examined using newly available data on incumbent-induced breakdown, spanning over 2,000 regimes. Different operationalizations of the dependent variable are employed to sufficiently account for the incremental nature of incumbent takeovers. A series of TSCS Logit and LPM models find consistent and largely robust support for the hypothesis.

The effects of population settlements and territorial labeling on domestic audience: Evidence from Israeli settlements in the West Bank

Oded Haklai, Anna Zhang

Full text
States trying to absorb previously external territories under dispute need to convince their national community that the newly acquired territory is an indivisible part of the homeland, important for national security, identity, and prosperity, and is generally worthy of incorporation even at a cost of a protracted conflict. Two strategies that states use to this end are territorial labeling, which means assigning a name to the territory that evokes intrinsic ties to the nation's history and populating the acquired territory with members of the national community (population settlements). To date, no empirical study has evaluated the impact of these two strategies. Using an original survey experiment in the case of Israel, we test the impact of territorial labeling and population settlements on the propensity of Israeli Jews to view the territories Israel conquered from Jordan in the 1967 war (West Bank/Judea and Samaria) as tied to their homeland, important for economic prosperity and national security, and on support for annexation and continued population settlements. We find that territorial labeling is consequential for all our outcomes of interest, whereas the impact of population settlements, specifically the demographic concentration of conationals, is on perceptions of connection to historic homeland and security importance, with no observed effect on policy preferences. These findings have theoretical implications for our understanding of how national territories are socially constructed and made seem more indivisible, demonstrating specifically the impact of symbolic-discursive practices and tangible, material changes on territorial attachments and policy preferences in territorial conflicts.

Whose China Shock? Partisanship, import exposure, and trade rhetoric in the US Senate

Weitian Liu

Full text
Trade among major powers is never purely economic; it is also a site of political conflict over blame, threat, and national interest. Why, then, do legislators facing similar trade shocks respond in such different ways? Focusing on US Senate rhetoric on trade with China between 2009 and 2023, this paper examines how partisanship conditions the translation of economic disruption into elite political speech. Drawing on sentiment analysis of Senate floor speeches and state-level measures of Chinese import exposure across two periods, it develops a partisan-conditioning argument: trade disruption acquires political meaning as parties interpret it. Democratic senators grew markedly more critical as local import exposure increased, especially under the earlier China Shock window. Republicans showed a much flatter response to early-period exposure and even a slightly positive association with later-period penetration. The China Shock left a durable mark on Senate discourse, but one structured by party, not geography alone.

The politics of crime prevention: Evidence from Colombia

Juan David Gelvez

Full text
Why do governments prevent crime in some places while neglecting others? Crime prevention policies are often viewed as too slow, diffuse, and politically intangible to generate electoral rewards. When implemented, existing research suggests they primarily serve to reward co-partisans or mobilize ideologically aligned voters. This article challenges that view by arguing that incumbents allocate crime prevention resources to electorally competitive municipalities because these investments yield electoral returns. Using detailed project-level data from Colombia, I show that national incumbents disproportionately direct crime prevention funds to municipalities where recent presidential elections were closely contested, rather than to areas with higher crime rates or strong partisan loyalties. I then show that these allocations increase the vote share of the right-wing incumbent coalition in subsequent elections. Drawing on quantitative analyses and interviews with policymakers and bureaucrats, I argue that these effects operate primarily through the visibility and attribution of crime prevention projects, particularly those aligned with right-wing issue ownership of public security.

Evaluations of institutional performance among adolescents: Comparing the socializing roles of schools and families

Giovanni D’Agostino

Full text
Democratic accountability requires citizens’ political trust to reflect institutional performance, making it crucial to understand what fosters such responsiveness. While existing studies suggest that education shapes evaluative attitudes, direct evidence remains limited because many analyses struggle to separate educational effects from those of parental background. This paper offers a comprehensive test of these explanations by examining adolescents within the same target grade and by assessing school-level civic education characteristics often credited with cultivating critical citizenship norms. It then compares school-level patterns with parental background to evaluate the proxy-versus-cause debate. Using data from three waves of the International Civic and Citizenship Education Study, the analysis shows that structurally low and declining institutional performance depresses political trust among adolescents. However, consistent with the education-as-proxy argument, the positive association between performance and trust is significantly stronger among adolescents with higher parental socio-economic status, while school-level civic education shows no meaningful moderating effect.

Who supports martial law in a democracy? Lessons from South Korea

Brandon Beomseob Park, Inkwan Chung

Full text
On 3 December 2024, South Korea experienced an unprecedented political crisis when the president declared martial law. While the declaration was rapidly reversed and condemned by key democratic institutions, public opinion did not converge on rejection. Instead, support for—and tolerance of—the measure persisted among a nontrivial share of citizens. Why, in a democracy with functioning institutions, are some citizens willing to accept extraordinary executive measures? Using original nationwide survey data, this research shows that public support for martial law is strongly associated with affective polarization. Individuals who express strong partisan and leader-based affective alignment with the incumbent are significantly more likely to accept martial law, while those polarized in the opposite direction overwhelmingly reject it. The findings suggest that affective polarization can align evaluations of executive overreach with partisan loyalties, weakening normative constraints even in consolidated democracies where institutional safeguards continue to function effectively.

Fair reciprocity and EU law

Pietro Intropi

Full text
Should all European Union (EU) citizens enjoy the same welfare benefits as nationals when they migrate within Europe? Which categories of EU migrants (workers, job-seekers and non-economically active ones) should be entitled to full equal treatment? I discuss reciprocity as a moral basis of EU law, extending White’s account of fair reciprocity to the supranational context of the EU. I suggest that the concept of workers in EU law should be reformed to account for a plurality of valuable contributions. I also discuss the implications of reciprocity in non-ideal conditions, arguing that EU migrants have a reduced contributive obligation when they face injustice in the host member state. The seriousness of such injustice may even imply lifting the contributive obligation entirely. The contributive obligation of non-economically active individuals is a duty to seek work, and I suggest it entitles them to limited equal treatment.

The trust in government gap: National trust, local trust, and compliance

Susan Banducci, Laszlo Horvath, Daniel Stevens

Full text
According to Tang and Huhe, individuals “are nested in a multiplicity of governments,” including national and local governments, “and . . . constantly find themselves in variant relationships with government at different levels”. Yet, previous research has mostly concentrated on individuals’ assessments of national governments, or supranational organizations such as the European Union, overlooking assessments of local governments and their effects. To address this, we fielded a four-wave survey in England in 2020–2021 capturing local and national government trust, as well as contemporaneous indicators of performance across these levels of government. Disaggregating between- and within-individual explanations for variation, we find distinct influences on trust in national and local government, and that trust in local government exerts an influence on pro-social behaviour whereas national government trust does not. Importantly, for future research on political trust, our findings show that the level of government matters for both the drivers and consequences of political trust.

Constituent power and constituent collectives in a Union context: Deciding the futures of Scotland and the United Kingdom

Anna Meine

Full text
Over the last few decades, interconnected debates about Scottish independence and Brexit have shaped politics in the United Kingdom and raised questions of constituent power that remain controversial both politically and theoretically. Against this backdrop, this article proposes a nuanced democratic conception of constituent power that serves as an alternative to orthodox sovereigntist accounts. This conception is theoretically more compelling and, moreover, enables fruitful engagement with contemporary constitutional controversies. By establishing a dialogue between theoretical discussion and the Scottish and UK case, the article, first, explores constituent power as one dimension of popular sovereignty and its interplay with constituted powers over time. Second, it examines how constituent collectives can and, in recent history, have become subject to contestation and change. Third, it sketches monist and pluralist conceptions of constituent power and peoples that co-exist, yet conflict within the United Kingdom, thereby mapping distinct trajectories for possible constitutional futures in a union setting.

Balancing Act: Coercion, Terrorism, and Regime Stability

Allison Carnegie, Aleksandra Gracheva, Adam Lichtenheld, Dipali Mukhopadhyay

Full text
A substantial literature seeks to understand the drivers and consequences of foreign policy instruments that states employ to change the behavior of other states. We argue that this body of work—and the surrounding policy discourse—often miscategorizes or misunderstands the goals of these instruments in the important set of cases involving the threat of terrorism. We contend that the deployment of these instruments depends on the relationship between the target regime and the coercing state and the degree to which the coercing state perceives a terrorist threat emanating from the target’s territory. We develop a typology that characterizes and predicts foreign policy approaches as a function of these two concerns. We theorize that, in cases where the target regime is adversarial and where a distinct terrorist threat is perceived, the coercer employs a balancing approach. It seeks to apply enough pressure to keep the target regime weak, but not so much that the regime collapses, which could allow extremist non-state actors to assume control. We use in-depth case studies of US foreign policy in Syria and Afghanistan to contest the common narrative that sanctions fail and, instead, contend that maintaining regime limbo often serves US goals and interests as defined in the post-9/11 era.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

Does populism challenge the status quo? Understanding transgression and subversion in contemporary politics

Sebastian Ronderos, Emmy Eklundh

Full text
This article argues that we need to distinguish between transgression and subversion in order to fully understand populism and contemporary politics more generally. We make three key contributions. First, we revisit contemporary scholarship on transgression, arguing that existing distinctions between different forms of norm-breaking do not fully explain their markedly different political effects. Second, drawing on psychoanalysis, particularly the concepts of fantasy and enjoyment, we foreground the role of affective and libidinal investment in shaping political identification. From this perspective, transgression may reaffirm the socio-normative order rather than challenge it. Third, we introduce the concept of subversion to offer greater analytical granularity when studying political movements and parties that challenge the status quo. While transgression may revitalise the norm through the enjoyment generated by its violation, subversion loosens the affective attachments through which socio-normative arrangements acquire their grip. By disentangling transgression from subversion, we call for a more fine-grained understanding of populist politics and advocate a rethinking of what subversive politics means today.

Cyclicality in reactionary internationalism: A genealogy of Realist, Spenglerian, and New Right theories of historical change

Nicholas Michelsen, Pablo De Orellana

Full text
Reactionary actors from Lavrov to Trump often appeal to ‘realism’ in denouncing the liberal rights-based international order. What makes Realism and contemporary New Right ethno-nationalist politics conceptually compatible? Examining the intertwined intellectual histories of their conceptualisations of time and historical change, we find that early Realism adopted from ethno-geopolitics a theory of historical change as perpetually recurring cycles of survivalist struggle. We first retrieve the intellectual history of cyclical theories in international thought and Realist traditions, showing how they were drawn from 19th-century German ethno-geopolitics. Second, we map their influence on contemporary New Right nationalism. The history of cyclical temporal theory reveals that early Realism and ethno-nationalism emerged as related and compatible strategies to manage Western decline. While most Realist International Relations isn’t necessarily ethno-nationalist, its theorisation of historical change as cyclical rise and decline through survivalist struggle has nonetheless come to support the global resurgence of New Right ethno-nationalism.

Denomination, Religiosity and Anti-Immigrant Attitudes in Europe: Comparative Evidence from the European Social Survey

Ilia Dorkhanov, Boris Sokolov

Full text
This study investigates the relationship between individual religiosity and attitudes towards immigrants of different religious backgrounds in Europe. Using data from the 7th wave of the European Social Survey (2014-2015), we examine the influence of individual denomination and subjective religiosity level on hostility towards Muslim immigrants and the importance of immigrants’ Christian background. Our analysis, guided by social identity theory and religious compassion theory, reveals mixed support for these theoretical frameworks. While Christians and individuals with higher levels of subjective religiosity value a Christian immigrant background more than their non-religious counterparts, neither denomination nor subjective religiosity level significantly influence attitudes towards Muslim immigrants. We also conduct an exploratory analysis which shows that country-level average religiosity and prevalent denomination do not directly affect the dependent variables but leverage the effect of subjective religiosity on both. These findings suggest that, in the European context, religious social identity and religious compassion may operate selectively, influencing attitudes based on perceived religious closeness and potentially being shaped by broader societal factors.

Human Hypersubstitution Theory: Critical Rehumanization in Algorithmic Ecosystems

Marcelo Salvador Celestino, José Willegaignon de Amorim de Carvalho, Vânia Cristina Pires Nogueira Valente

Full text
he rise of autonomous algorithmic systems has been reconfiguring the ontological, epistemological, and relational foundations of the human condition. Confronted with the social and ethical gap of the “erosion of human agency” and the urgency of a perspective of “society shaping the algorithms,” actively rather than passively, we still lack integrated frameworks that articulate trust, empathy, ethics, biases, and autonomy in diagnosis, in sociotechnical structure, and in institutional implications. This study develops such a model, presenting Human Hypersubstitution Theory as a novel conceptual proposal, systematically named, structured, and here formalized for the first time. It analyzes the effects of algorithmic automation on subjectivity, language, and social practices, offering directions for confronting the algorithmic situation. The theory is anchored in a genealogy that comprises Anders’s philosophy of technology (erosion of agency and responsibility), Baudrillard’s critique of simulation (implosion of the real and dissolution of the Other), and McLuhan’s media theory (sensory environments, hyperextension, and the global village). It dialogues with contemporary critical perspectives and is structured around three axes of response: planetary ethical governance, critical and algorithmic literacy, and institutional practices of rehumanization. Empirical evidence in fields such as education, health, and culture supports its propositions, revealing unprecedented forms of alienation—simulated bonds, parameterized subjectivities, and automated decisions as the norm. The notion of an Algorithmic Nebula, designed on fuzzy logic, models the transition from personalization to personification. The study concludes that Human Hypersubstitution Theory provides foundations for ethical, political, and critical-literacy interventions, calling for sociotechnical governance capable of reclaiming autonomy, empathy, and responsibility in an AI-mediated future.

Locke on Money and Trust: Between Fides, Trusteeship, and Confidence

JHET Assistant, Tomas Lima Pimenta

Full text
This paper reinterprets the money–trust nexus in John Locke by distinguishing three rationalities of trust at work across his moral, political, and monetary writings: fides, trusteeship (fiduciary power), and confidence. Against England’s monetary turmoil of the 1690s, it reads Locke’s monetary interventions as attempts to secure public faith and contractual stability amid monetary deterioration and contested proposals to ““raise” money’s value. Fides underwrites promising and the moral intelligibility of conventional signs yet remains too fragile to sustain monetary order without reliable sanctions. Trusteeship explains why fiduciary power can secure coining, standard maintenance, and the punishment of monetary crime, but cannot create value by decree. Finally, it tackles the puzzle of “intrinsick value” by reading Locke’s universal “consent of mankind” as confidence: an impersonal trust in the persistence of a social convention, neither natural essence nor sovereign fiat.

Diagnosing social norms and their influence on adolescent girls’ experience of menstruation in Khulna, Bangladesh: A theory-led qualitative exploration

Heather Suttor, Tasfiyah Jalil, Arifa Bente Mohosin, Erin C Hunter, Abdul Jabbar, Shampa Guha, Bithy Podder, Giorgia Gon, Sabina F. Rashid, Elissa Kennedy

Full text
Menstrual health advocacy often states that harmful social norms influence adolescents' menstrual health. However, there is a lack of specificity in identifying which social norms drive menstrual behaviors and how these norms operate and are sustained. We carried out a phased qualitative study with adolescent girls in Khulna, Bangladesh, guided by Bicchieri’s (2017) social norms theory. The research team identified initial candidate behaviors, which were refined through three FGDs with mothers and five FGDs with girls. Subsequent data collection involved five FGDs with girls and three with boys, along with in-depth individual interviews (IDIs) with girls (n=15), mothers (n=7), fathers (n=3), and male (n=2) and female (n=3) teachers. Our framework analysis blended deductive coding against social norms constructs, including empirical and normative expectations, reference groups and sanctions, with inductive analysis to understand experiences. We found that a privacy norm influenced the concealment of menstruation and restricted discussions, limiting learning and support. A second influential factor was the expectation that a “good mother” teaches her daughter how to care for herself at menarche. Other behaviors, such as school attendance or care-seeking, were primarily influenced by non-normative drivers, including beliefs. Our results provide evidence and an applied theoretical framework to guide interventions. We emphasize that both empirical and normative expectations and sanctions must be identified and addressed in interventions aimed at shifting social norms. Our findings emphasize mothers and families as key reference groups and suggest interventions targeting girls alone may be unable to shift norms.

The Social Life of R₀: Why the reproduction number is less a fact about a virus than a rumor about the society it lands in

Fabio Sanchez

Full text
The basic reproduction number, R₀, is routinely reported as though it were an intrinsic property of a pathogen, a biological constant to be looked up and endured. It is not. R₀ is an average over a population, and its value is set largely by how that population lives: household size and age structure, density, housing and sanitation, work and mobility, and access to care. The same pathogen therefore carries different reproduction numbers across social settings, and the scalar R₀ obscures the contact structure that determines who is infected and where transmission will concentrate. None of this is news to epidemiologists; the confusion lives downstream, where a working average is repeated as though it were a fixed trait of the microbe. This essay argues that R₀ is best read not as a verdict a virus hands down but as a summary of the conditions a society arranges for it: in an outbreak, a map of where transmission will run hot; over the longer run, a lever held largely by sectors well beyond emergency response.

Relying on the Mainstream? Entity Networks in Alternative Media

Paul Balluff, Hajo G. Boomgaarden, Annie Waldherr

Full text
Alternative media, characterized by their heterogeneous nature and self-perceived corrective role to legacy media, have gained increasing relevance in shaping public opinion. Despite their influence, large-scale analyses of their referential and actor-related reporting characteristics remain scarce. This exploratory study investigates named entity networks in German alternative and legacy media, focusing on their reporting on the Nord Stream 2 gas pipeline from 2011 to 2022. Using computational methods, we analyze news articles from 37 alternative and 98 legacy outlets, extracting named entities and constructing co-occurrence networks to identify distinct reporting patterns. Our findings challenge assumptions that alternative media inherently diverge from legacy media in their referenced entities. While alternative media exhibit distinctive network structures—such as lower modularity and higher transitivity—they also demonstrate substantial overlap in their choice of entities and patterns of media mentions. These findings suggest that alternative media frequently mention other media, which may reflect resource limitations and reactive reporting tendencies. This study contributes to a deeper understanding of alternative media by highlighting both their unique characteristics and shared practices with legacy media.

Full text
In February 2019, Network Rail sold 5,261 railway arches to a Blackstone-led consortium for £1.46 billion, terminating the security of several thousand micro-enterprises, migrant traders, and arts producers. The Social Stratification in Generative Artificial Intelligence Literacy, Use Frequency, and Workplace Acceptance in 11 European countries

zhuofei lu, Yao Ma

Full text
Generative AI (GenAI) has diffused rapidly across Europe, yet who feels able to use it—and who is willing to work with it—remains poorly understood. Using the latest CRONOS-3 panel of the probability-based European Social Survey (N = 7,556; 11 European countries, fielded in 2025), we model six separate measures of GenAI literacy and use frequency, together with acceptance of GenAI as an assistant and as a peer in the workplace, using country-clustered estimators with wild-bootstrap inference. Acceptance is strongly role-differentiated: a majority accept GenAI as an assistant, but only one in ten as a peer. Both competence and acceptance are sharply stratified by education, age, and gender—and the gender divide is the most consistent pattern of all: women trail men on every one of the six literacy and use measures and on both acceptance outcomes, and the deficit appears in behavioural use as well as in self-assessed skill, so it cannot be reduced to gendered self-assessment alone. GenAI literacy and use frequency in turn strongly predict acceptance in both roles. Cross-national variation is substantial, but with only 11 countries no association between national media, scholarly, or AI-talent environments and individual outcomes survives strict small-cluster and multiple-testing corrections, so national context is treated descriptively. GenAI competence and workplace acceptance thus emerge as socially stratified from the outset—with women consistently furthest behind—an early-forming divide that risks hardening as GenAI becomes workplace infrastructure.

Full text
Environmental justice for China’s rural populations is especially important in the context of China’s multifaceted environmental crises as existing disparities have exacerbated the unequal distribution of economic wealth and environmental harm between rural and urban communities. Households’ regional housing affordability strain and mental health: insights from the Netherlands

Agata A. Troost, Mark van Duijn

Full text
Housing affordability pressures are increasingly recognised as a key determinant of mental well-being, yet existing research largely focuses on individual or household-level cost burdens, omitting the spatial context. This paper argues that housing market pressures are subject to regional market dynamics, shaping mental health in addition to individual housing affordability stress. We examine how regional housing affordability strain is associated with mental health in the Netherlands by combining LISS Panel survey data with geo-coded administrative data from Statistics Netherlands. We use a novel rank-based measure of regional price-income mismatch to capture the relative position of households within regional housing markets. Regression results show that regional housing market pressure is negatively associated with mental health in 2023, although no comparable association is found in 2018. The 2023 relationship persists after controlling for individual characteristics and household-level affordability stress. These findings suggest that the mental health consequences of the housing crisis extend beyond individual cost burdens, highlighting the importance of regional housing market dynamics as an independent source of stress.

Beyond Interrogation: A Dialogic Autoethnography of Freedom, Security, and Competing Worldviews in Iran

Ali benabbas

Full text
Abstract Social conflicts are often interpreted as disagreements over political or ideological positions. This article argues that many such conflicts emerge from deeper differences in the systems of meaning through which individuals interpret shared concepts. Using dialogic autoethnography, the study presents a composite analytical dialogue, developed from the author's lived experiences, long-term social observations, and multiple conversations within the sociopolitical context of contemporary Iran, to examine how concepts including freedom, security, justice, religion, and legitimacy acquire competing meanings within contrasting worldviews. Rather than seeking to reconstruct a single historical conversation or privilege one political position over another, the dialogue serves as a qualitative interpretive device through which competing systems of meaning are explored. Dialogic autoethnography is therefore presented as a qualitative methodological approach for exploring competing systems of meaning and facilitating reflective engagement across deeply divided perspectives.

Full text
We examined the male-female collaboration practices of all internationally visible Polish university professors (N = 25,463) based on their Scopus-indexed publications from 2009–2018 (158,743 journal articles). We merged a national registry of 99,935 scientists (with full administrative and biographical data) with the Scopus publication database, using probabilistic and deterministic record linkage. The young and the old, the fast and the slow: a large-scale study of productivity classes and rank advancement

Marek Kwiek, Wojciech Roszka

Full text
We examined a large sample of Polish science, technology, engineering, mathematics, and medicine (STEMM) scientists (N = 16,083) to study rank advancement and productivity in the past 40 years. We used two previously neglected time dimensions – promotion age and promotion speed – to construct individual lifetime biographical and publication profiles. We followed a classificatory approach and the new methodological approach of journal prestige–normalized productivity. All scientists were allocated to different productivity, promotion age, and promotion speed classes (top 20%, middle 60%, and bottom 20%). Beyond Gender Headcounts in Higher Education: A Multidimensional Justice Framework for Equality, Equity and Parity

Sixbert Sangwa

Full text
Gender equality in higher education is commonly monitored through sex-disaggregated ratios, but numerical parity is neither a complete definition of equality nor a self-interpreting verdict on institutional justice. This critical integrative review examines how equality, equity, parity, formal opportunity, substantive opportunity, descriptive representation and substantive influence are conceptualised across higher education scholarship. A transparently bounded, PRISMA-adapted search completed on 23 July 2026 identified 58 records; after duplicate removal, screening and eligibility assessment, 26 studies and reviews were included. The synthesis reveals two recurrent analytical errors: headcount reductionism, which treats a 50:50 distribution as sufficient evidence of justice, and procedural formalism, which treats identical rules as sufficient evidence of fairness. To address both errors, the article develops a Multidimensional Gender Justice Assessment Framework integrating normative equality, access, resources, procedures, participation, outcomes, influence, intersectionality, temporal persistence and remedial justification. Application to a four-member committee demonstrates that the same numerical composition may be fair, unfair or indeterminate depending on how candidates entered the pipeline, how criteria were constructed and applied, and how authority is distributed after selection. Parity is therefore best treated as a sentinel indicator that prompts investigation rather than as a substitute for causal and normative inquiry. The framework offers higher education leaders a practical basis for auditing admissions, appointments, governance, academic careers and student participation while preserving both substantive equality and procedural fairness. It also specifies when targets, thresholds or temporary quotas are defensible: persistent disparity, an evidenced mechanism, proportionality, transparency, safeguards against tokenism and scheduled review are required.

Intelligence as a Partial Order: Why g Compresses a Structure It Cannot Rank

Aaron Hoegn

Full text
The general intelligence factor "g" is one of the most, if not the most replicated constructs in psychology, and its predictive validity is not in question. My question is its interpretation. The positive manifold is the current popular proof that intelligence is a scalar, a quantity along which people can in principle be ranked. I argue that such inference is wrong. Brains that have similar functionality differ significantly in architecture, and the properties they differ on (efficiency, specialization, cost, resilience) are particular trade-offs which form a Pareto front instead of a single optimum. On this front each different architecture that "excels" in different environments is incomparable to the others, as ranking them would require privileging a specific set of abilities to reward, and for human life none is favored. Intelligence, thus, is better described as a partial order rather than a line. Ranking remains legitimate between widely separated scores, where a whole-life average lets a large gap dominate; within a band of equal g, it does not. This account returns a prediction a scalar model cannot make: equally scoring individuals should cross over in performance as demands change. It also names the core assumption whose failure would reinstate g - that for a human life, no environment is privileged.

Charging the Gaps: Identifying Strategic Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Locations in Rural Ohio

Full text
For rural Ohio drivers, running low on battery charge isn't a minor inconvenience. It can mean stranding, detours, or abandoning the trip altogether. Despite Ohio's growing vehicle fleet, less than one percent are electric, and charging infrastructure remains concentrated in Columbus, Cleveland, and Cincinnati, leaving rural corridors without reliable access. Prior research on EV infrastructure has focused almost exclusively on urban centers and highway corridors, largely overlooking rural mobility needs.

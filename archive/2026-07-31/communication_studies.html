International Journal of Press-Politics

Media Use and Attitudes Toward Homosexuality: A Cross-National Analysis of the Moderating Roles of Polarization and Press Freedom

Lazar Dragić, Michael Chan

Full text
The media can have an influential role in shaping citizens’ attitudes toward marginalized groups in society, such as homosexuals. Drawing from a cross-national survey sample from 43 countries ( N = 57,583), this study showed that traditional media use was related to negative attitudes toward homosexuals, while digital media use was positively related. The relationships were also contingent on levels of country-level societal political polarization and press freedom. Higher levels of polarization weaken the relationship between media use and attitudes toward homosexuals across media types. Under higher press freedom, traditional media are associated with higher justification of homosexuality and higher rejection of homosexual neighbors, while digital media use is associated with lower rejection of homosexual neighbors. These findings demonstrate that the relationship between media use and attitudes is to some extent contextual on a country’s political and media environment.

Social Media + Society

Social Media and the Smart City: A Systematic Review of City- and Citizen-Led Digital Spaces

Max Schindler

Full text
This systematic review examines how social media is conceptualized and studied within the context of smart cities, with a particular focus on the distinction between city-led and citizen-led digital spaces. Analyzing a sample of 50 peer-reviewed articles, the review identifies dominant techno-functionalist approaches that frame social media primarily as a data source for urban optimization, event detection, and service delivery. While some studies gesture toward participatory or socially embedded uses of social media, the majority lack engagement with specific urban contexts or active representations of cities and their citizens. The review highlights a significant gap in research on the actually existing smart city and the sociocultural dimensions of urban digital life. The findings underscore the need for future research to move beyond top-down, efficiency-driven narratives and to critically engage with emerging technologies, such as generative artificial intelligence, in both institutional and grassroots urban media practices. The article calls for a reimagining of smart cities and smart citizenship that centers participatory and socially grounded perspectives.

When Herstory Goes Viral: Popular Resistance in the State-Platform Nexus in China

Qingyue Sun, Runze Ding

Full text
This article examines the rise of herstory content on Douyin to explore how feminist resistance takes shape within China’s platform environment. Drawing on digital ethnography, we develop the concept of popular resistance, a form of platform-mediated resistance whose popularity serves both as a condition of political traction and as a mechanism of containment. To clarify this duality, we identify two dimensions: (a) popularity on the platform, driven by trends, aestheticization, and commodification; and (b) popularity within orthodoxy, shaped by state-sanctioned values that render certain forms of feminist expression politically permissible. Popular resistance retains a critical, if slippery, edge. It gives voice to gendered injustices while remaining entangled with the algorithmic, commercial, and political infrastructures that simultaneously enable and discipline it. We conclude by proposing popular resistance as a transferable analytic for understanding how political expression in the digital age gains visibility, negotiates power, and persists within the very systems that constrain it.

New Media & Society

Presumed media influence and support for social media bans: The roles of social and elite-based validation

Sarah Geber, Anne Schulz, Mike S. Schäfer, Thomas N. Friemel

Full text
The study examines how presumed influences of social media on young people, as well as perceptions of how widely such beliefs are shared, shape support for social media bans. Extending the Influence of Presumed Influence (IPI) model, we introduce second-order presumed influences, defined as perceptions about how widely others presume media influences, and propose a mechanism of social validation. We additionally examine elite-based validation, referring to perceived beliefs of influential actors (i.e. scientists, politicians, and the media), as well as perceived social norms. Using survey data ( N = 1270) collected in the context of debates about a social media ban for minors under 16 in Switzerland, we find that stronger presumed influences predict support for a ban. While we find no support for social validation, the results point to the relevance of elite-related perceptions, particularly presumed scientific consensus. Perceived social norms of ban support emerge as an additional predictor of regulatory preferences.

Rethinking platform vernaculars: Collective practices and power dynamics in platform environments

Lydia Kollyri

Full text
In 2015, Gibbs et al. introduced the concept of platform vernaculars to describe how communicative genres emerge through interactions between platform affordances and user practices. Although widely adopted in media research, the concept has not been systematically refined in light of recent developments in affordance theory and platform studies. Drawing on an integrative literature review, this paper revisits platform vernaculars as plural, recognizable, repeatable, temporarily stabilized, and contested formations emerging through collective negotiations of affordances within platform environments. The paper further introduces four heuristic categories–intended vernaculars, dominant vernaculars, community vernaculars, and counter-public vernaculars–to discuss how communicative norms, genres, and modes of participation become normalized, marginalized, contested, or transformed across platforms. By rethinking platform vernaculars, the paper offers a systematic analytical framework for studying platform practices and norms. Furthermore, it positions vernaculars as a lens for analyzing platform power and user agency, opening avenues for future research.

Communication Research

The Bio-Functional Audience: Shared Physiological Alignment to Narrative Media

Ralf Schmälzle, Hee Jung Cho, Monique Mitchell Turner

Full text
Communication research into audience responses has traditionally emphasized how individual or group differences create variance in interpretation while overlooking a more fundamental phenomenon: the capacity of the same message to induce similar responses across receivers. We introduce a bio-functional definition of the audience, a temporal collective bound together by the moment-to-moment alignment of its members’ physiological responses. In a large-scale experiment ( N = 198) monitoring eye-tracking, heart rate, and electrodermal activity during a film, we mapped a processing hierarchy in which audience response alignment was strongest for gaze but also extended into downstream autonomic responses. Furthermore, a temporal manipulation of the narrative sequence abolished aligned responses, confirming that the induced audience state is driven by the message content. We conclude that messages function as alignment devices that reduce individual noise and bring distinct individuals into a shared processing state, thus providing a grounded, signal-based definition of the audience as a bio-functional entity.

Music Was My First Love: Biographic Resonance Makes Everyday Music Listening Meaningful

Felix Dietrich, Alicia Ernst, Benedikt Rohr, Michael Scharkow, Leonard Reinecke

Full text
Despite much research on eudaimonic media experiences, little is known about how and why certain content is perceived as meaningful by certain users in certain situations. We therefore examine biographic resonance — connections between entertainment content and users’ biographic memories — during everyday music listening on Spotify and propose that memory recall, as well as its emotional valence, relate to meaningfulness via distinct patterns of intrinsic need satisfaction. Results from a preregistered, intensive-longitudinal study (144 persons; 6,920 situations) reveal that biographic resonance relates to both high- and low-effort modes of eudaimonic entertainment. Moreover, by linking users’ self-reports with their tracked digital music use (2,786 sessions; 107,147 songs), we find that the occurrence and quality of biographic resonance vary across situations and are shaped by both content and contextual factors within algorithmically curated environments. Our results highlight how resonances between media users’ selves and entertainment content foster situational meaningfulness in everyday life.

Policy and Internet

Digital Futures Between Participatory Digital Democracy and Participatory Digital Fascism

Christian Fuchs, Kevin Friesch, Joel Museba

Full text
This paper explores one of the defining political questions of the twenty‐first century: will digital technologies strengthen democracy or accelerate new forms of authoritarianism and fascism? Against the backdrop of platform capitalism, AI‐driven communication, surveillance infrastructures, disinformation and democratic crisis, the study asks how experts in digital democracy assess the present condition and possible futures of digital society. To address this question, the paper draws on the Futures of Digital Democracy Survey (FDDS), a two‐round international expert survey involving researchers, practitioners and civil society actors working in the fields of digital democracy, media, Internet governance and digital society. Combining futures research, scenario analysis, and qualitative and quantitative content analysis, the study investigates competing trajectories of digital development ranging from participatory digital democracy to corporate and state‐controlled digital fascism. The findings reveal a profound tension at the heart of contemporary digital society: while respondents overwhelmingly imagine an ideal future democracy in participatory and deliberative terms—centred on citizens’ assemblies, digital participation, public‐service Internet platforms and democratic AI—they simultaneously express deep pessimism about the actual direction of digital capitalism and platform power. Experts consider authoritarian and fascistic digital futures significantly more likely than democratic ones. The paper analyses these contradictions by examining the relationship between deliberative democracy, constitutional rights, platform governance, digital commons, public service media, AI, surveillance and the political economy of Big Tech. It argues that democracy in the digital age can only survive if participatory and deliberative digital structures are institutionally combined with constitutional safeguards, public ownership, democratic regulation and the defence of human rights. By bringing together debates on digital democracy, authoritarianism, political economy and digital futures, the paper offers both a critical diagnosis of the contemporary digital condition and a normative vision of democratic alternatives in the age of AI and platform capitalism.

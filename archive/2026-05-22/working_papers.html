Transfiguring Smithian Liberalism: The Intellectual Legacy of Hiroshi Mizuta and Hideo Tanaka in Japan

JHET Assistant, Shinji Nohara

Full text
Historians have noted how Smith’s name has been invoked to justify liberal economic policies in diverse national contexts. The historical elasticity and political implications of Smithian economic liberalism become clearer by examining the case of Japan. The previous studies of the reception of Smith in Japan have not sufficiently explored its liberal implications after the postwar period. Attention to these implications is essential for understanding not only the popularity of Smith studies in Japan but also the rapid growth of research on the Scottish Enlightenment from the 1970s onward, a period when the field remained comparatively small outside Britain. These two distinctive features of Japanese scholarship are closely connected, yet their relationship has not been systematically analyzed. This paper argues that this connection becomes clearer when examined through the intellectual trajectories of Hiroshi Mizuta and Hideo Tanaka, two influential scholars on the studies of Smith and the Scottish Enlightenment.

Thomas Edward Cliffe Leslie and the political economy of British land systems: From the critique of the classical landed property reform to the birth of Irish historical economics

JHET Assistant, Manolis Manioudis

Full text
Thomas Edward Cliffe Leslie is an influential figure in the Irish history of economic thought. This paper presents Leslie’s critical appraisal of British land systems and discusses his critique towards the classical landed property reform. The paper illustrates the affinities between Leslie’s and John Stuart Mill's economic thought regarding the Irish land question. Furthermore, it shows that Leslie’s support for small proprietorship in Ireland is regarded as a prerequisite for defending the Act of Union of 1800. Lastly, the paper argues that Leslie’s political economy of British land systems is a significant stream in the foundation of the Irish historical school and in the promotion of an alternative political economy.

Concentrated Affluence: The Spatial Segregation of High-Income Households across Richmond, VA

Billy Southern

Full text
Researchers examining issues of inequality focus overwhelmingly on racially segregated neighborhoods, concentrated poverty, and spatial isolation. Such focus is appropriate, given that spatial concentration amplifies the harms of poverty. But this overemphasis has driven policymakers to prioritize mitigating concentration, as evidenced by numerous dispersal policies. In contrast, scholars pay far less attention to the opposite extreme: the concentration of affluent households. The spatial concentration of affluence enables advantaged households to hoard opportunity, skew resource distribution, limit access to public amenities, and consolidate political power. Across public discourse, concentrated affluence is routinely treated as neutral, and policymakers rarely pursue strategies to confront this consequential component of segregation. This study focuses on the segregation of affluence, examining changes in the concentration of high-income households since 1990. Using Richmond, Virginia, as a case study, findings demonstrate that affluent households are the most unevenly distributed group, rapidly segregating in the city and growing more concentrated in the suburbs. This paper makes two contributions: First, it captures changes in the geography of poverty and affluence by tracking low- and high-income households. Second, it provides an intraurban analysis of segregation, detailing household sorting across urban and suburban spaces. Growing spatial segregation of affluent households reveals a planning system that privileges the advantaged, and a focus on concentrated affluence is essential for urban policy to address spatial inequality.

Policing the Screen, Screening the Policed: Immigrant Representation, Surveillance, and Racial Capitalism in Icelandic Cinema

Armando Garcia

Full text
Abstract: This article develops an anthropological reading of immigrant representation in Icelandic cinema through the lens of racial capitalism. Focusing on Ísold Uggadóttir's Andið eðlilega / And Breathe Normally (2018), alongside Tryggð / The Deposit (2019), Gullregn (2020), Kona fer í stríð / Woman at War (2018), Ikíngut (2000), Ófærð / Trapped (2015–), and a wider corpus of films and series, it argues that recent Icelandic audio-visual production renders visible the surveillance and policing infrastructures through which racialised mobility is governed at the Nordic periphery. The article concludes with policy implications for anti-racist reform of Icelandic border and policing practices, and for public support of migrant-centred film-making.

The Second-Generation Boom: U.S.-born Children of Immigrants’ Growing Contribution to Working-Age Demographics, Socioeconomics, and Health

Leafia Ye, Neil Mehta

Full text
Since the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act, an increased number of foreign-born individuals have been renewing the U.S. labor force and countering population aging. While immigration is currently slowing down, the second-generation population is reaching prime working age in growing numbers. In this study, we use the Current Population Survey to document demographic trends from 2000-25 and quantify the impact of the second-generation on the labor force. We show that the second-generation population aged 25-64 has doubled since the turn of the century, accounting for more than half of the labor force growth in three out of five largest states. The median age of the population has declined by five years over this period, even as it has increased for the first- and third+ generations. Although we observe many subgroup differences that echo well-documented patterns of stratification within immigrants, we also show that the working-age second-generation are overall demonstrating strong socioeconomic and health outcomes. Together with their rising population share, the second-generation has already played an important role in countering a decline in the overall labor force participation rate and driving improvement in population health.

Philosophy as Cognitive Assay: Measuring the Delegation Legitimacy Boundary in AI-Assisted Knowledge Work

Kengo Tomita

Full text
This article operationalizes the concept of a delegation legitimacy boundary — the structural line along which human judgment can and cannot be legitimately delegated to artificial intelligence — and proposes a minimal scoring protocol for locating it within any knowledge-work task. Building on the Mekiki framework (Tomita 2026), which distinguished specification — the task-defining substrate of domain expertise — from externalization cost — the technical barrier that AI selectively removes — the present article derives a further decomposition within specification itself. Drawing on case-study evidence in which recorded specification judgments contain separable factual components (Sein-type: what the data structure affords, how users will perceive a display) and value-laden components (Sollen-type: what information ought to be excluded, which priority ranking is appropriate), the article grounds this distinction in Hume’s is–ought separation and Kant’s Sein/Sollen architecture, and redeploys it as the axis of a cognitive assay — a measurement system in which philosophical categories serve not as normative prescriptions but as diagnostic coordinates. The resulting five-step scoring protocol assigns Sein-type components to AI evaluation and Sollen-type components to domain-expert evaluation; this asymmetry is not a design choice but a structural consequence of the boundary itself, which holds as long as legitimacy over value judgments remains institutionally human-attributed. Most individual judgments are hybrid, carrying both components in varying ratios; the protocol therefore yields ratio profiles rather than binary classifications. As a first application, the article re-describes the four processes of Nonaka and Takeuchi’s Socialization–Externalization–Combination–Internalization (SECI) model through the assay, deriving as an analytic consequence the finding that AI acceleration of Externalization and Combination shifts the effective rate-limiting stage to Socialization and Internalization — both human-limited cognitive and social processes that cannot be accelerated by AI investment alone.

Layer-Skipping Disclosure in Ritualized Containers: Sanctioned Shadow Surfacing as a Mechanism Beyond Social Penetration Theory

Meng-Han Lee

Full text
Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) holds that self-disclosure proceeds gradually from outer to inner layers, with rapid disclosure typically explained by anonymity or reduced future accountability. We identify a theoretically anomalous phenomenon current frameworks cannot accommodate: layer-skipping disclosure in fully non-anonymous, sustained-relationship contexts. This occurs among high-functioning socialized individuals—those who maintain a strategic, self-aware adapted false self as social competence rather than reactive defense. We propose Sanctioned Shadow Surfacing (SSS) as a mechanism-level account of how ritualized social fields make masked self-state content discloseable under accountable relational conditions. Here this content takes the form of self-aware but routinely masked shadow aspects ('shadow' used descriptively rather than metaphysically). Ritualized subcultural containers operate as dual-permission structures: the field grants specific invitation to self-state surfacing while the individual actively elects to un-mask. The driving force is recognition-by-peer (the discloser's confidence that the witness possesses interpretive competence to receive the content intelligibly) rather than reciprocity or reduced social cost. We illustrate through two cases along an intensity spectrum: BDSM scenes and Monsterhearts 2 tabletop role-play. We distinguish disclosure from integration, clarifying what such containers can and cannot deliver. [Note: This paper is currently under peer review.]

Max Amuchie

Full text
In this landmark inaugural paper, Max Amuchie, architect of The Insecurity Triad and the Trinity of State Decay theory, introduces the MLM Framework (Money, Land, and Mind) as a definitive analytical architecture for diagnosing the structural collapse of state sovereignty. Published as part of the Sundiata Post Intelligence Unit (SPIU) Working Paper Series, the study posits that kidnapping, banditry, and terrorism function as a synergistic "Insecurity Triad"—a convergence of mutually constitutive forces. This research contests conventional theories of "state failure," arguing instead that these forces produce a sophisticated structural contest over legitimate authority. While Nigeria serves as the generative case study, the framework’s logic is universal: it travels wherever these three forces coalesce to replace institutional governance with shadow sovereignty. The paper argues that contemporary security discourse is fundamentally flawed, as it remains tethered to isolated threat categories that fail to perceive the convergent logic of modern insecurity. THE INSECURITY TRIAD provides the necessary corrective, offering an integrated strategic architecture that positions state recovery not as the repair of a "failed" entity, but as the deliberate production of a new political and security equilibrium.

AI-Powered CBT Chatbots for Young Adults: A Systematic Review of Efficacy, Engagement, and Ethical Concerns

Afrida Zahin

Full text
Background: AI-powered CBT chatbots are a promising way to scale mental health interventions based on demand, eliminating hurdles such as cost and social stigma. Young adults are an especially vulnerable subset of the population, who could potentially benefit from these digital mental health interventions. Objective: This systematic literature review aimed to determine the factors of AI-powered CBT chatbots that promote or impede young adult engagement, as well as the effectiveness and ethical considerations. Methods: In this review, 24 papers were systematically screened from PubMed (primary) and Google Scholar within the timeline 2015 to 2026. Papers studying CBT chatbots and population consisting of 18 to 39 years were included in this review. Results: AI-powered CBT chatbots have a small to moderate effect in alleviating symptoms of depression, anxiety and hopelessness. Quality engagement improves the effectiveness of these chatbots, however, sustained adherence is an issue. Users engage mainly due to need, followed by anonymity, non-judgemental nature, human-like support and round the clock availability. Overall, these digital interventions are promising but have ethical concerns such as inadequacy in risk identification, algorithmic bias, transparency issues and risk of AI psychosis. Conclusion: Overall, AI-powered CBT chatbots demonstrate potential for scalability of mental health interventions to young adults (18 to 39 years). Apart from some technological and trust related ethical concerns, these digital interventions have proven to be engaging, as well as effective on a short-term basis.

Participatory Descriptive Phenomenology: a methodological framework

Mark Adley, stephanie scott, Amy O'Donnell

Full text
This methodological paper presents a participatory adaptation of descriptive phenomenology, co-produced with public contributors. Traditionally, descriptive phenomenological analysis is performed by a sole trained analyst responsible for conducting bracketing, reduction, and essence-seeking. In contrast, we explore how these processes can be shared within collaborative partnerships. Through iterative development and reflexive discussion, we adapted key phenomenological processes to make them usable and meaningful for public contributors. In doing so, we reoriented the epistemic assumptions of phenomenological inquiry, with meaning-making situated within relational, participatory processes. Public contributors are seldom involved in data analysis, or when they are, are asked to assess themes already generated by researchers. We argue that a participatory descriptive phenomenology offers a promising model for inclusive, culturally grounded qualitative research. Our methodological reflections contribute to debates on power, co-production, and epistemic justice, and demonstrate how descriptive phenomenology can be expanded methodologically and theoretically to bring people’s lived experiences to the fore.

The reasons and aims for listening to music while going to sleep: results from an ESM study

Rory Kirk, William Randall, Suvi Saarikallio

Full text
Current trends in music research are emphasising the importance of contextual factors in the use and effects of listening to music, such as mood, situation, and emotional goals. This undoubtedly has implications for our understanding of the use of music for wellness strategies, such as helping going to sleep. Using data collected in previous experience sampling studies, we conducted an exploratory investigation on listener behaviours in the context of going to sleep with accounts reported in real-life situations. Compared to other listening activities, we found that listeners going to sleep mainly used music to relax, which corresponded with increased valence, varying arousal changes, and diverse music choices. Beyond the Takeover: Youth Gatherings, Urban Public Space, and the Racialization of Disorder in Chicago

Almethia C. Franklin

Full text
In recent years, Chicago has witnessed a significant rise in large-scale youth gatherings in high-visibility public spaces. Labeled "teen takeovers" in dominant media and policy discourse, these events are almost exclusively framed as public safety threats requiring law enforcement response. This paper argues that this framing is both analytically insufficient and politically consequential. Drawing on preliminary media and news analysis, an emerging qualitative research agenda, and a multi-disciplinary theoretical framework, this paper reframes youth gatherings as social phenomena that reveal fundamental dynamics of race, space, territorial boundary, and urban belonging. We identify three dimensions that existing scholarship has not yet systematically examined: (1) the role of social media and digital technology in organizing, escalating, and documenting these events; (2) the apparent dissolution of hyperlocal territorial boundaries historically maintained by street organizations in Chicago; and (3) the variation in form, activity, and framing of analogous youth gatherings across U.S. cities. We situate these dimensions within a five-part theoretical architecture drawing on moral panic theory, critical criminology, spatial theory, Black cultural studies, and the sociology of punishment. We conclude by presenting a research agenda for empirical investigation and discussing the implications of this work for policy and community response. Keywords: youth gatherings, teen takeovers, urban public space, moral panic, territorial boundaries, Chicago, racialization, social media, cultural criminology

Risk Aversion with Nothing to Lose

Stefano Pegoraro

Full text
In a continuous-time model, a risk-neutral decision-maker chooses the volatility of a state variable and is terminated when the variable falls below a threshold. I provide economically interpretable conditions under which the decision-maker becomes risk averse endogenously and minimizes volatility near termination, even if she faces myopic incentives to gamble for resurrection. The conditions introduce forward-looking incentives to preserve economic rents. I show these conditions are met in a wide range of apparently unrelated models, thus identifying forward-looking rents as a unifying economic mechanism behind endogenous risk aversion. I also provide conditions for the decision-maker to become risk loving endogenously.

Yi Duan

Full text
This paper examines how the Hakka people of southern Jiangxi, China, sustain a three-thousand-year cultural memory of Shang Dynasty elephant domestication through the "Nine Lions Worship Elephant" lantern festival. Drawing on archaeological evidence, paleographic analysis, historical geography, climate history, and ethnographic observation, I trace a symbol transmission chain from the Central Plains "Elephant Land" to a ritualized homecoming ceremony performed in villages that have never hosted live elephants. Institutionalizing Nature's Contributions to People: How a Chinese County Fabricated Tradeable Ecological Assets Through Sequential Governance Innovation

Yi Duan

Full text
The gap between ecosystem services valuation and practical monetization remains a persistent governance challenge, particularly in underdeveloped regions where ecological endowments are richest and fiscal capacity is weakest. This paper presents an in depth case study of Shangyou County, Jiangxi Province, China, which sequentially constructed an ecosystem services monetization architecture through four interlocking institutional mechanisms: (1) GEP/VEP accounting that rendered ecological value legible to financial actors; (2) a centralized state owned platform that consolidated scattered ecological resources; (3) market creating instruments, including China's first collapsed hill arable land indicator trade (RMB 344,960), the nation's second soil and water conservation carbon sink trade (70,000 tons CO₂e, RMB 2.1 million), and Jiangxi Province's largest VEP backed green loan (RMB 200 million); and (4) a zero carbon industrial park that anchored long term sustainability. Drawing on the IPBES framework of Nature's Contributions to People (NCP), the analysis reveals a cumulative sequential logic in which each mechanism created the preconditions for the next: measurement enabled consolidation, consolidation enabled market creation, and market creation provided the financial and policy foundation for industrial anchoring. The case contributes to the ecosystem services literature by demonstrating that the institutionalization of NCP is not a menu of independent options but a path dependent process in which sequencing determines outcomes. The findings carry implications for Payments for Ecosystem Services design, natural capital accounting, and the governance of green finance in developing economies.

Reinventing Well-being Measurements for Older Persons: Psychometric Evaluation of the Older Persons’ Well-being Scale (OPWELLS)

Jeanette Melin, Marit Preuter, Kristofer Årestedt, Marie-Louise Möllerberg

Full text
Purpose: To establish a set of items with good content coverage and satisfactory measurement properties for measuring older persons’ well-being; the Older Persons’ Well-being Scale (OPWELLS). Method: A two-stage cross-sectional psychometric design was employed among older persons living in the community. In the first stage (n =441), the focus was on identifying potentially redundant items. In the second stage (n =1,882), stepwise item removal and/or alternation were conducted to optimise the balance between content coverage and measurement properties. Rasch analyses were used to evaluate measurement properties. Results: The first-stage study revealed issues primarily related to local dependency, leading to the removal of four items prior to the second-stage study. The second-stage study yielded a set of 10 items with good content representation and satisfactory measurement properties, as well as convergent validity with general health and life satisfaction. Conclusion: The 10-item OPWELLS provides a practical and psychometrically robust scale for measuring older persons’ well-being. Although there is no simple solution to challenges of limited content heterogeneity and insufficient measurement quality in existing well-being scales, this study represents an important step toward addressing both. By combining systematic content refinement with Rasch analysis, the OPWELLS contribute with a scale that strengthens the foundation for future research and practice.

Executivism, the Parallel State, and the Paradoxical Path to Restoring Democratic Accountability

William Resh

Full text
Rebuilding democratic accountability after a period of personalist consolidation requires more than reversing executive orders or reinstating dismissed personnel. The SIMC Framework: Aligning Academic Research and Government Practice in Field Experiments

Jae Yeon Kim, Florian Keppeler

Full text
Field experiments offer governments a credible route to causal evidence about what works. Yet translating experimental designs into practice remains difficult: researchers prioritize scientific rigor, while practitioners emphasize operational feasibility. We conceptualize this tension as an alignment problem. We introduce the SIMC framework (Scoping, Implementation, Measurement, and Communication), which identifies four stages where collaboration between researchers and practitioners can break down or succeed. Drawing on the COM-B model, we analyze the capability, opportunity, and motivation required to sustain collaboration. Using examples from field experiments in the U.S. and Europe, we show how trade-offs between rigor and feasibility emerge at each stage. We translate the framework into a stage-by-stage diagnostic that researchers and government agencies can apply before, during, and after fieldwork. Unlike existing guidance that treats collaboration as knowledge transfer, the SIMC framework treats it as an ongoing relational process with identifiable failure points.

A Measurement Framework for Detecting High-Velocity Events in Social Media

Peter C. Baker

Full text
This paper presents a measurement framework for detecting high-velocity events in social media using time-resolved post tracking. Rather than relying on static snapshots, the framework repeatedly samples posts over time and reconstructs their growth trajectories from timestamped observations. Inflectional Paradigms as a Diagnostic Tool for Tokenizers in Morphologically Rich Languages: A Proposal for a Linguistic Benchmark

Elżbieta Dawidek

Full text
This article addresses the problem of evaluating the morphological quality of tokenizers used in natural language processing models for synthetic languages. The starting point is the observation that the standard measure of fertility ratio — the mean number of tokens per inflected form — captures the problem of fragmentation quantitatively but does not diagnose its morphological structure. A tokenizer achieving a low fertility ratio may nonetheless systematically cut morpheme boundaries at grammatically significant points, destroying information about the lexical stem, inflectional suffixes, or derivational prefixes. Based on empirical analysis of four tokenizers with different architectures (BPE, WordPiece, Unigram LM) and training corpus compositions (monolingual Polish, monolingual English, multilingual), conducted on 109 Polish inflected forms across full paradigms of 10 lexemes, this article proposes a linguistic benchmark based on three metrics: stem integrity (SI), inflectional suffix separation (ISS), and mean fragmentation per lexeme (MFL). Results show a 3.2x ratio between the monolingual Polish tokenizer (HerBERT BPE-PL, MFL = 1.22) and the monolingual English tokenizer (GPT-2 BPE-EN, MFL = 3.91), and indicate that tokenizer architecture has independent explanatory power: XLM-R Unigram (MFL = 1.66) with a multilingual training corpus approaches the monolingual tokenizer more closely than mBERT WordPiece (MFL = 2.45) with comparable data composition. The proposed benchmark is designed as minimal yet sufficient: aspect and conditional mood do not require separate diagnostic categories, because their behavior follows structurally from the behavior of the prefix and suffix that the benchmark measures directly. Inferring these categories from a correctly tokenized form is a task for the language model, not the tokenizer. This article continues research published in the preprint Dawidek (2026a) and constitutes a proof of concept for an evaluation tool that fills the gap between performance-based and linguistic assessment of tokenizers in morphologically rich languages.

Perceptions of the Ideal Mother: An Experimental Investigation

Nevena Kulic, Maria-Valentina Stoicescu, Anne-Marie Therese Jeannet, Giulia Dotti Sani

Full text
The normative framework that defines ideal motherhood, prescribing what it means to be a “good mother” in a given cultural and historical context, significantly influences women’s career aspirations, family choices, and fertility decisions. Yet, there is limited understanding of what women themselves consider to be “ideal motherhood”, particularly given that such ideals may reflect both genuine preferences and adaptations to structural constraints. Gaining insight into both personal and perceived societal ideals is crucial for evaluating the extent to which women’s views of motherhood align with, or diverge from, prevailing social expectations. This study builds on hegemonic and non-hegemonic models of motherhood differing in the degree of maternal involvement, the role of maternal employment, and the division of childcare responsibilities. In particular, maternal employment (whether full- or part-time) and the sharing of childcare responsibilities with fathers emerge as central components of the ideal mother. These results challenge conventional assumptions about women’s endorsement of the dominant notion of mothering, offering new insight into shifting motherhood ideals in contemporary Italy.

Intergroup Violence Without Essentialist Beliefs: The Case of Turkana Cattle Raiders

Matthew Zefferman, Sarah Mathew

Full text
Essentialism has been hypothesized to be associated with intergroup hostility. However, it is unclear whether ethnic essentialism is a feature of human social cognition across a range of socio-ecological settings and what the causal direction of its association with intergroup hostility is. One possibility is that essentialist beliefs cause intergroup violence because they make violence on members of out-groups more palatable. An alternative hypothesis is that essentialist beliefs may develop as a response to intergroup violence. Using “switched at birth” vignettes to assess how intergenerationally inherited and fixed people’s identities are, we assessed the extent to which Turkana pastoralist warriors have essentialist beliefs about themselves and the neighboring Toposa ethnic group in South Sudan. The Turkana and Toposa engage in mutually lethal cattle raids, which account for about half of adult Turkana male mortality in our study area. We found that only four of 162 (2.5%) Turkana surveyed expressed essentialist beliefs about themselves and the Toposa, suggesting that ethnic essentialism may not be a cross-culturally widespread feature of social cognition nor a necessary ideology for engaging in intergroup hostilities. The four participants who expressed essentialist beliefs did not exhibit unusual combat-related behavior and attitudes towards the outgroup. Specifically, they were no different than non-essentialist survey respondents in their levels of combat exposure, killing of enemies in combat, witnessing morally injurious events, and moral judgments about killing Toposa. However, one of the four was an outlier in offensive raiding and killing, anecdotally suggesting that essentialism may contribute to, but be insufficient for, very prolific raiding in individual Turkana. The cultural similarity between the Toposa and Turkana, a common origin story, well-known examples of cross-group adoption and assimilation, and a tradition of cross-border friendships may explain the Turkana’s low frequency of essentialist beliefs despite widespread lethal violence.

JHET Assistant, Paolo Santori

Full text

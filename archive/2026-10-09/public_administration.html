Artificial Intelligence and Punctuated Equilibrium: Shaping Government Attention in the Digital Age

Shuo Chen, Yiran Li, Yuzheng Wang

Full text
How do government attention and policy agendas shift when information constraints are relaxed through technological advancement? Punctuated equilibrium theory links agenda‐setting dynamics to governments' limited information‐processing capabilities, yet we know little about whether and how technology improvement such as artificial intelligence (AI) influences policy agendas. This article proposes a conditional digital punctuation framework to specify the conditions under which technological advancements drive agenda shifts. Using China's implementation of AI initiatives as a quasi‐natural experiment, we find that government attention exhibits a punctuated pattern. AI increases technology‐focused attention, signaling incremental changes, while having minimal impact on general government attention. This selective impact stems from AI's asymmetric effects on information processing. Specifically, AI significantly increases the procurement‐based indicator of information quantity, which is associated with greater attention to technology sectors; however, no significant change is observed in the corresponding indicator of information quality, consistent with its limited impact on general government attention.

Hindered From Making a Difference: How Increasing Organizational Constraints Undermine Public Servants' Perceptions of Prosocial Impact and Why It Matters

Caroline Manville, Karim Mignonac

Full text
Public servants often work under organizational constraints that hinder how they experience and carry out their work. Drawing on conservation of resources theory, this research examines how changes in these constraints affect public servants' perceptions that their work benefits others and, in turn, their well‐being and withdrawal behavior. Using data from two longitudinal studies conducted in the United States and France, the findings show that increases in organizational constraints are associated with declines in perceived prosocial impact, which in turn predict lower well‐being and greater withdrawal over time. These results identify perceived prosocial impact as a key psychological resource that links changing work conditions to employee functioning. By emphasizing the dynamic nature of organizational constraints, this study advances the understanding of how evolving administrative conditions can weaken the psychological foundations that sustain meaningful and effective public service.

A Response to Don Kettl's Debate Article on the Future of Public Administration and Democracy

György Hajnal

Full text
This commentary critically engages with Kettl's analysis of the crisis of public administration (PA) scholarship and its relationship with democratic decline. It argues that both phenomena should be understood within broader global developments rather than primarily as problems specific to the United States. While acknowledging the role of governance failures in facilitating autocratization, it questions the claim that autocratization results predominantly from the failures of PA scholarship and practice. Rather, it emphasizes that broader societal phenomena, such as the erosion of societal trust and the power‐consolidating objectives of authoritarian leaders play a key role. Drawing on Hungary's experience, the commentary illustrates how authoritarian governance may perpetuate administrative instability and institutional transformation long after the (perceived) failures of democratic governance have been addressed in the early phase of illiberal transformation. However, it also argues that poor governance and declining policy performance can eventually undermine authoritarian rule, as illustrated by Hungary's 2026 political transformation. The commentary thus highlights the complex and potentially self‐undermining relationship between authoritarianism and public governance.

Is Public Sector a Better Place for Moms to Work? Exploring Sector Difference in Child Penalties on Women

Fangda Ding, Yifan Chen

Full text
With commonly stabler and more flexible working environment, is public sector a better place for moms to work? Theorizing child penalties from sector difference perspective, we argue that childbirth may cause less gender gaps in unemployment and earnings but greater gender disparities in working time commitments in the public sector since job stability and family‐friendliness may form gender norms that depreciate women's work in the public sector. We test our hypotheses by employing event study designs using data from China Family Panel Studies (2010–2022). We found that women public employees experienced smaller child penalties in unemployment and earnings while greater child penalties in working hours than private counterparts, which can be explained by greater family responsibilities and spousal depreciation of women's jobs after childbirth. This paper advances theories of gender equity in public administration by proposing distinct child penalties in the public sector that challenges conventional beliefs.

A Public Philosophy for Public Administration in Times of Democratic Crisis

Curtis Ventriss, Tina Nabatchi

Full text
Public administration is at the center of the global democratic crisis. Meeting this challenge requires more than managerial and analytic competence—it requires a public philosophy grounded in the first principles of democratic ethos. We offer such a philosophy, drawing on four seminal thinkers whose concepts reflect the conditions public administrators face in times of democratic crisis. Judith Shklar's liberalism of fear establishes a negative obligation to refuse cruelty and resist its institutionalization. Alasdair MacIntyre's internal goods of practice create a positive obligation to cultivate virtue and protect the integrity of administrative practice. Isaiah Berlin's agony of choice obligates administrators to acknowledge irreducible value conflicts and their consequential impact on democratic life. Hannah Arendt's enlarged mentality provides a framework for thinking across perspectives so that judgment under conflict becomes democratically defensible. Together, these concepts form a public philosophy for public administration with practical significance for administrative judgment, organizational culture, and institutional courage.

Making Policy Evidence Traceable: A Modular Preregistration Framework for Policy Evaluation

Yiyun Xia, Tianhao Zhao, Yuhao Ba

Full text
Preregistration is increasingly used in policy research, but its value depends on whether preregistered commitments can be clearly linked to reported analyses. Policy studies often involve multiple outcomes, evolving administrative data, consequential design and analytic choices, and adaptive implementation, making it difficult to distinguish prespecified tests from subsequent adjustments. This research note proposes a modular, policy‐oriented companion framework for using existing preregistration infrastructure. The framework combines a shared core that defines the estimand, outcomes, and identification strategy with design‐specific modules for field experiments, administrative‐data quasi‐experiments, and behavioral policy trials. Together, the components serve two purposes: claim traceability, by linking reported results to the original plan, and governed adaptation, by providing a transparent record of changes made during the study. The aim is not to prevent adaptation, but to show which analyses were prespecified, which were revised through documented amendments, and which were developed later. This approach aims to improve the interpretability and transparency of policy research while remaining feasible in applied policy settings.

From Public to Private Governance, and Back—Eco‐Modulation as De‐Privatization in Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

Harri Kalimo, Antti Jukka, Reid Lifset, Petrus Kautto

Full text
The European Union is advancing circular economy policies, aimed at reducing the environmental impacts of products across their life‐cycles. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) exemplifies this strategy. It privatizes the end‐of‐life management of products to their producers, seeking thereby to incentivize the producer to design environmentally more sustainable products. However, EPR has failed to deliver significant improvements in product design. This article examines the EU's attempts to strengthen eco‐design in EPR through ‘eco‐modulation’. Applying the theory of competence‐and‐control, the article argues that the introduction of eco‐modulation will destabilize the public‐private balance in EPR, shifting EPR back from private towards public governance. This de‐privatization is expected to refine EPR's role within the EU's policy mix. Our findings thus offer insights for the development of EPR‐related circular economy policies, while also contributing to theories of privatization.

Regulatory Learning or Regulatory Privilege? How Sandbox Design Shapes the Governance Value of Experimental Regulation

János Kálmán

Full text
Regulatory sandboxes are controlled, time‐limited environments in which selected innovations are tested under regulatory supervision. This article asks how sandbox design can make selective experimentation capable of generating public regulatory value rather than regulatory privilege. It develops regulatory learning asymmetry to distinguish private, supervisory, and public regulatory learning and unequal distributions of access, information, learning, and benefits. A structured reading of public documents from five deliberately diverse FinTech and artificial intelligence cases illustrates a six‐element operational framework. The case material is not used to infer private or supervisory learning; it records only publication, generalization mechanisms, and documented uptake. The framework identifies a public‐learning configuration, a supervisory‐learning configuration that public documents cannot verify, and a regulatory‐privilege risk configuration. Market signaling is treated as a cross‐cutting dynamic. The article offers a bounded diagnostic for assessing whether case‐specific experience becomes contestable and institutionally usable knowledge.

International Organization

Unrest, Shifting Power, and Death-Watch War

Jesse C. Johnson, Scott Wolford

Full text
Domestic unrest often creates problems of both shifting power and shifting preferences. We analyze a model in which a foreign state considers a “death-watch” attack against a domestic state temporarily weakened by unrest in which (1) two polarized factions compete for the power to set policy in the future and (2) the foreign state’s favored faction may support an invasion. Military success allows the foreign state to seize policy control, but defeat may hasten the rise of its disfavored faction when the favored faction links the outcomes of the two conflicts by bandwagoning with the attacker. In equilibrium, decisions to attack and bandwagon depend on the interaction of shifting power and shifting preferences. We show first that temporary weakness causes war, but only when the favored faction is unlikely to survive unrest. Second, increasing factional polarization can encourage or discourage foreign attack and local bandwagoning, conditional on how domestic preferences are shifting. Third, extensions show that (1) death-watch attacks are possible at any distribution of power; (2) the threat of attack may not be sufficient to deter domestic unrest; and (3) attack may be profitable even when alternative options of aiding a favored faction are available. We also use the model to explain why similar episodes of unrest in Ukraine prompted Russian invasion in 2014 but not 2004.

Contestation of International Organizations: Patterns, Drivers, and Responses

Jonas Tallberg, Stefanie Walter, Michael Zürn

Full text
The contestation of international organizations (IOs) over past decades has inspired a rich research program. This essay takes stock of progress by reviewing key findings in existing scholarship and identifying critical areas for future study. We propose a typology that distinguishes between internal contestation, which criticizes the institutions or policies of an IO with the aim of reform, and external contestation, which challenges an IO’s foundational principles and very existence. Building on this conceptual distinction, we review the most important findings of existing research on three key themes: patterns of contestation, drivers of contestation, and responses to contestation. We also argue that this conceptualization helps us better understand the changing nature of contestation: while criticism of how IOs function has long been a staple of world politics, it recently appears to have shifted toward a deeper form involving more fundamental questioning of IOs themselves. We conclude by highlighting the need for dynamic theories and improved measurements to explain when and how IO contestation shifts from internal criticism to external rejection, and by outlining a forward-looking research agenda on organizational robustness, hegemonic contestation, and the relationship between IO contestation and the broader contestation of global governance.

Review of International Organizations

Deliberating international politics: Listening and consensus-orientation in the United Nations Security Council

Gisela Hirschmann, Monika Heupel, Evgenija Kröker

Full text
The United Nations Security Council (UNSC) is typically seen as a forum in which states engage in bargaining: Powerful states pressure others to vote in their favor, and weaker states go along with this in exchange for support on other issues. We complement this view by redirecting the analytical lens from voting outcomes to the discursive processes that shape them. We argue that UNSC members also engage in deliberative behavior – specifically, listening and consensus-orientation – to foster cooperative solutions. We suggest that variation in deliberative practices can be explained by selected debate- and speaker-related factors. To test our claims, we analyze 27,812 speeches from UNSC meetings between 1995 and 2020, using a custom dictionary to identify deliberative cues, and corroborate our findings through six interviews with current and former UNSC members. We show that listening and consensus-orientation are indeed central to UNSC debates but vary based on the timing and format of a debate, the contestedness of the issue, and the economic wealth of a speaker’s country. This article calls on scholars to take deliberative behavior in the study of UNSC politics seriously to develop a more comprehensive understanding of international cooperation.

Journal of Peace Research

Ideology, revolutionary redistribution, and mobilization in civil war

Ning He, Wenbing Wu

Full text
Armed groups frequently intervene in civilian life during civil war, but the consequences of such interventions for their military mobilization remain poorly understood. We argue that revolutionary social intervention, when driven by a radical ideology, induces a trade-off for armed groups in military mobilization. While radical social intervention effectively mobilizes ideologically congruent supporters, it imposes significant costs on the broader population and discourages their participation in the revolutionary army. We illustrate this argument with evidence from the Chinese Civil War (1946–1949), focusing on how revolutionary land reform by the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) affected its wartime military mobilization. Leveraging a novel dataset on about 220,000 martyred CCP soldiers, we estimate how land reform affected the CCP’s recruitment of soldiers and their battle death rates with a difference-in-differences design. We find a substantial decrease in the CCP’s recruitment of new soldiers after land reform, consistent with historical accounts emphasizing the campaign’s extensive victimization of civilians. Additionally, we find that soldiers who enlisted after land reform died at significantly higher rates, suggesting that the campaign drew more ideologically committed recruits with greater combat motivation into the CCP’s army. Our findings shed new light on how ideology shapes mobilization outcomes in civil war.

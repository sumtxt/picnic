The Representation Trap: How Minority Power Shapes Electoral Politics in India

FEYAAD ALLIE

Full text
Liberal democracy requires that minority groups be represented in politics. This article argues that minority representation can also generate headwinds for political inclusion by dividing the minority group and consolidating the dominant group. I study the combination of these processes, termed the representation trap, through a mixed-methods examination of Muslims in India. Analyses of election results and an original survey of Hindu and Muslim voters show that electing a Muslim representative divides Muslim votes across an increased share of coethnic candidates and consolidates Hindu votes for the majoritarian party in the subsequent election. Qualitative evidence and a vignette experiment trace these patterns to two mechanisms: sub-identity activation among Muslims and super-ordinate identity mobilization among Hindus. By showing how representation restructures elite and voter behavior within both minority and dominant groups, the article highlights the dynamic nature of ethnic politics and complicates the view of minorities as politically unified.

Crime and Participation

DAVID DOHERTY, G. AGUSTIN MARKARIAN, MENA WHALEN, DANA GARBARSKI

Full text
Does exposure to crime mobilize or demobilize political participation? We report findings from a preregistered design, leveraging data from an original survey of 21 major U.S. cities and five waves of the Cooperative Election Study. We assess the relationships between direct victimization, local (ZCTA-level) crime rates, and perceived neighborhood safety and two outcomes: turnout (including validated turnout) and non-electoral participation. We find that direct victimization is significantly associated with more non-electoral engagement but lower turnout. The negative estimated effects of victimization on turnout exceed the magnitude of positive effects associated with common get-out-the-vote efforts. We find only limited evidence that crime rates are associated with participation. Perceived neighborhood safety is associated with higher rates of non-electoral, but not electoral, participation. Our findings offer new, comprehensive evidence on the relationship between crime exposure and political participation and illustrate the importance of distinguishing between types of exposure and forms of participation.

When Can Clientelism Serve Democratic Ends? A Democratic Theory of Distributive Politics

SAMUEL ELY BAGG, UDIT BHATIA

Full text
This article employs normative democratic theory to examine a counterintuitive suggestion raised repeatedly by empirical scholars of clientelism: namely, that practices of particularistic exchange might sometimes serve democratic ends. Certainly, most particularistic practices are widely agreed to undermine democracy, on both intrinsic and instrumental grounds. Yet some observers resist blanket condemnation—and in particular, many highlight the redeeming democratic virtues of certain practices that seem to facilitate collective organization among disempowered groups. Drawing on “realist” approaches to democratic theory, we vindicate this basic insight while clarifying its scope conditions. Countervailing power is a crucial democratic priority, and programmatic methods of promoting it sometimes face serious obstacles: as when clientelist practices are deeply entrenched, or incumbents capture the tools of programmatic competition. Under such conditions, we conclude, democracy-seeking leaders may justifiably employ particularistic practices to promote countervailing power—but only if they respect five constraints tethering them strictly to that goal.

The Idea of a Counter-Structure: Rethinking Collective Action against Climate Injustice

JONATHAN MASIN-PETERS

Full text
This article argues that addressing the structural injustices of climate change requires rethinking collective action. Disruptive and prefigurative politics are often treated as separate collective action repertoires, but I argue that a just energy transition requires that they work in tandem. To capture this interaction, I propose the idea of “counter-structural” collective action, in which disruptive protest creates windows of opportunity that prefigurative alternatives can then fill. Through analysis of two UK climate movements, Transition Towns (TTs) and Camps for Climate Action (CCA), I show how activists reconfigure structural elements to build new, carbon-free social and political forms. The theory of counter-structural action provides a framework for assessing the empirical cases. The cases, in turn, illustrate a broader conception of collective action that better reflects the social theory grounding structural injustice. Together, the theory and cases support the dual aims of climate justice: challenging fossil-fuel path dependence and advancing egalitarianism.

Teaching the past, informing the present? School history education and attitudes toward foreign countries

Soyoung Lee, Jeongmin Park

Full text
How does school history education emphasizing past grievances with other countries impact attitudes toward conflict and cooperation with the countries today? We examine this question using original data from 34 classrooms across 17 high schools in Seoul, South Korea. We first test whether exposure to anti‐Japanese content in school history education negatively impacts students' attitudes toward cooperation with Japan. We then compare how the attitudes of students educated with a more pro‐US textbook differ from those using a more anti‐US one. We find that school history education influenced what historical events students believed as “facts” and their evaluation of historical events. However, the effect on students' historical memories did not by itself automatically translate into corresponding attitudes toward present‐day Japan or the United States. Our findings offer various implications for studies on international rivalries and historical memories, as well as for debates on the political effects of school education.

Gender Bias in Bribe Solicitation: Evidence From Firm–State Interactions

Daniela Donno, Andreas Kern, Bernhard Reinsberg, Juliette Taylor

Full text
Previous research has shown that women-led businesses are less likely to bribe officials to bypass administrative burdens, assuming that this is because women are less susceptible to corruption. We uncover an additional mechanism that focuses on the beliefs and practices of corrupt officials: bureaucrats underestimate women as entrepreneurs and hence reduce their demand for bribes from women-led enterprises. We confirm these expectations using the World Bank Enterprise Survey on a sample of 109,929 firms in 149 countries from 2006 to 2022. Split-sample analyses focusing on gender-unequal societies further support our hypothesized mechanism and suggest tentatively that sexual corruption may substitute for bribery as female-led businesses interact with public sector officials. The results have important implications for understanding the drivers of corruption, notably its gendered dimension and the role of women’s leadership as an engine for integrity, though for perhaps unexpected reasons.

Perspectives on Politics

One Message, Multiple Functions: Local Propaganda as Bureaucratic Trace Management

Jianjun Yu

Full text
This article explores how local government propaganda in China can function as a tool for bureaucratic management. I argue that certain propaganda primarily serves as public documentation of government activities and officials’ performance, enhancing bureaucratic transparency and credibility. This documentation is particularly important when leaders lack personal ties with subordinates and thus require independent verification of their work. However, prioritizing record keeping over public persuasion often produces rigid, unengaging media content, which I term “trace-management propaganda.” Analyzing 1.4 million WeChat posts from Chinese local governments, I demonstrate that trace-management propaganda significantly increases during official evaluation periods and is especially prevalent where personal connections between leaders and subordinates are weak. A difference-in-differences analysis further confirms that a lack of personal ties increases reliance on trace-management propaganda. These findings reveal that local government propaganda in China frequently fulfills both administrative and expressive purposes, providing new insights into state media dynamics and bureaucratic oversight.

How Do Voters Reason about Intraparty Democracy? Constraints Rather than Influence

Ann-Kristin Kölln, Helene Helboe Pedersen

Full text
Widespread intraparty reforms empower party members and create coprincipalship with party voters. This alters the structure of democratic delegation and may lead to policy drift and ineffective accountability. Existing research is vague on how voters see this arrangement, and we therefore ask, What leads voters to accept coprincipalship despite its potential representational costs? We apply exploratory analysis to focus-group data to examine how voters reason about intraparty authority and show that participants accepted their role as coprincipals not for self-empowerment, but for parties’ organizational integrity: they viewed party members as effective constraints on self-serving leaders. Based on these findings, we formulate an intraparty constraint hypothesis stating that voters favor intraparty democracy as a constraint mechanism rather than a participatory venue, which provides a theoretical answer to the unsolved puzzle of why voters want intraparty democracy when they do not intend to use it or reward parties for having it.

Romania’s immigration debate under neoliberal hegemony: Constructing diligent migrants and individualising Transylvanian Hungarians as racist

Luis Escobedo, Tamás Kiss

Full text
In 2020, a group of residents of the rural Transylvanian Hungarian-majority municipality of Ditró (in Hungarian), or Ditrău (in Romanian), mobilised themselves against two Sri Lankan bakers. Mainstream media portrayed the incident as xenophobic and racist, the locals as right-wing extremists, and the Sri Lankans as merely ‘migrant workers’, turning the ‘Ditrău incident’ into Romania’s first and most important nationwide immigration debate. This article analyses what the media-coverage of these events tells us about the country’s approach to immigration, and how the symbols, signs, languages, meanings, knowledges, and memories articulated around the ‘Ditrău incident’ make this and other relatable events feed into this approach. We employ discourse analysis, including Lotman’s cultural semiotics, and engage with the institutional, political, and historical framing of ‘the incident’, as well as the broader socio-political and historical context of Romania’s immigration debate. In this way, we argue that, first, the ‘Ditrău incident’ was instrumental to Romania’s dominant societal core in portraying itself as closer to ‘the West’, its absolution of any responsibility for xenophobia and racism, the reproduction of the hegemony of neoliberalism and the middle-class ideology, and the construction of ‘diligent migrant workers’. Second, ‘the incident’ was fundamental in the silencing, individualisation, and invisibilisation of working-class voices and particularising Transylvanian Hungarians as racists and extremists. Finally, it allowed Romanian mainstream actors to brand their nation as open, tolerant, and humane, while normalising the commodification and overexploitation of third-country migrant labour.

Powered by (discrete) emotions? The consequences of affective polarization for self-censorship and outgroup silencing

Luna Laufer, Laura Gerstner, Pia Kinzler, Julia Stockwald, Anna-Luisa Sacher, Carsten Reinemann

Full text
Amid ongoing debates about political polarization in Western Democracies, this study investigates the role of affective polarization in the context of practices related to political discourse. In doing so, it moves beyond generalized affective polarization and delves into discrete negative emotions (fear, disgust, and anger), examining how they relate to the willingness to engage in self-censorship and the belief in the importance of silencing others, i.e., the respective political outgroup. Findings from a quota-based online survey conducted in autumn 2024 in Germany ( N = 1,918) reveal that the effects of affective polarization vary depending on the underlying emotion: fear of the political outgroup is linked to an increased willingness to self-censor. In contrast, anger is associated with a reduced willingness to self-censor, while all three negative emotions are connected to a higher belief in the importance to silence the political outgroup. These results underscore the critical role of discrete emotions in affective polarization, offering nuanced insights that extend beyond generalized dislike. In addition, they provide valuable insights into how affective polarization may threaten democratic values.

Questionnaire Democrats? Reconsidering the Relevance of Abstract Democracy Support in the American Public

Adam R. Panish, Ariel Malka

Full text
The American public largely favors “democracy,” yet many citizens tolerate undemocratic actions and candidates. This raises doubts about the relevance of abstract democracy support to actual democratic commitment. In contrast, we argue that abstract democracy support is a normatively significant dimension of attitude variation among Americans. Using nineteen surveys fielded between 1995 and 2024, we show that a sizable segment of the public expresses only tepid support for democracy, and that a nontrivial minority expresses opposition. Moreover, commonly used abstract democracy support items converge on a single latent construct which displays high rank-order stability, but also meaningful within-person variation, over a two-year period. Abstract democracy support negatively predicts openness to a wide range of electoral and liberal democratic violations and is associated with greater likelihood of electorally punishing anti-democratic candidates. In panel data, positive within-person deviations in abstract democracy support precede negative within-person deviations in openness to authoritarian governance, suggesting that individual-level shifts in abstract support predict later change in substantive democracy attitudes. Overall our findings suggest that the value of ‘democracy’ is contested among Americans, with potential implications for democratic erosion. We recommend that efforts to understand the American public’s role in democratic backsliding and restoration treat abstract support as a distinct attitude construct, measure it with multi-item scales, and explore whether or not it possesses causal significance.

How Policy Design Affects Perceptions of Beneficiaries and Support for Welfare

Lucia Lopez

Full text
Most of the public supports welfare policies in the abstract, but many hold deep misgivings about the deservingness of welfare recipients, driving down support for specific policies. Welfare policies are often designed to mitigate these concerns by restricting access to benefits, but it is unclear to what effect. According to an influential strain of research drawing on social construction theory, these restrictions may backfire, instead increasing concerns about a free-riding public. In contrast -- drawing on psychological research on the deservingness heuristic -- I argue that policy features, such as work requirements, signal effort on behalf of recipients and thus deservingness. Using an experiment, I find that several policy features increase perceptions of deservingness and, in turn, increase policy support and generosity. However, some policy features are also perceived as overly burdensome, reducing the effects on policy support. Treatment effects on perceptions of beneficiary deservingness are largest among ambivalent respondents, but largely generalize across partisanship, ideology, and racial resentment. Overall, these findings help clarify the implications of the deservingness heuristic while challenging predictions from social construction theory and informing ongoing debates over public opinion and policy feedback.

What Works Against Populist Rhetoric? The Effects of Counter-Arguments on Democratic Attitudes

Markus Kollberg, Julia Leschke

Full text
Politicians use populist rhetoric – arguments that portray politics as an enduring conflict between “the good people” and “the evil elites” – to generate support for attacks on constitutional safeguards, the media, and minorities. Yet, little is known about how to counteract such rhetoric. We utilize a pre-registered survey experiment in the UK (n $$\cong$$ 4000) to investigate the effects of counter-arguments put forward by fellow citizens. We hypothesize pluralist and democratic elitist counter-arguments to be promising antidotes to populist rhetoric and test to what extent such arguments can strengthen liberal democratic attitudes and weaken populist majoritarian ones. We find that populism can be challenged successfully but not with all types of counter-arguments. Pluralist appeals can decrease support for populist majoritarian democracy, while elitist counter-arguments have no effect on democratic attitudes. Additional analyses suggest that counter-arguments might also affect how voters evaluate candidates. These results contribute by demonstrating the potentials of counter-arguments in strengthening the resilience of liberal democracies vis-à-vis populist challenges.

Democratic but Still White: The Impact of Racial Identity Threats on White Democrats’ Support for Racial Justice Protests

Rachel Lienesch

Full text
In recent years, observers have noted an apparent increase in racially progressive attitudes among White Democrats. Among journalists, this “great awokening” of White Democrats is practically taken as a political fact. However, what this consensus about the racial liberalism of White Democrats often overlooks is that while White Democrats have in many ways moved in line with the Democratic Party’s position on race and racial inequality, their White identity makes them vulnerable to racial identity threats. In this study, I test this potential for racial identity-driven backlash among White Democrats in the context of racial justice protest movements. To do this, I conduct a survey experiment in which respondents are exposed to an image from a racial justice protest, and I vary the messaging on the signs in the image as well as the race of the protesters. I find that when protesters use messaging that is critical of Whites and Whiteness, White Democrats’ support for the movement and the protesters declines. Additionally, such messaging causes respondents to distance themselves from the views of the protesters and raises concerns about violence erupting at the protests. These results underscore how racial identity threats can erode White Democrats’ support for political messages and political movements that their increasingly liberal racial attitudes should make them amenable to.

Un-Priming Politics: A Method to Reduce Overreporting of Political Engagement

Elizabeth C. Connors, John V. Kane

Full text
Survey researchers frequently employ measures of political engagement, yet social desirability pressures can lead to overreporting. Building upon existing literature, we adopt an iterative approach to determine how such overreporting can be reduced. Four preregistered survey experiments were conducted (total N=7,122). We first confirm that social desirability considerations can underlie some types of self-reported political engagement. Second, having tested a variety of interventions that were designed to reduce overreporting, we find that our most effective treatment ( Dampen Politics) was one that aimed to decrease the salience of politics. This treatment significantly reduced (1) the degree to which respondents perceived the survey as political, and (2) some types of self-reported political engagement, particularly political interest and voting. We tested alternative implementations of the Dampen Politics treatment, each time assessing its robustness and validity. Our findings suggest a simple adjustment in survey wording and organization can meaningfully decrease overreporting of political engagement.

“We need the right kind of people in this neighborhood”: racial motivation in opposition to new housing construction

Clayton Becker

Full text
How we measure racial bias matters. Previous literature demonstrates that direct questioning leads to unreliable responses. Scholars have thus developed a number of techniques to uncover racial bias without cuing a defensive response. I investigate the performance of text- and image-based factorial experiments in the case of racial motivation for opposition to new housing development relative to a more traditional list experiment. My results demonstrate that these often-used methods yield substantially different conclusions about the importance of racial biases in opposition to new housing construction. While the factorial experiments suggest little evidence of racial bias, the more traditional list experiment reveals that the potential for racial change in their neighborhood is a concern for a meaningful share of respondents.

From identity to caring: A 44‐country examination of the role of global social identifications in concern for global issues

Marta Penczek, Katarzyna Hamer, Katarzyna Branowska, Zuzanna Zdanowicz, Laura Loy, David Sparkman, Maria Baran, Paul Bertin, Margarida Carmona, Theofilos Gkinopoulos, ... (+60 authors)

Full text
Previous research suggests that people who identify with global communities tend to show greater concern for global challenges. Using student samples from 44 countries across different cultural clusters ( N = 13,055), we examined whether (1) three forms of dispositional global social identification (GSI, with all humanity, citizens of the world, and people all over the world) are related to concern for global issues, (2) situational activation of these identifications increases such concern, (3) country‐level traditional and survival values are negatively associated with global concern, and (4) these values moderate the GSI–concern link. Both dispositional and situational GSIs positively predicted global concern. Situational activation showed inconsistent effects, whereas dispositional GSIs demonstrated moderate and consistent associations with global concern across the full sample and most countries. Overall, dispositional forms of GSI appeared more influential than situational ones. Contrary to expectations, traditional values (and survival values—marginally) were positively related to global concern and showed no evidence of moderating GSI–concern associations. Additional analyses showed the indices of GDP per capita, Human Development, Gini, and poverty as robust predictors of global concern. We discuss how these findings refine claims about the universality of global identification outcomes and highlight the importance of socio‐cultural context in shaping such concern.

Bad news for (young) gamers: Using gaming platforms as news sources is more intimately associated with radical belief dispositions than other online spaces

Simon Greipl, Ulrike Schwertberger, Diana Rieger, Brigitte Naderer

Full text
This study explores how digital news use among young Europeans links to radical belief dispositions, particularly within gaming platforms and messengers, where socio‐affective dynamics shape engagement with political information. We argue that online groups are an overlooked locus for biased information processing and the normalization of extremist worldviews. The conflation of an increasingly fragmented online news space with the inherently politicized gaming cosmos can relate to amplified problematic worldviews based on shared activity, identity, and reality. Based on a cross‐country survey ( N = 1489; eight European countries), we compare (the use of) gaming, messenger apps, social media, and traditional media for news in terms of how they afford problematic forms of collectivity, particularly in relation to social, cynical, and radical epistemologies. We find a robust correlation between news use and a set of radical belief dispositions, prominently with messenger apps, but even more so in gaming‐related online environments—less so on social media and none with traditional news use. Notably, a mediation analysis suggests that shared meaning‐making exhibits a negative rather than a positive indirect association with radical belief dispositions. We discuss the complex interplay of how individual factors and platform architectures connect with news engagement utilizing a vulnerability‐affordance framework.

How perceived economic inequality shapes political efficacy: The mediating role of institutional trust

Mar Montoya‐Lozano, Guillermo B. Willis, Juan Diego García‐Castro, Rosa Rodríguez‐Bailón

Full text
Economic inequality shapes citizens' attitudes and democratic support, yet little is known about how perceived inequality influences political efficacy. This research examines the relationship between perceived economic inequality and political efficacy, testing institutional trust as a psychosocial mechanism underlying this link. Across two correlational and two experimental studies, we present converging evidence for this process. Study 1 ( N = 9985) revealed multilevel evidence of a negative association between perceived economic inequality and institutional trust, beyond the effect of objective inequality, using Latin American survey data. Study 2 ( N = 1536) replicated this finding with a Spanish quota sample, revealing an indirect effect of perceived inequality on political efficacy through institutional trust. Study 3 ( N = 168) involved the experimental manipulation of inequality. Participants in the high‐inequality condition reported lower institutional trust and political efficacy, and the indirect effect was replicated. Study 4 ( N = 638) employed a concurrent double‐randomization design to manipulate perceived inequality and institutional trust. In contexts of high institutional trust, political efficacy differed between highly and less unequal conditions, with lower political efficacy observed in the high‐inequality condition. Similar patterns emerged for collective action. Overall, perceived inequality undermines institutional trust, reducing political efficacy and democratic engagement.

Conceptualizing and measuring global consciousness

James H. Liu, Robert Jiqi Zhang, Jacky C. K. Ng, Sylvia Xiaohua Chen

Full text
To examine the conceptualization and the measurement of global consciousness (GC) in cross‐national contexts, we conducted a secondary analysis on survey data collected from 16,241 individuals across 35 societies. Results demonstrate that GC is best conceptualized as a multidimensional construct by a bifactor ESEM model, in which GC captures an overarching and direct source of common variance across all manifest indicators, whereas domain‐specific residual variance is retained by three existing measures: Identification with All Humanity (IWAH), Cosmopolitan Orientation (COS), and a shortened version of Global Orientations (GOS). Metric invariance was successfully achieved across 30 out of 35 societies. At the individual level, GC was negatively associated with political conservatism, an effect that was amplified in societies with higher HDI, more robust rule of law, and a salient culture of individualism. In an exploratory societal‐level analysis, aggregated GC functioned as an informative ecological indicator, appearing more prominent in the Global South societies across the sample. This preliminary pattern is probably attributable to the open system where GC is measured, reflecting the hopes and aspirations of people in the making.

Seniority Amidst Change: Parliamentary Turnover and Persistence in 14 European Democracies

Torill Stavenes, Ragnhild Louise Muriaas

Full text
This article provides a multi-dimensional perspective on parliamentary composition in an era of political turbulence. It extends existing turnover models by proposing a three-dimensional framework to understand persistence which includes the structure of the ballot, electoral volatility and institutional career opportunities. Using novel comparative data from 14 European democracies (1965–2021), where we distinguish between newcomers, seniors (3+ terms) and super-seniors (5+ terms), we find that persistence is most likely in politico-institutional contexts characterised by candidate control over ballot access, low electoral volatility and unicameral legislatures. Super-seniors seem to occupy an advantaged position, as prior seniority levels strongly shape subsequent ones, and they are also less vulnerable to electoral shocks. At the same time, we descriptively show that none of the countries in our sample provide the full configuration conducive to persistence, suggesting that contemporary democracies contain institutional arrangements that promote circulation within the legislative elite and limit concentration.

The relationship between EU fiscal transfers and the European policy profile of political parties

Johannes Lattmann, Marc Debus

Full text
This research note examines how fiscal transfers within the European Union shape the programmatic strategies of domestic political parties. We argue that a country’s net-contributor or net-receiving status conditions the strategies how parties articulate their positions on European integration. In net-receiving states, Euroskeptic parties strategically mute EU-related rhetoric to avoid undermining their broader policy stance. Moreover, the increase in pro-EU positions among parties in net-receiving states is driven mainly by support for further EU-level redistribution. We evaluate these claims using Chapel Hill Expert Survey data from 2002–2024 and a regression discontinuity design that uses continuous measures of net contributions to the EU budget. The empirical analyses, in which we also test for a linear relationship, align with our expectations. Euroskeptic parties mention the EU less in net-receiving contexts while beneficiary status solely shifts party positions toward pro-redistribution preferences at the EU level. These results illustrate how Euroskeptic parties cope with the potential positive impacts of the EU.

Wait for It: Why Authoritarians Reshuffle Ruling Coalitions After Elections

Ilia Nadporozhskii

Full text
This article asks whether elections, even if they are not free and fair, might still have important consequences for authoritarian ruling coalitions, testing the theory that dictators avoid destabilizing their coalition before an election, but can afford to do this once the vote has passed. Disregarding the competitiveness and integrity of elections, the post-electoral period may serve as a face-saving time for resignations and as an opportunity for promotions or other cabinet changes, which autocrats can leverage to manage elites. Examining panel data on authoritarian regimes that held presidential elections in which the incumbent retained office between 1967 and 2023, I find that cabinet turnover is significantly higher in the period following elections. By exploiting the quasi-exogenous timing of scheduled elections and multiple robustness checks, the analysis shows that elections trigger elite reshuffling rather than merely coinciding with it.

Explaining Military Defections in Democracies: The Impact of Executive Self-Coups

Kara Kingma Neu

Full text
Militaries sometimes defect during protests, including in democracies. The scholarship on defections in nondemocracies highlights militaries’ material interests, societal roles and socialties, and organizational divisions. In this article, I assess those factors for militaries in democracies while incorporating another consideration: civilian control and specifically the principle that militaries should obey the elected government’s orders. I argue that a crucial condition for defections in democracies is therefore executive self-coups. When protests are prompted by an executive’s attempts to decisively and illegally grab power, the military may see defecting as both necessary and justifiable. I find support for the importance of executive self-coups, combined with other conditions, using Qualitative Comparative Analysis of protests from 1991 to 2019 and case studies of Bolivia in 2019 and Ecuador in 2015.

Debate and Legislative Productivity in US State Legislatures

Nicholas O. Howard, Tessa Provins

Full text
Scholarship holds that procedural rules shape legislative proceedings, but most existing evidence comes from the US Congress' limited procedural variation. This article develops a theoretical framework for how different forms of debate restriction—both limits on member participation and procedural hurdles to closing debate—affect legislative productivity, and how these effects depend on chambers' institutional capacity. Using an original dataset of debate rules and bill progression spanning 2011–2018 from all 99 US state legislative chambers, we show that restrictions on member speaking opportunities and ending debate are associated with higher legislative productivity, with the relationship operating through distinct mechanisms. Procedural hurdles to ending debate are associated with higher productivity in low‐professionalism legislatures, while these associations diminish or reverse in more professionalized chambers. These findings demonstrate the conditional nature of debate rules and institutional capacity, offering new insight into procedural designs and the tradeoff between deliberation and efficiency in lawmaking.

Party and Prejudice: Are Asian American Candidates Better Suited to Win in White Districts?

Eric Gonzalez Juenke, Paru Shah, Bernard L. Fraga

Full text
Recent work on Asian American descriptive representation has found that Asian American officeholders are more prevalent in racially diverse districts when compared to Black or Latino representatives, but still face substantial obstacles in very white electoral districts. This work, however, models Asian American representation by observing officeholders only, ignoring both the competitive election environment and Asian American losing candidates, biasing its inferences. We reassess these conclusions using a candidate‐centered approach in thousands of state legislative races in 2018 and 2020. Our results demonstrate that Asian American, Black, and Latino candidates all perform substantially better in white districts than officeholder models predict, and that any differences between minority groups in white districts are due to the partisan match between candidates and their districts. The findings speak to the need for researchers to model vote choice as a candidate‐centered process, paying careful attention to the actual choices on voters' election day ballots.

Soil Degradation in Sub-Saharan Agriculture

Julia Berazneva, David Güereña, Annemie Maertens, Hope C. Michelson

Full text
Soil degradation poses a critical challenge to food security, poverty reduction, and sustainable development in Sub-Saharan Africa. In this review, we synthesize economic perspectives on soil degradation in Sub-Saharan Africa. We draw on concepts, measures, and analysis from the biophysical sciences to define soil degradation and present evidence of its extent. We review the drivers of soil degradation and its economic consequences and indicate possible approaches toward remediation. We highlight opportunities for interdisciplinary collaboration to jointly address soil health and rural welfare.

Robert S. Pindyck: Real Options, Optimal Control, and the Pedagogical Legacy of Uncertainty

Gordon Rausser, David Zilberman

Full text
Robert S. Pindyck, who is the Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi Professor in Finance and Economics as well as a professor of applied economics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), has shaped how economists and policymakers analyze investment, energy markets, and climate policy under uncertainty. Beginning with early work in dynamic optimization, he helped migrate option-pricing logic from finance into the economics of “real” decisions (such as entry, exit, and capacity choice) via the real options program developed with Saman Majd and Avinash Dixit. In energy and natural resources, Pindyck's models and empirics clarified interfuel substitution, storage and inventory dynamics, and cartel behavior. In climate economics, he became a prominent critic of the overprecision of integrated assessment models (IAMs), proposing decision frameworks that emphasize deep uncertainty and tail risk while arguing for robust carbon pricing. Equally important is his pedagogical legacy: with Daniel Rubinfeld, Pindyck coauthored two widely used textbooks ( Microeconomics and Econometric Models and Economic Forecasts ) that trained generations in rigorous modeling, identification, forecasting, and market analysis. This article synthesizes his career and research arc, contrasts the Dixit–Pindyck real options approach with the Fisher–Arrow–Henry tradition of quasi-option value, reviews his energy and climate policy contributions, and highlights his influence as a teacher and econometric innovator.

China's Climate Policies: Climate Targets and Implementation Mechanisms

Yazhen Gong, Min Wang, Jinhua Zhao

Full text
China leads the world in both greenhouse gas emissions and renewable energy deployment. In recent years, China has committed to increasingly ambitious climate goals and is on track to meeting these goals. This article provides a systematic review of China's climate policies and their impacts. We argue that China's climate policies can be classified into two components: binding climate-related targets and a mixture of command-and-control and market-based implementation mechanisms. We discuss how the mechanisms have performed and the lessons they offer for the future climate policies of China and other countries.

Current State of Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage Technologies

Yanyou Guan, Gordon Rausser, David Zilberman

Full text
Addressing climate change requires significant efforts toward decarbonization. This review provides a comprehensive technical, economic, and policy assessment of 239 carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies. Categorizing pathways by application and physicochemical processes, we evaluate technological maturity using Technology Readiness Levels and economic viability via capture costs and the impact of capture on product price. Findings indicate that CCUS technologies are heterogeneous; most currently remain immature and face prohibitive costs, trapping private capital in a wait-and-see dynamic driven by severe risk premiums and externalities. To overcome these market failures, we advocate for a hybrid public-private framework: utilizing early-stage public funding to derisk development and catalyze learning-by-doing, subsequently transitioning to robust carbon pricing mechanisms. Furthermore, we highlight the critical role of international coordination in resolving systemic challenges associated with global externalities, spatial heterogeneity, and the fragmentation of markets, supply chains, and regulatory regimes.

Global Environmental Politics

Public Opinion and the Political Logic of International Climate Pledges

Michaël Aklin, Mark T. Buntaine, Matto Mildenberger

Full text
The 2015 Paris Agreement shifted the focus of global climate bargaining from collective, binding emissions reductions to regular, public, and unilateral climate pledges. These pledges reveal new information to the public about a government’s intended policies, bargaining approach, and responses to counterpart offers. We conducted three experiments across ten high-emitting countries with more than 10,000 respondents to examine whether the public becomes more supportive of climate action when observing conditional or unconditional public pledges. Domestic audiences prefer ambitious unilateral pledges over inaction. However, at high ambition levels, they favor conditional pledges emphasizing fairness between countries. Conditional pledges by counterpart countries do not increase support for domestic climate action, and they increase support for international transfers to low- and middle-income countries only when accompanied by ambitious unconditional pledges. Overall, conditional public pledging is insufficient to catalyze international cooperation by acting on public opinion.

Don’t Fly Me to the Moon: A Moratorium on Lunar Industrialization

Xiao-Shan Yap, Florian Rabitz

Full text
A complex mix of geopolitical and commercial interests is accelerating plans for large-scale infrastructure development and resource extraction on the Moon. While the industrialization of the Moon may provide numerous economic benefits and facilitate further exploration of the solar system, it will likely cause adverse, irreversible impacts on the lunar environment and harmful knock-on effects on scientific research and cultural values. We suggest that there is a strong rationale and ample legal precedent for imposing a moratorium on lunar industrialization. As in other issue areas, a temporary prohibition could provide room for the development of robust international regulation, but also for broad and inclusive deliberation on the very desirability of lunar industrialization. The pace with which operational planning for lunar industrialization progresses suggests that there is an urgent need for governance interventions to safeguard the space environment and associated human values.

Gateway Clubs: How ESG Indices Reinforce Transnational Voluntary Environmental Initiatives

Christian Elliott, Inhwan Ko

Full text
Many initiatives marshaling voluntary commitments to corporate sustainability have grown enormously through the inducement of reputational benefits and the partnership of NGOs and international organizations. Yet, as participants increasingly include less-than-ambitious companies, growth can nonetheless accelerate, even as it undermines the reputational distinction that membership originally offered. Why do initiatives then continue to draw new members? In this article, we advance a novel explanation: transnational environmental governance can become “embedded” in a parallel economic or political context such that joining “gateway clubs” becomes a means of ensuring access to external benefits. We demonstrate our argument with the case of the United Nations Global Compact (UNGC), where alignment with UNGC principles became a key criterion for inclusion in the S&P 500 ESG Index in January 2019. Using a difference-in-differences approach, our results suggest that US-domiciled companies eligible for S&P indexation were more likely to join and remain in the UNGC after January 2019.

Who Shapes Air Pollution Governance? How Economic Development, Democracy, and Data Condition Stakeholder Influence in Six Majority World Cities

Ella Henninger, E. Keith Smith, Thomas Bernauer

Full text
Air pollution is a major global health hazard, with the worst burden falling on cities in the Majority World. Yet which conditions shape governments’ willingness to act on air pollution remains poorly understood. Drawing on sixty-three semi structured interviews with seventy-two air pollution experts and stakeholders in six highly polluted cities (Accra, Kampala, Johannesburg, Jakarta, Dhaka, and Delhi), we examine how economic development, democracy, and data availability structure the influence of citizens, local advocates, economic elites, and country-external agencies on governments’ mitigation efforts. We find that governments remain primarily responsive to economic elites across levels of economic development, while external actors play a stronger role in the lowest-income case, Dhaka. Democracy strengthens citizens and local advocates through electoral accountability and civil society mobilization. Data availability creates opportunities for influence but also provokes governmental counter strategies. These findings highlight the centrality of stakeholder power in clean-air governance, showing how political, economic, and informational constraints shape whose demands governments respond to.

How Information About Disaster Vulnerability Shapes Climate Policy Preferences

Talbot M. Andrews, Richard Clark, Rebecca Perlman

Full text
Climate change is significantly increasing the frequency and severity of extreme weather events. Prior research indicates that exposure to such events can make individuals more supportive of green policies. But we know less about how this shapes individuals’ priorities across policies. Given that governments are budget constrained, policymakers may confront a trade-off between climate policy goals. We conducted original survey experiments in the United States to understand how information about climate vulnerability shapes preferences for adaptation and mitigation, particularly when voters are forced to choose between the two. We find that when individuals with weak priors are told about their vulnerability to extreme weather events, they prioritize local adaptation relative to mitigation, while those with stronger priors remain largely unmoved. Effects are especially consistent among independents, a crucial voting bloc as climate change becomes polarized. These findings underscore a potential challenge of mobilizing support for mitigation in cases in which mitigation and adaptation compete for funding.

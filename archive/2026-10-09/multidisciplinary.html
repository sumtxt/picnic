The WorkReach model for urban work location choices through economic complexity and informality

Ollin D. Langle-Chimal, Steffen Knoblauch, Marta C. González

Full text
Human mobility in urban areas largely depends on the spatial distribution of jobs and who pursues them, yet how workers balance proximity against economic opportunity remains poorly understood. Existing mechanistic models capture physically interpretable effects such as distance decay, but overlook how socioeconomic factors shape commuting decisions. We present WorkReach, a model grounded in discrete choice theory that embeds informality and economic complexity into a utility function and represents commuting as the outcome of workers behaving as if maximizing it. Applied to four cities in the United States, Mexico, and Brazil, it reproduces commuting flows as accurately as widely used benchmarks while making the underlying choices interpretable. Here, we show that workers consistently commute farther to economically sophisticated areas, while the role of informality differs across regions, and that measuring accessibility by the perceived benefit of opportunities, not just physical proximity, highlights disparities conventional metrics overlook.

In this paper, we simulate networked systems involving thousands of LLM agents across different backbone models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, ChatGLM, Llama-3, and DeepSeek-V3), in which agents interact through LLM-guided conversations and update their opinions over time, resulting in the emergence of opinion polarization. Psychological and computational evidence that merit is a constructed evaluative process

Sarah Vahed, Alan G. Sanfey

Full text
Merit is a foundational moral principle invoked to justify inequality between people, yet an individual’s productivity is shaped not only by their efforts but also their opportunities. How, then, do people construct merit judgements? Across four preregistered, incentivized studies with US-based samples (total N = 759), we provide psychological and computational evidence that merit is not the product of a fixed rule, but rather the outcome of a subjective valuation. In a two-stage paradigm, participants first completed a real-effort tournament that dissociated performance (effort) from structural productivity (output dependent on opportunity). Thereafter, groups of observers and stakeholders allocated resources to tournament players. Across studies, a player’s performance and structural productivity influenced how people judged deservingness. Computational modeling revealed that decision-makers integrated these dimensions of merit into a value computation that shaped allocations. Crucially, individuals differed in their latent weighting patterns, yielding subgroups with directly opposing conceptions of deservingness. By revealing merit as a nonuniform ideal, these findings provide a mechanistic account of how deservingness is determined, helping explain the compelling yet contested nature of meritocracy.

Children from six cultures reliably help their peers—but in different ways

Henriette Zeidler, Marie Schäfer, Esther Herrmann, Patricia Kanngiesser, Luke Maurits, Daniel Haun, Michael Tomasello

Full text
Helping others is an integral part of human sociality, but systematic cross-cultural comparisons of children’s helping behavior are rare. Existing work has mainly focused on interactions between adults and children, paying less attention to peer dynamics—which are more relevant in many cultural settings. Here, we report a study of 4- to 9-y-old children’s (N = 336) spontaneous helping in 6 highly diverse populations in Latin America, Africa, and Europe (Wichí, Quechua, ≠Akhoe Hai||om, Samburu, Kikuyu, and Germans). In all settings, we observed pairs of same-aged children in an experimental task in which one partner could assist the other by providing either instrumental help or information. Across cultures, children reliably helped their peers by offering either type of help, with the provision of information increasing over age. We found striking commonalities in peer helping in all settings, with only small culture-specific patterns in children’s responses. This suggests that helping among children is a universal human behavior which is subtly shaped by local norms and practices.

Carneiro’s global dataset of cultural traits in ethnographic and historical societies: a digital edition

Václav Hrnčíř, Robert Forkel, Russell D. Gray

Full text
The late Robert L. Carneiro was a prominent anthropologist who devoted his life to the study of political evolution and the evolution of complex societies. In the 1960s and 1970s, he and his colleagues created a cross-cultural dataset for their Scale Analysis project, collecting ethnographic and historical data on hundreds of cultural traits in more than a hundred societies worldwide. Although Carneiro presented the results of the project in several papers, the dataset itself has not yet been published. Here we present a digital version of two editions of the dataset, based on Carneiro’s documentation stored in the archives of the American Museum of Natural History. The 4th edition contains 127 societies and 354 cultural traits, and the 6th edition features 74 societies and 618 traits. Both represent unique sets of coded cross-cultural information that can be used as a comparative source to other cross-cultural datasets, as a starting point for one’s own collection of cross-cultural data, and for quantitative testing of cultural evolutionary hypotheses.

The inequality of voice: how belonging to lower social status groups impacts worker voice

Arjuna Snoep-Delleman, Agnes Akkerman, Katerina Manevska

Full text
We draw on relational inequality theory (RIT) and status beliefs research to examine how social status shapes worker voice and supervisor responses to work-related issues. We investigate whether women, non-Western migrants, and lower-educated workers are less likely than men, native-born, and higher-educated workers to engage in claims-making by voicing concerns to their supervisor. Additionally, we examine whether supervisors are more likely to reject claims made by workers from lower-status groups. Using survey data (N = 3,215) from the Dutch labor market, we analyze status differences in worker voice behavior and perceived supervisor responses across workplaces. We find that workers with a non-Western migrant background or lower education are more likely to remain silent when experiencing a workplace issue, whereas gender differences in worker voice are explained by working hours. We find no evidence that lower-status workers report more suppression of their voice.

Living off assets? European household incomes in the ‘asset economy’

Miguel García-Duch, Lorenzo Vidal

Full text
This study examines whether the expansion of asset ownership has given rise to ‘societies of petit rentiers’ shaped by generational differences in asset acquisition opportunities. Using the ECB Household Finance and Consumption Survey (2011–2021) covering up to 23 EU countries, we identify ‘petit-rentier’ households that derive at least 10% (and 20%) of total income from the ownership of rent-bearing assets. Together with descriptive statistics, we combine machine-learning feature selection with multilevel modelling to determine the main predictors of petit-rentier status. Our findings point to the continued dominance of traditional axes of inequality—class position and family wealth—albeit with an increasingly central role for real estate. Petit-rentier prevalence declines in most countries and asset-based income shifts towards real-estate rents. Non-worker households and multiple-property owners, rather than age cohort, show the strongest positive associations with petit-rentier status. The ‘asset economy’ appears to consolidate trajectories of income concentration rather than democratisation.

Rising market power and its political discontents

Tommaso Crescioli, Toon Van Overbeke

Full text
How does market power affect the political behavior of small business owners? As the growing market power of large multinational firms has fundamentally changed the opportunity structure for small businesses and their beneficiaries, we try to understand how those economic developments influence European democracies. We argue that growing market power lowers small business owners’ trust in key democratic institutions and the functioning of markets, driving them to reject incumbents and shift their support to radical right parties. We employ firm-level, individual-level, and regional-level data on fourteen European countries to test this argument. Our findings indicate that rising market power, measured by combining mergers and increases in market concentration, is associated with the declining economic performance of micro-firms. In turn, mergers and the declining economic share of these firms are robust predictors of anti-incumbent and increased radical right voting both at the individual and constituency levels. This article provides the first analysis of the effect of market concentration on rich democracies and, in doing so, offers a novel explanation for the surprising radical right support among small business owners in Europe.

Peer-to-peer pressure? Social network-based opposition to cryptocurrency regulation

Anton Brännlund

Full text
This study examines whether peer influence shapes public attitudes toward cryptocurrency regulation among individuals without direct market exposure. While previous research has shown that participation in financial markets can foster regulatory skepticism, it remains unclear whether similar attitudinal shifts may emerge indirectly through interpersonal interaction. Drawing on three waves of panel survey data from Finland (2023–2025), the analysis investigates whether social proximity to cryptocurrency investors affects regulatory preferences among non-owners. Peer exposure is operationalized using three distinct components: the number of cryptocurrency-investing peers (passive influence), the frequency of cryptocurrency-related discussion (active influence), and perceived peer influence (receptiveness). Across dynamic ordered logit specifications incorporating lagged dependent variables and asset ownership fixed effects, the results indicate that discussion frequency is consistently associated with greater opposition to regulatory oversight. In contrast, passive exposure and self-reported receptiveness are not systematically related to regulatory attitudes once active interaction is accounted for.

The role of collective ownership beliefs in shaping welfare chauvinism: experimental evidence from Türkiye’s dual welfare funding landscape

Duygu Merve Uysal

Full text
Starting from the premise that public perceptions are endogenous to policy structures, this research examines how the interplay between policy funding schemes and identity differences affects public support for immigrant access to welfare assistance. It argues that collective proprietary claims over national resources are a key factor underpinning welfare chauvinism as natives seek to protect their exclusive entitlements within the national system. Leveraging Türkiye’s dual policy funding landscape, where foreign-aid-funded programs coexist with traditional welfare models, a population-representative survey experiment was conducted, differentiating between claimant identities and welfare assistance funding structures. The results show that the native–immigrant support gap emerges only when assistance is funded domestically but not through foreign aid. Moreover, solidarity for immigrants does not significantly increase when funding is switched from the state to foreign finance unless there are dire needs. The results carry implications for studies on welfare chauvinism and transnational migration governance.

The politics of categorizing people of african descent - global frameworks, political meanings, and shifting boundaries of belonging

Nicole Sommer

Full text
The article examines how the United Nations (UN) politically creates and structures the category “people of African descent” (PAD). It shows that, in international political debates, this category is closely linked to migration-related categories but not necessarily solely through overlaps, but also through processes of differentiation that further complicate the categorization process. Through a qualitative analysis this article examines the relationship between the category of ‘migrant’ and the ‘people of African descent’. It shows that the two are closely linked: Migration functions both as an internal mechanism of differentiation and as a demarcation from other migrant groups. At the same time, 'migrant itself is not a uniform category, but is constantly redefined in conjunction with race, legal status, gender and generation. This creates a complex political grammar in which the intertwining of people of African descent and migration marks specific forms of discrimination, vulnerability and visibility in the UN discourse.

Uninstitutionalized regional integration: a comparative analysis of migration governance and migrant integration in the Organization of Turkic States

Barış Demirel, Fatma Rümeysa Taşbaş, Ömer Faruk Cantekin

Full text
This study comparatively examines the level and patterns of institutionalization of migrant integration in the member countries of the Organization of Turkic States (OTS). Focusing on Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Uzbekistan, and Türkiye, the study investigates how integration is positioned across different policy areas and discusses the governance dynamics that may explain the observed institutionalization patterns. The study is based on directed content analysis of national legislation and policy documents that were in force or constituted the relevant policy framework during the 2010–2026 study period, supplemented by OTS and international organization documents used for contextual analysis. The eight policy areas of MIPEX were used as the analytical reference framework, and the intensity of integration institutionalization was comparatively assessed through a qualitative evaluation framework based on normative recognition, administrative mechanisms, and rights-based scope. The findings show that integration policies are relatively more visible, particularly in the area of labor market access; however, institutional intensity remains lower in areas such as political participation, long-term residency, and combating discrimination. This suggests that integration is positioned not as an autonomous multidimensional membership regime, but rather as a complementary element of regulatory and economically focused migration management. The study interprets this pattern in relation to a governance logic formed by the intersection of the assumption of transience, security-based governance, and economic instrumentalization, and proposes the concept of “uninstitutionalized regional integration” as an original contribution to the migration governance literature. The findings suggest that the institutionalization of integration is shaped not only by migration intensity or administrative capacity, but also by governance rationalities that define migrants’ position within the political and social order.

Crisis, segmentation, and immigrant labor market incorporation: COVID-19 in the U.S. restaurant industry

Ernesto F. L. Amaral, Huyen Pham, Raymond Robertson, Suojin Wang, Nereyda Y. Ortiz Osejo de Lemus

Full text
The COVID-19 pandemic generated an unprecedented labor market shock that disproportionately affected face-to-face service sectors central to immigrant employment. This study examines whether the pandemic reaffirmed or revealed limits to the narrative of immigrant resilience that emerged from research on the Great Recession. Focusing on the U.S. restaurant industry (a large, low-wage, nontraded sector with high immigrant representation), we analyze differential employment trajectories of foreign-born and U.S.-born workers across contraction and recovery phases. We integrate three data sources: 2000–2022 CPS Annual Social and Economic Supplement data to establish long-term occupational trends; longitudinal CPS Basic Monthly data to estimate worker transitions during the pandemic; and an original nationwide survey of restaurant owners and hiring managers conducted in Summer 2021 with retrospective staffing information for January 2020 and January 2021. CPS transition results indicate that foreign-born workers experienced sharper transitions into unemployment in certain occupational segments during the contraction phase. Employer survey results suggest that foreign-born workers became increasingly concentrated in lower-wage restaurant types during recovery. These findings suggest that the national scope and mobility-restricting nature of COVID-19 constrained traditional immigrant adjustment mechanisms. Rather than uniformly reinforcing immigrant resilience, the pandemic intensified intra-industry segmentation and revealed how crisis characteristics condition immigrant labor market incorporation.

The Role of Sex Ratios in Refugee Inflows and Gendered Host Attitudes: A Vignette Survey Experiment in a German State

Chia-Jung Tsai, Robert G. Rinderknecht, John R.B. Palmer, Emilio Zagheni

Full text
This study investigates how the sex ratio of refugee populations can shape attitudes toward refugee inflows in a German state. We use an online vignette survey experiment to examine respondents’ nonacceptance of refugee arrivals and four types of perceived threats: job competition, mate competition, threats to German culture, and safety. Respondents are recruited via Meta targeted online advertisement in the German state of Mecklenburg–Western Pomerania. Our results suggest that nonacceptance of a refugee group increases as its male-to-female sex ratio rises. This relationship is particularly pronounced for perceived threats to German culture and safety, when the refugee group is non-white. Respondents’ gender also shapes both overall acceptance and the specific threats that respondents perceive. Female respondents showed more tolerant attitudes than male respondents toward refugee groups with lower male-to-female sex ratios. Female respondents also perceived greater threats to culture and mate competition in the neighborhood from non-white male refugees. Male respondents perceived greater threats from job competition. Both groups showed heightened perceived threats to safety. Overall, our findings show that the demographic composition of refugee inflows interacts with respondent gender to produce distinct patterns of acceptance and threat perception, shedding light on the mechanisms underlying negative attitudes toward refugee arrivals in Germany.

The Circuitous Routes to Permanence: Understanding International Students’ Permit Transition Pathways to Permanent Residency in Canada

Jonathan Anim Amoyaw, Yoko Yoshida, Michael Haan, George Botchey

Full text
As countries adopt a “two-step” immigrant selection process, understanding how temporary residents granted study or work permit(s) transition to permanent residency (PR) is crucial. Using linked administrative records and sequence and cluster analysis, we identify five permit transition pathways to PR among international students that vary by timing, duration, and permit sequence. Our multinomial logistic regression analysis highlights pathway differences across study level, arrival cohort, and province. Students pursuing a master's degree or non-university diploma were more likely to obtain off-campus work permit(s) during studies and a post-study work permit before PR. Post-2008 cohorts were also more likely to follow this pathway. Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Quebec stood out for transitions to PR without a post-study work permit. This study reveals complex, non-linear transitions to PR, emphasizing the role of evolving immigration policies in shaping diverse pathways. Insights can inform discussions on temporary status transitions and differential access to PR.

Settled or Unsettled? Linking Migration Trajectories From Adolescence to Young Adulthood and Their Social Correlates in the United Kingdom

Xiuxiang Pan, Tim F. Liao

Full text
Early‐life migration can shape subsequent migration, but how migration patterns in young adulthood resemble or differ from those in adolescence remains unclear. This study examines cross‐phase migration trajectory linkedness between adolescence (ages 13–17) and young adulthood (ages 21–25), and the social conditions under which such linkedness takes different forms. Using the British Household Panel Survey and its successor, Understanding Society ( N = 478), we construct annual migration sequences incorporating frequency, timing and distance, and quantify their resemblance using dyadic sequence analysis. The analysis identifies both resemblance and contrast in cross‐phase linkedness between adolescent and young‐adult migration. Resemblance is dominated by sustained immobility across both life phases, whereas contrast reflects divergence between the two phases, most often from immobility in adolescence to mobility in young adulthood. Regression analyses suggest that early marriage is associated with a lower likelihood of sustained immobility across both phases, whereas adolescent residence in London–South East is associated with a higher likelihood of this pattern. Being female and living in owner‐occupied housing during adolescence are both associated with the pattern of an immobile adolescence followed by a mobile young adulthood. The main findings are broadly robust to alternative classification schemes. Together, these trajectory‐level patterns caution against assuming that changes in mobility at one life stage translate uniformly into longer‐term internal migration trends.

Anticipating Denied Futures: Temporalities of Protracted Refuge Among Congolese in Kampala

Alessandro Gusman

Full text
This article develops a temporal perspective on protracted displacement by analysing the experiences of Congolese refugees in Kampala. It argues that displacement is both a spatial and a temporal condition, in which asylum and resettlement regimes are experienced as dispossessing refugees of their capacity to orient themselves toward the future. Drawing on longitudinal ethnographic research (2013–2025), the study traces the shift from initial expectations of rapid resettlement—imagined as a linear trajectory from Congo to Uganda to the Global North—to what many describe as a “life without a future.” This form of temporal dispossession shows how humanitarian and state regimes are experienced as governing not only mobility, but also refugees' capacity to imagine and inhabit the future. The article makes two main contributions to debates in migration and displacement studies. First, it contributes to the temporal turn in migration studies by distinguishing denied futurity from “waiting”: the collapse of future imaginaries reveals a condition in which waiting itself becomes difficult to sustain. Second, it highlights the ambivalent role of Pentecostal and Charismatic Christianity as a key arena of temporal reorientation. Religious narratives of “God's plan” reinstate teleology and sustain traces of futurity, while also functioning as vernacular critiques of humanitarian failure; yet they may simultaneously reproduce forms of deferred hope. Ultimately, the article demonstrates that time is a key terrain of governance, dispossession, and contestation in protracted displacement. By foregrounding the ambivalent role of faith, it shows how Congolese refugees negotiate temporal agency when institutional pathways have foreclosed the future.

Escalators, not Equalisers: Core‐City Premiums in Intergenerational Social Mobility in China

Yiyue Huangfu, Haiyan Hao, Hongli Liang

Full text
Do large cities promote intergenerational mobility? Existing evidence comes almost exclusively from developed societies, where urbanisation largely preceded metropolitan concentration. In rapidly developing contexts, these two processes unfold together, making it unclear whether large cities confer mobility advantages beyond urbanisation itself. This study disentangles these forces using pooled data from the 2014, 2016 and 2018 China Labour‐force Dynamics Survey. Comparing intergenerational occupational mobility across rural areas, non‐core cities, and core cities, defined as municipalities, provincial capitals, and sub‐provincial cities, we find a metropolitan mobility premium in absolute terms. In China, core‐city residence is associated with substantially higher absolute mobility (an escalator effect) but does not weaken intergenerational persistence (no equaliser effect). This escalator premium is evident among rural‐origin migrants, the in‐situ urbanised, and urban locals, is smaller and not statistically significant among urban‐origin migrants, and is largely attenuated after accounting for educational attainment and hukou incorporation. These findings caution against equating rising mobility with expanding equality of opportunity. In developing contexts, large cities may accelerate structural upgrading while leaving the intergenerational transmission of advantage intact.

Supporting Without Steering: The Role of International Actors in Colombia's Policy on Venezuelan Displacement

Nieves Fernández‐Rodríguez, María Gabriela Trompetero‐Vicent

Full text
Research on the role of international organisations (IOs) and donors in migration governance has largely focused on low‐income countries, while Latin America remains underrepresented despite the importance of its middle‐income states in responding to large‐scale displacement. This article examines Colombia's Temporary Protection Statute for Venezuelan Migrants (TPS), introduced in 2021 to grant Venezuelan nationals legal status, rights, and a pathway to permanent residency. Drawing on 65 interviews with policymakers and representatives of IOs and aid agencies, as well as policy documents, it analyses how Colombian authorities, IOs, and donors jointly shaped the policy. The findings show that international actors contributed through financial assistance, ideational framing, knowledge provision, and capacity‐building. Yet Colombian authorities retained policy ownership, selectively adapting and mobilising international resources to advance domestically defined objectives. The article demonstrates how extensive international involvement and domestic ownership can coexist, challenging portrayals of Southern states as reactive to external agendas.

From Taiwan Dream to Everyday Resilience: Temporal Trajectories of Vietnamese Marriage Migrant Women in Taiwan

Huynh Le Anh Huy, Huynh Quoc Tuan

Full text
Marriage migration from Vietnam to Taiwan has often been portrayed in static terms of integration or failure, overlooking how women adapt to ruptures across time. This study applies resilience theory to examine how 27 Vietnamese marriage migrant women confronted disappointment and developed coping strategies within transnational marriages. Drawing on in‐depth interviews, the findings reveal that Vietnamese marriage migrant women endured disillusionments such as domestic violence, neglect, and isolation but actively recalibrated their lives through spirituality practices, small‐scale entrepreneurship and investments in children's education. By situating these practices within a resilience framework, the study highlights how adaptation is not a linear recovery but an iterative process shaped by agency, family obligations and cross‐border ties. Through this perspective, the study shows how aspiration, rupture and recalibration recur across the life course, leading to different rather than converging trajectories. The study extends resilience theory into the domain of marriage migration, contributes to feminist and transnational scholarship by foregrounding everyday agency, and carries policy implications for supporting migrant women beyond early settlement stages.

Migration and Education Choices: How Low‐Skilled Migration Shapes the Educational Attainment of Mexican Youth

Eskander Alvi, Md Ohiul Islam

Full text
This paper examines the impact of low‐skilled migration on educational attainment in developing countries, specifically focusing on Mexico. While previous research has extensively studied brain drain (the migration of high‐skilled workers to developed countries), this study delves into the lesser‐discussed phenomenon of ‘brawn drain’, which involves the migration of low‐skilled workers to developed nations. The paper analyses data from the Mesoamerican Migration Project to understand how the presence of low‐skilled migrant elders (household members aged 26 or older) affects the educational decisions of young individuals (ages 18–25) in high‐migration communities. The results reveal a strong negative impact of family‐level exposure to low‐skilled US migrants on Mexican youth, discouraging them from pursuing further education. The study explores other factors, such as community‐level influences and the presence of high‐skilled individuals as role models, on educational outcomes. Overall, the paper contributes valuable insights to the understanding of how low‐skilled migration affects educational attainment in developing countries like Mexico.

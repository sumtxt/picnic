Entailment and implicature in the language of thought

Irene Canudas Grabolosa, Luca Lorenzo Bonatti, Gennaro Chierchia

Full text
Human language processing involves constant computation of entailments and implicatures, yet the extent to which these inferences depend on linguistic communication remains unclear. This study investigates whether logical structures akin to those in natural language can be derived from purely visual input. Specifically, we examine the polarity sensitivity of scalar inferences (SIs) in non-linguistic contexts. Prior research has established that SIs are preferentially computed in Upward Entailing (UE) rather than Downward Entailing (DE) environments, and we extend those studies by examining whether this pattern extends to visual conceptualization. We conducted three experiments using an oddball paradigm with minimal instructions to our participants, where they witenessed animated ball collisions. Across studies, participants consistently detected these changes to the quantified structure of events (changes from some to all) more readily in UE than in DE conditions, mirroring SI patterns observed in language. These findings suggest that the conceptualization of visual scenes parallels linguistic representations, implying that entailment relations are not exclusive to communication but emerge naturally from cognitive structures. Our results support grammaticalized theories of implicatures, challenging traditional Gricean views that implicatures stem solely from communicative intent. This work contributes to ongoing debates on the relationship between language and thought, demonstrating that logical inferences can arise independently of linguistic input.

Buying Books, Buying Diversity? Public Procurement Theory and the Brazilian State as Book Buyer

Marcel Felipe Machado Lopes, Paula Roméro Cajaty Lopes

Full text
Governments are among the largest single buyers of books, yet public procurement research rarely asks what state purchasing does to the diversity of the market it buys from. This article examines federal book procurement in Brazil using item-level electronic invoice (NF-e) data published by the Office of the Comptroller General (CGU). The data cover 60 months between October 2021 and September 2026 and R$21.9 billion in purchases, 96.7% of which were made by a single buyer, the National Fund for Education Development (FNDE). Drawing on Stirling's variety–balance–disparity framework, we build five indicators of bibliodiversity: supplier concentration (Herfindahl–Hirschman index), the share of the four and eight largest suppliers, supplier evenness, title evenness, and territorial inequality among suppliers. The results describe a market with high variety but low balance. A median of 280 suppliers invoice the federal government each month, yet the four largest take a median 68% of value, and two publishers, FTD and Moderna, account for 45% of the whole series. Concentration is episodic rather than trending: 13 of 60 months exceed the HHI threshold of 2,500, with no significant monotonic trend. Territorial inequality is extreme and stable (median Gini 0.93), with São Paulo-based emitters taking 93% of value. Title diversity falls when supplier concentration rises (Spearman ρ = −0.61) and has declined sharply since mid-2025. We read these patterns against transaction cost, principal–agent, competition, strategic-procurement and institutional theories. We argue that, in markets for unique cultural goods purchased without price competition, the locus of competition moves from the tender to the pre-selection stage, so programme design rather than tendering rules becomes the main lever for diversity. We derive five propositions and discuss the data-quality limits of invoice-based procurement analytics.

Compensatory Input Versus Unequal Gains: Why Schooling Does Not Necessarily Equalize Socioeconomic Achievement Gaps

Jan Skopek

Full text
Recent research has failed to provide clear-cut evidence that schooling equalizes social gaps in achievement. This stands at odds with the dominant view of compensatory input, which holds that even unequal schooling equalizes learning because it compensates for the even more unequal learning inputs children would receive in its absence. I test the previously neglected mechanism of unequal gains from schooling, which could counteract compensatory input and thus explain why studies often fail to find equalization. The unequal gains hypothesis posits a Matthew effect whereby learning gains from schooling depend on children’s prior skills, so that students from higher (lower) socioeconomic backgrounds benefit more (less) from schooling because of their, on average, higher (lower) skills at school entry. Because both mechanisms can operate simultaneously, assessing the equalizing power of schooling requires accounting for achievement prior to schooling. Using data from the newborn cohort of the German National Educational Panel Study (NEPS), I first replicated earlier findings from Germany on the beneficial but non-equalizing effects of school exposure on student achievement—scientific literacy and vocabulary—in Grade 1. Second, after accounting for children’s achievement before schooling, I found evidence of unequal gains counteracting equalization; net of these unequal gains, school exposure did level out learning gaps by social background, although only in the domain of scientific literacy. The findings underline the importance of incorporating prior achievement into studies of school equalization.

Flourishing and Health in Kenya. Philosophical Perspectives and Empirical Insights.

Maike Ritzer, Steffen Flessa

Full text
Numerous studies have demonstrated positive associations between broader dimensions of wellbeing and health. However, evidence from Kenya on this association is scarce and mostly focused on broad evaluative measures of wellbeing, such as life satisfaction. This study draws on conceptualizations of wellbeing from Sub-Saharan African philosophical literature as motivation for examining various specific dimensions of wellbeing in the Global Flourishing Study and analyzing their association with subsequent health in Kenya as a case study, using a longitudinal panel design. Data were drawn from waves 1 (2023; n=11389 individuals) and 2 (2024; n= 7698 individuals) of the Global Flourishing Study. The data are representative of the general population in Kenya. The Impact of U.S. Tariffs on European Public Opinion: Evidence From a Natural Experiment

Joseph Noonan

Full text
How do tariff escalations shape public attitudes in targeted countries? This registered report examines how the second Trump Administration's April 2025 tariff escalation affected European public opinion. Leveraging the announcement's timing during the fieldwork of Standard Eurobarometer 103.3 as an unexpected event, I compare respondents interviewed before and after. Drawing on theories of external threat, in-group identity, and cooperative reciprocity, I test whether the escalation strengthened pro-EU sentiment, worsened views of the United States, and shifted cooperation preferences. I find that it reduced favorable views of the United States, while effects on EU attachment and cooperation preferences were small and non-significant. The decline in favorable views of the United States is concentrated in the most negative response category, indicating that negative views intensified. These results suggest that economic threats from an ally can sharpen antagonism toward the threatening actor without producing broad in-group consolidation or shifts in trade cooperation preferences.

A key element in alignment research is commonly captured by the phrase “alignment with human values.” Human ethical judgment, however, is plural, unstable, affectively charged, socially embedded, and often justified after the fact. Digital capacity in Brazilian municipalities: fiscal autonomy, bureaucratic structure and heterogeneity

Yago Paiva, Lizandro Lui

Full text
Over the past decades, digital transformation has become a central aspect of state modernization. In this article, we treat this process as a form of state capacity building — specifically, of the digital capacity of the state. Our aim is to assess whether, at the municipal level, fiscal capacity and bureaucratic quality account for higher levels of digital capacity. To this end, we adapt to the municipal context a concept originally formulated for national states and construct the Municipal Digital Capacity Index (ICDM), a novel and comprehensive measure built from the seven waves of the Brazilian Survey of Basic Municipal Information (MUNIC) that include an information and communication technology module between 2004 and 2024, covering virtually all Brazilian municipalities in each wave. Using the ICDM as the dependent variable, we estimate panel models designed to recover between-municipality effects. Fiscal autonomy is the strongest determinant of digital capacity, with a magnitude far exceeding that of bureaucratic attributes, whose effects are positive but modest. None of these effects, however, operates uniformly: own-source revenue yields more in low-income municipalities, staff qualification pays off only where there is organizational structure to absorb it, and the share of politically appointed staff, whose average effect is null, is positive in poorer municipalities and negative in wealthier ones.

Full text
In this paper, I aim to make sense of the meanings surrounding the recent surge in popularity of non-professional investing in the Czech and Slovak postsocialist context. The research is based on interviews with individual retail investors and fieldwork conducted at various investor events, meet-ups, and conferences. My Czech and Slovak research partners view investing as a means to ensure the long-term continuation of their household’s wealth and security. A Sociolinguistic Inquiry into Vietnamese Swearing: Regional Variation and Cross-Cultural Parallels

Hàng Hỷ Vinh

Full text
This exploratory, pilot-scale study investigates the Vietnamese swearing construction built from an obscene verb, a kinship term and a second-person pronoun (địt mẹ mày, đụ má mày 'fuck your mother', mả cha mi 'your father's grave') through two questions: how kin-directed swearing differs across regions and which part of the construction reveals the speaker's region (RQ1); and why Vietnamese swearing targets kin and women, viewed against the full repertoire of swearing themes in Vietnamese and neighbouring languages (RQ2). It combines eight Vietnamese dictionaries (1651–2021) and comparative dictionaries of Mường, Cham, Teochew and Cantonese with an online convenience-sample survey of 50 young speakers across four regions, a small Quốc ngữ prose corpus, and a thematic comparison with ten other language communities. For RQ1, the dictionaries show no regional boundary in the verb before 1931, whereas the survey finds a clear North/South split in both the verb and the word for "mother"; regional information is asymmetric, with Southern and Central forms acting as strong markers while standard-compatible forms (địt, mẹ) and online abbreviations (đm, vkl) spread across all regions. For RQ2, sex and kin dominate the free lists; the mother-directed sexual formula occurs in Vietnamese, Chinese, Thai, Hindi and English but is absent from Japanese, so it cannot be attributed to Confucian heritage alone. Kin-directed swearing is rated more offensive than self-directed swearing and triggers more active responses; swears that deny the hearer's descent (đồ con hoang, cả họ nhà mày) are the heaviest and the least mitigated among friends. Young respondents name affection for kin, not family face, as the main reason; we read this as a contemporary synchronic reinterpretation of templates whose diachronic origin lies in honour-based lineage norms. Taken together, the target of kin-directed swearing is the hearer's legitimate standing within the lineage. Results describe young, internet-using speakers and should be read as hypothesis-generating.

Impact of Social Media Platforms on Youth: An Anthropological Study

Sivakumar A

Full text
Social media has become a ubiquitous sociocultural phenomenon, transforming how young people communicate, socialize, and construct identities. This anthropological study examines social media use among young people aged 18–25 in urban and semi-urban areas of Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, South India. It explores usage patterns, self-identity expression, maintenance of social relationships, digital literacy, and the influence of gender, education, and socioeconomic background on digital participation. It also considers how young people perceive social media’s everyday effects on their social and cultural lives. A mixed-methods design was adopted. Quantitative data were collected through a questionnaire administered to 60 youth respondents, while semi-structured interviews were conducted with 10 participants. The questionnaire demonstrated high overall reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91), and its individual scales showed acceptable to high reliability. Content validity was reviewed by experts, while exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were used to assess construct validity. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests of independence; qualitative data were analyzed thematically using NVivo. Findings indicate that social media is embedded in young people’s everyday lives: 73.3% reported using platforms more than once daily. WhatsApp (93.3%), Instagram (88.3%), and YouTube (81.7%) were the most widely used platforms. Entertainment (90.0%) and maintaining contact with friends (85.0%) were the leading purposes, followed by information seeking (63.3%) and identity expression (35.0%). Digital literacy levels were low for 23.3% of respondents, medium for 48.3%, and high for 28.3%. Differences were particularly evident in advanced skills, including evaluating information and managing privacy. Hypothesis testing found significant associations between digital literacy and frequency of use (p = 0.048; Cramér’s V = 0.324), digital literacy and platform diversity (p = 0.047; Cramér’s V = 0.283), gender and identity expression (p = 0.037; Cramér’s V = 0.331), and gender and platform choice (p = 0.041; Cramér’s V = 0.326). Other hypothesized relationships were not statistically significant. The qualitative analysis identified five themes: curated identity performance, digital kinship and connectivity, stratified digital competencies, gendered digital surveillance, and ambivalent impression perceptions. The study contributes to digital anthropology by illustrating how global platforms are locally adopted in South Indian contexts, how social relationships are sustained in digital spaces, and how digital literacy may function as cultural capital. It offers recommendations for educators, parents, policymakers, platform designers, and young people to promote more equitable and beneficial digital participation.

Instrument Revision and False Change in Repeated Surveys: A Typology and Diagnostic Protocol for Secondary Users

Iara Teixeira

Full text
Repeated surveys revise their instruments between rounds and document each revision item by item. Such documentation records what changed but not what the change does to a trend, so secondary users can mistake an instrument effect for change in the population. This article proposes a typology that classifies revisions by their consequence for the information in the released data (removal, redistribution, stimulus change, and misidentification), links each type to its identification status and remedy, and puts it into practice through a five-step diagnostic protocol: comparing source instruments, screening against a reference distribution of unmodified trend items, classifying the revision, choosing an estimand, and auditing metadata. Applied to the 2024 European Working Conditions Survey, the protocol shows that a filter and a self-labeling question placed before the discrimination battery leave the 2015 to 2024 contrast identified only as a lower bound; six of seven grounds fell below every unmodified reference item, and the observed decline would be real only if the new instrument retained more than 60 percent of reports. A new cyberbullying item redistributed reports from the adjacent bullying item, and the data bound the comparable change between odds ratios of 0.75 and 0.98. Changes in item order and response coding left a pace-of-work battery unresolved, while a minor stem change produced no detectable break. The metadata audit found nine variables labeled with the stem of a different question, in both data editions, and a harmonized trend variable that omits one of the two items its label combines. Comparability should be established from the source instruments and the released data before substantive analysis begins.

Visual Resistance Assemblages: Recurring Multimodal Configurations in Kenya’s Gen Z Finance Bill Protests

Castro Chebukosi

Full text
Social-media protest communication is increasingly multimodal, combining language, imagery, typography, sound, performance and platform-specific features. While existing research has demonstrated how such resources produce meaning within individual artefacts and genres, comparatively less attention has been given to how functionally related multimodal configurations recur across heterogeneous artefacts within the same protest corpus. This study addresses this corpus-level problem through a qualitative multimodal analysis of 72 protest artefacts published or circulated on X during Kenya’s June–July 2024 Finance Bill protests, analysed through multimodal social semiotics and cross-artefact comparison. The findings demonstrate how constitutional discourse, distributed participation, comparative fiscal reasoning, collective representation and satirical recontextualisation were organised through heterogeneous multimodal resources. VRA complements existing multimodal, assemblage-oriented and digital activism approaches by shifting attention from multimodality within individual artefacts to recurring multimodal organisation across protest corpora.

The Emotional Meaning and Sociocultural Implications of 'Eojunggan-hada' in Korean Society: An Exploratory Study

Heeju Lee

Full text
This study explores how the Korean expression 'eojunggan-hada' (being in-between) extends beyond simple linguistic ambiguity to reflect issues of personal identity and social belonging. Unlike previous Korean linguistics research that focused on linguistic ambiguity and mitigation expressions, this study defines 'eojunggan-hada' as a way of being positioned at the boundaries of identity and belonging, analyzing its sociocultural implications. Automated Essay Scoring with GPT Models: A Comparison with Human Ratings of IEP Written Products

Daniel Murcia

Full text
This study explores the integration of Large Language Model (LLM)-based Automated Essay Scoring (AES) into classroom writing assessment within an Intensive English Program (IEP). Using a corpus of 46 multi-draft capstone essays written by international students in an Academic Literacies course, the study evaluates the performance of two OpenAI models (GPT-4.1-mini and GPT-5.1) against rubric-based human instructor scores. Essays were scored twice by each model under controlled temperature settings to evaluate linear association, systematic bias, inter-model consistency, and performance-band agreement. Findings reveal that while both LLM scores maintain moderate positive correlations with human ratings (r = .44 for GPT-4.1-mini; r = .49 for GPT-5.1), both models exhibit systematic negative bias, scoring essays an average of 13 points lower than the human instructor. The results suggest that uncalibrated zero-shot LLMs serve better as formative diagnostic tools than autonomous grading systems. Castro Chebukosi, Bernard Kodak, Jane Ombati

Full text
The June–July 2024 Finance Bill protests in Kenya produced a distinctive corpus of digitally mediated political communication in which Gen Z-associated protesters on X (formerly Twitter) relied on omission, shared background knowledge and stylistic markedness to construct political meaning beyond the literal content of their posts. This study examines how such strategies generated Generalised Conversational Implicature (GCI), applying Levinson’s (2000) Q-, I-, and M-heuristic framework to a purposively sampled corpus of 72 protest artefacts retrieved from X in June 2026. Accountability Gap Behind Predatory Publishing in Philippine Academia

Jeconiah Dreisbach

Full text
The mandate of the Philippines’ Commission on Higher Education ties faculty promotion in state universities and colleges to research publications. This commentary argues that the policy's emphasis on publication volume, unmatched by a corresponding verification mechanism, has created conditions in which predatory journals, conferences, and award bodies can thrive. Rising demand for indexed journal publications has already produced unsolicited offers to acquire legitimate, non-profit university journals, commercializing a system meant to signal quality. The threat extends beyond obviously fraudulent venues: recent evidence of paper mines (Barnett & Byrne, 2026), in which openly available datasets are mass-mined into formulaic, artificial-intelligence-assisted manuscripts that pass peer review in ostensibly legitimate journals, shows that volume-based incentives corrupt the research record even when every formal quality marker is present. Reading the Philippine case alongside this global pattern suggests that whitelisting indexed venues is necessary but insufficient. Accountability requires Philippine authorities to complement indexing criteria with a national research integrity framework, institutional verification of authorship, conference, and data provenance, and financial support that does not make ethical publishing contingent on personal wealth.

Full text
Since 2015, Japanese law has required every workplace with 50 or more employees to screen workers for psychological stress once a year. Full text
Research on Kurdish memory in Turkey often treats internal migrants as a single analytic category. This article asks whether the way memory is managed varies with a participant's settlement condition: arriving in a new place, staying in a place that others are leaving, or growing up after the move.

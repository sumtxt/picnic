Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication

The link between three smartphone school policies and students’ mental health: a growth trajectory approach

Lara Schreurs, Robyn Vanherle, Jana Vereecke, Lise-Marie Nassen, Laura Vandenbosch, Kathleen Beullens

Full text
Amid growing concerns about smartphones and students’ mental health, schools worldwide are introducing smartphone policies, often without evidence on their effectiveness. This longitudinal study examines how three naturally implemented policies (full restrictions, partial restrictions allowing use during lunchbreak, partial restrictions allowing use during lunch, and afternoon break) relate to mental health across one school year (up to seven waves). Differences were observed at the first measurement wave (two months after implementation): Students in the full-restriction school reported highest depressive symptoms and anxiety. Differences in life satisfaction were also observed without a clear pattern across policy types. Growth trajectory analyses revealed no significant differences in mental health changes over time. Yet, within schools, both the full-restrictions and the lunchbreak school showed increases in depressive symptoms. Life satisfaction decreased slightly in the lunchbreak school. These findings suggest that smartphone restrictions are not a simple solution to youth mental health challenges.

International Journal of Press-Politics

Television Time and Electoral Success: Evidence From the Constitutional Convention Election in Chile in 2021

Roberto Munita-Morgan, Patricio Navia

Full text
Some democracies give all political parties free television time to level the electoral campaign playing field. Relying on a case that uses open-list proportional representation rules and where voters select individual candidates, but where free television time is assigned to party lists, we explore the association between appearing in free television ads (franja) and electoral success in Chile’s 2021 Constitutional Convention election. In this unprecedented contest, airtime was allocated to parties and lists of independents. We use an original coding of 3,157 mentions across 28 days of television ads for candidates in the four largest electoral lists (692 candidates). Candidates mentioned in ads received higher vote shares (2.9 vs. 2.2 percent) and were more likely to win seats (18.6 vs. 7.7 percent). Multivariate models show that campaign spending is more strongly associated with electoral success, but mentions in the franja have a modest but statistically significant positive coefficient. Although we cannot disentangle the potential endogeneity of being more electable and appearing in the franja, the findings highlight the importance of television time for electoral success and point to how party-controlled media exposure influences intra-list competition.

How Personal Curation Shapes Political Engagement on Social Media? Examining The Politics of Personal Curation Model

Biying Wu-Ouyang

Full text
Although individuals today have a wide range of media choices to engage in politics, recent investigations have revealed that many are increasingly avoiding news and disengaging from politics, especially in social media. From a user-centered perspective, few effective strategies on social media have been systematically examined to enhance political engagement. Established through the curated flows framework, this study proposes the Politics of Personal Curation (PPC) model, which offers a fresh perspective by examining how everyday mundane personal curation in social media (platform expansiveness, content heterogeneity, news attentiveness, and source belongingness curation) have significantly influences on political engagement. A preregistered study from a nationally representative panel in the United States ( N = 1,270) generally indicates that expansiveness, heterogeneity, and news attentiveness curation yield higher political engagement, whereas belongingness curation is linked to lower engagement. This study further supports that these media effects on political engagement are channeled through enhanced cognitive elaboration and moderated by cross-cutting discussion.

Webs of Ainu Mosir: Indigenous Creative Networks on YouTube

Xanat Vargas Meza, Takumi Yokoyama, Chieko Nishimura, Ryohei Nishida, Yoichi Ochiai

Full text
The Ainu are Indigenous people living primarily in Japan. As they have recently adopted social media, and network-based analyses of First Nations cultural manifestations are uncommon, this study drew on Indigenous and social network frameworks to examine creative networks in the virtual Mosir (the world as viewed by the Ainu). Our objective was to clarify the types of collaborators, their cooperation patterns and relationships relevant to producing Ainu-related YouTube videos. Three YouTube lists of 428 videos were utilised in this study. Publicly available data were downloaded using a YouTube Application Programming Interface (API). The videos were watched and classified, including anonymised data on collaborators. The relationships of the collaborators were quantified and mapped, network measurements were calculated and collaborator clusters were examined. The most frequent types of collaborators were individuals and communities. We also observed that individuals moderately affected network clustering ( p = .002). Furthermore, individuals were somewhat correlated with the average number of ties ( p = .004) and the bridging of clusters through betweenness ( p = .006), with Japanese male celebrities and Ainu people, particularly Ainu women, showing high centrality. As Indigenous rights advance internationally, the virtual Mosir shows how crucial it is for First Nations to participate in multimedia production processes, particularly if such productions are available on social media. Indigenous network frameworks were highly compatible with network analysis, allowing for a more comprehensive overview of Indigenous production processes. This can enhance the multiple knowledges of First Nations, while fostering accountability and reciprocity of their allies.

Careful Dis/Connection: Bringing Digital (Inter)Dependent Relations Out From the Shadows of Autonomy

Sara Van Bruyssel

Full text
Although always-on connectivity affords autonomy, it also creates dependencies on digital devices. Digital disconnection has emerged as a countermovement, with people seeking ‘authentic’ offline reconnection under the discourse of (self-)care. Yet, a pertinent question is whether certain care relations are contingent on digital dependencies between people and, despite being perceived as burdensome, should therefore not be broken through disconnection. Drawing on feminist ethics of care, which understands care as all the work that maintains everyday life, this article examines how care and digital dis/connection practices shape each other. Empirically, it draws on ethnographic fieldwork with 20 adults and analyses three vignettes through a relational framework that follows care occurring in, through and away from digital devices. The findings show that digital well-being is shaped less by individual media use than by the care relations that digital technologies sustain. Experiences of digital dis/connection reveal how connectivity is embedded in often invisible responsibilities, dependencies, and forms of care. Rather than locating digital tensions in technologies themselves, the analysis suggests that such tensions expose underlying challenges in how care is organised, valued, and distributed. In response, the article develops the framework of careful dis/connection to evaluate digital practices according to how they sustain, redistribute, and make visible the care on which everyday life depends.

Algorithms of Injustice: An Ethnographic Content Analysis of Black Youth’s Experiences with Racial Trauma Online

Amanda M. McLeroy, Tiera Tanksley

Full text
This study explores the pervasive presence of racially traumatic content (RTC) on social media and its profound impact on the mental well-being of Black youth. Utilizing ethnographic content analysis and grounded in critical race technology theory (CRTT), the research identifies key manifestations of RTC, including Black death, digital harassment, deepfakes, and war content. Social media’s algorithmic structures amplify RTC, perpetuating systemic anti-Black racism while exposing Black youth to vicarious trauma, anxiety, and depression. Despite these challenges, social media platforms also provide spaces for activism and community-building. Black youth use these digital arenas to engage in social justice narratives, find solidarity, and develop coping strategies. The findings emphasize the critical roles of educators and counselors in mitigating the adverse effects of RTC by fostering media literacy, digital resilience, and culturally responsive mental health support. This research contributes to evidence-based interventions aimed at empowering Black youth to navigate digital spaces safely, advocate for systemic change, and thrive in an equitable and inclusive online environment.

Automated Influence: How Social Bots Reshape Discursive Opportunities in Online Activism

Linda Li, Helen Margetts

Full text
Social bots are an increasingly prominent presence in online protest discourse. We adapt the discursive opportunity structure (DOS) framework for digital protest, refining its dimensions of visibility, resonance and legitimacy to account for platform-mediated discourse and automated activity. Applying the refined framework through computer-assisted content analysis of bot activity in two major protests – Black Lives Matter (BLM) and Extinction Rebellion (XR) – we move beyond treating bots as a monolithic or uniformly disruptive force, identifying competing pro- and counter-protest strategies distributed differently across the three dimensions. In BLM, pro-protest bot content concentrates on cultural resonance, whereas counter-bot content targets legitimacy; in XR, pro-protest bots are used to build legitimacy and participatory visibility, whereas counter-bots more often work to downplay the movement’s salience. The framework’s granularity discriminates between movement types in ways coarser accounts of ‘political bots’ cannot, showing that understanding contemporary movements requires attending to how automated activity is deployed within their discourse.

New Media & Society

Praise, rent, and the platform: Substack and the enclosure of blog culture

Will Riley

Full text
This article situates Substack within the platformisation of blogging and the enclosure of cultural production within proprietary infrastructures. Tracing the genealogy of blogging as a decentralised practice that carried within it the seeds of citizen journalism and informal intellectual life, I argue that Substack does not restore an apparently lost cultural practice but renegotiates and interpolates blogging into a platformised logic. Framed as a return to independent, writer-led publishing, Substack instead, as I argue, renders free-labour interaction as a source of rent. I analyse the rhetoric and revolving discourse around Substack’s internal curatorial project, ‘The Substack Post’. Drawing on Jodi Dean’s account of blogs as displaced mediators alongside theories of digital labour and platform capitalism, and approaching Substack as a simultaneously platformised and infrastructuralised formation, I argue that praise operates as a platform-specific form of commodity fetishism, animating the platform while obscuring the labour relations that sustain it.

Platformed affective withdrawal: Quiet quitting and the refusal of gendered emotional labour on TikTok

Aybüke Büyükkağnıcı

Full text
This article examines #quietquitting on TikTok as a platform-mediated articulation of gendered affective withdrawal. While organisational research interprets quiet quitting as reduced discretionary effort or declining work engagement, this study examines how quiet quitting is articulated as a digitally circulated refusal of feminised emotional labour. Drawing on feminist theories of affective labour and critical platform studies, the analysis explores how workers renegotiate expectations of emotional availability within algorithmically structured visibility regimes. The study is based on a qualitative-dominant mixed-methods analysis of 586 TikTok videos shared under #quietquitting between 2020 and 2026. Combining thematic discourse analysis with descriptive engagement indicators, the findings show that creators frequently frame quiet quitting as a refusal of unpaid relational mediation, emotional management, and workplace care. The article introduces the concept of platformed affective withdrawal to explain how narratives of workplace disengagement circulate through algorithmic visibility systems that amplify and individualise gendered labour inequalities.

Stress and self-efficacy under artificial intelligence disruption: A mixed-methods study on the usage of artificial intelligence among Finnish workers

Atte Oksanen, Teo Keipi, Moona Heiskari, Iina Savolainen

Full text
Rapid workplace integration of artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping work practices and challenging employees’ self-efficacy and well-being. This mixed-methods study examines these experiences using national survey data ( N = 2109) and qualitative interviews ( N = 54) with Finnish workers. Frequent AI use, AI-induced identity threat, and organizational AI deployment are associated with higher AI strain, whereas AI self-efficacy relates to frequent use, positive AI attitudes, and subjective well-being. Identity threat moderated several of these associations, highlighting the role of professional identity in AI-related experiences. Analysis drawing on Greimas’ semiotic modalities (must, can, know, and want) explains how these patterns are interpreted and negotiated in practice, revealing tensions between perceived self-efficacy and pressures to integrate AI. AI use was often described as voluntary. However, participants anticipated increasing competence demands and responsibility. The findings emphasize the need for organizational strategies that address identity-related challenges and support employee well-being in AI-driven work transformations.

Human Imagery and Psychological Reactance in Persuasive Health Messages

Chelsea L. Ratcliff, Helen Lillie, Manusheela Pokharel, Jakob D. Jensen, Doris E. Acheme, Benjamin Sam-Paintsil

Full text
Although mass-mediated persuasive messages often feature images of people, their impacts are surprisingly understudied. We tested whether attributes of human imagery influence psychological reactance to a health promotion PSA, either alone or combined with language style. Participants ( N = 1,100) viewed a message promoting sun safety that contained a forceful (vs. courteous) appeal and featured a model with forceful (vs. neutral) body language. The model’s physical characteristics (gender and skin tone) also varied. Both forceful text and forceful body language indirectly lowered protective behavioral intentions via reactance, and male-model messages had this effect for female participants. Our findings demonstrate the potential for features of human imagery to inadvertently hinder persuasion. Study results also highlight the value in measuring perceived visual freedom threat alongside traditional reactance measures. We discuss implications for reactance theory and measurement and highlight important directions for research on reactance to visuals.

Understanding Early Attitudes Towards Generative AI Regulation in Higher Education: The Role of Academic Scientists’ Experience and Risk Perceptions

Jinghuan Ma, Tipeng Chen, Rong Zuo, Timothy P. Johnson, Eric W. Welch, Ashlee Frandell, Wenceslao Arroyo‐Machado, Lesley Michalegko

Full text
As generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) technology rapidly permeates higher education, it creates opportunities to transform teaching, research, and workforce preparation while raising ethical, social and environmental concerns. Academic scientists have a unique role in this transition as their early exposure to publicly accessible GenAI tools shapes emerging practices, norms, and institutional responses. Existing research often treats AI‐related risk as a single construct, limiting understanding of how distinct risk dimensions shape regulatory preferences. This study investigates how academic scientists’ experience with GenAI relates to risk perceptions over agency loss and data breach, and how these perceptions are associated with their regulatory attitudes during its formative stage. Using survey data collected between September and November 2023, shortly after ChatGPT became widely accessible, we find that greater GenAI experience is associated with lower perceived risks. Meanwhile, different risk perceptions are linked to distinct regulatory preferences: perceived risk of agency loss is associated with a greater likelihood of supporting federal regulation and a lower likelihood of supporting academic self‐regulation, whereas perceived data breach risk is associated with a greater likelihood of supporting federal regulation but not associated with the likelihood of supporting self‐regulation. These findings provide an early baseline understanding of faculty attitudes toward GenAI regulation in academic contexts.

Gambling Advertising and Problem Gambling in the United States: Multi‐Level Evidence Beyond Sports‐Betting Legal Status

Chris J. Vargo, Toby Hopp, Harsha Gangadharbatla

Full text
US gambling debates often center on the legal status of sports‐betting, but advertising can also be used as a distinct policy lever. This paper presents the findings of two related studies. Study 1 links quarterly state‐level gambling advertising outlays across paid media (2014–2023) to Google Trends indices for 116 problem‐gambling help‐seeking queries across 41 states (1640 state‐quarters). Study 2 combines surveys with passive digital traces from 419 exposure‐eligible mobile‐platform users. Higher state‐level ad spend, but not sports‐betting legal status, was associated with greater help‐seeking search interest, and this association strengthened over time. Platform‐specific gambling‐ad exposure on Facebook, TikTok, and X was associated with gambler status, gambling frequency, and the probability of any observed gambling‐site visit. Clean gambling ads were also associated with higher 24‐h odds of subsequent gambling touchpoints, especially web visits; among gamblers, ad‐triggered touchpoints were associated with problem‐gambling scores more consistently than raw clean ad volume. Convergent state‐level and individual‐level evidence links gambling advertising with engagement and harm‐related indicators, while the observational designs do not identify causal effects. The findings support stronger restrictions on advertising volume, placement, inducements, and targeting.

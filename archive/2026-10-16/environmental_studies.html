Enhancing fairness in climate action through policy packages

Toon Vandyck, Umed Temursho, Florian Landis, David Klenert, Matthias Weitzel

Full text
Achieving a fair transition to climate neutrality requires a careful balancing of policy options. Here we quantitatively evaluate the equity implications of different European Union climate policy packages, considering pricing, regulation via standards, subsidies and transfers. Leveraging an integrated modelling framework, we show that standards limit impact variability within income groups, whereas carbon pricing with transfer-based revenue recycling offsets regressive impacts between income groups. Restricting climate policy implementation to either option, therefore, gives rise to a trade-off between horizontal and vertical equity. We then demonstrate numerically how a multi-instrument budget-neutral policy package can resolve this trade-off. Adopting a broader equity perspective that encompasses both horizontal and vertical equity shifts the preferred policy mix towards a combination of standards, pricing, subsidies and transfers. These results provide an explicit, quantified, equity-based rationale for the adoption and acceptability of comprehensive climate policy packages, offering a more nuanced view on policy design.

Citizens’ assemblies propose ambitious climate policy but impacts remain limited

Janosch Pfeffer, Hannah Einhauser, Johanna Hauke, Kira Hoffmann, Lisa Ruf, Milena Weber, Florian Weckbecker, Marlene Wellhausen, Jens Newig

Full text
Climate citizens’ assemblies convene randomly selected citizens who, after structured learning and deliberation, develop policy recommendations. Yet, whether they produce more ambitious recommendations and influence political decisions remains contested. Here we conduct a comparative, counterfactual assessment of 488 recommendations from five national and one state-level assembly in Western Europe, drawing on document analysis and expert interviews. The results show that citizens consistently proposed more ambitious climate measures than existing government policy. Recommendations sometimes influenced policy by adding momentum, enriching plans, breaking deadlocks or introducing novel ideas, but such influence was rare. Only the French assembly shows substantial policy impact, with one-third of its recommendations having influenced policy, due to strong power delegation, political leadership and resulting public pressure. In the other cases, policy impact was minimal or absent. These findings show that citizens’ assemblies can raise policy ambition, but translating recommendations into political decisions depends strongly on political conditions.

Skill and spatial mismatches for sustainable development in Brazil

Anna K. Berryman, Fernanda Senra de Moura, Joris Bücker, Pete Barbrook-Johnson, Marek Hanusch, Penny Mealy, J. Doyne Farmer, R. Maria del Rio-Chanona

Full text
Deforestation is a major source of global greenhouse gas emissions, and continued forest loss risks pushing the Amazon from a carbon sink to a carbon source. While recent work shows that productivity-led structural transformation towards less land-intensive sectors can reduce deforestation, the labour market implications of such pathways remain poorly understood. Here we address this gap by developing a spatially and occupationally explicit agent-based labour market model informed by empirically observed job-transition networks to simulate the ability of workers to respond to labour demand shocks. Our analysis reveals significant skill and geographic mobility frictions that constrain workers’ ability to adapt to structural change. Applying the model to productivity-driven development scenarios, we find that pathways delivering the largest deforestation and emission reductions disproportionately benefit high-skill workers while increasing unemployment among agricultural and other low-skill workers. Mobility-enhancing labour market policies can mitigate these effects, highlighting the importance of understanding labour market dynamics for equitable and feasible deforestation-reducing development pathways.

Quantifying assets for the geopolitics of climate change mitigation

Guanna Pan, Ulrike Kornek, Chen Chris Gong, Edna Johanna Molina Bacca, Gunnar Luderer, Ottmar Edenhofer

Full text
Climate change mitigation reshapes the distribution of economic assets in the low-carbon transition. Here we quantify climate-related assets for 169 countries across four dimensions: fossil fuel production, climate change damages, renewable energy potential and land carbon sequestration capacity. We combine integrated assessment models, climate projections, economic damage functions and biosphere datasets to assess asset changes under multiple mitigation scenarios. Geopolitical challenges and opportunities arise from climate change mitigation because losses and gains across energy and environmental assets are country-specific, which we further characterize through a broad clustering and a detailed pairwise similarity analysis. Our analysis suggests comparatively moderate impacts for the USA, the European Union and China, highlighting the challenge that the largest economic powers may not be those with the strongest asset-based interest for climate action. While our framework remains limited, our data provide input for integrating other ethical, governance and socioeconomic factors in future analyses of opportunities and challenges for ambitious climate change mitigation.

Managing Groundwater Salinization Toward Sustainability

Dongmei Han, Matthew J. Currell, Yoseph Yechieli, Ryan T. Bailey, Abhijit Mukherjee, Xuan Yu, Dawid Potrykus, Gerson Cardoso da Silva, Guoliang Cao, Yi Xiao, ... (+6 authors)

Full text
Groundwater, a crucial freshwater source worldwide, is under threat of salinization due to the combined pressures of climate change and human activities, hindering sustainable development. This review explores both natural and anthropogenic sources and mechanisms of groundwater salinization (GWS). We describe the impacts of GWS on environmental and socioeconomic systems, highlighting the risks to agriculture, water quality, and public health. We also discuss monitoring and assessment techniques and knowledge gaps essential for effective management. We present successful case studies of interventions in seven countries, showcasing the feasibility and difficulty of effective management and mitigation strategies. Finally, we address sustainability challenges and outline future directions for research and policy, emphasizing the need for a paradigm shift from reactive management to proactive, holistic, and adaptive stewardship.

A Review of Beyond GDP: History, Barriers, Opportunities, and Cautions

Joseph Eastoe, Ida Kubiszewski, Robert Costanza, Rutger Hoekstra, Annegeke Jansen, Peter Benczur, Kenneth Mulder, Richard Heys, Sandrine Dixson-Declève

Full text
Gross domestic product (GDP) remains the dominant measure of economic progress, despite long-standing critiques including its failure to account for environmental sustainability, inequality, and social well-being. In the context of escalating climate breakdown and rising inequality, GDP is increasingly being recognized as an inadequate metric for national prosperity. This article reviews the growing movement toward alternative measures that prioritize sustainability, inclusion, and the well-being of humans and the rest of nature. Hundreds of alternatives exist, but recent research shows significant similarity among them, forming the basis for building a broad consensus. This review explores how academics and policy makers are responding to the limitations of standard national accounts and highlights broad public support for expanding economic indicators. This review also identifies the key barriers to be overcome in order to move beyond GDP—including vested interests, institutional buy-in, and the fragmentation of alternatives—and suggests strategies to address each. By examining these barriers and evaluating potential solutions within the current political landscape, this review contributes to the discourse on how national accounting systems can better evolve to reflect the goals of a just and sustainable economy embedded in society and the rest of nature.

Governing Solar Geoengineering at Planetary Scale: Pipe Dream or Possibility?

Frank Biermann, Aarti Gupta, Rakhyun E. Kim, Florian Rabitz

Full text
Solar geoengineering is being suggested by some scientists as a partial solution to the climate crisis. However, these hypothetical technologies to reflect parts of incoming sunlight back out into space pose significant governance challenges, due to their ecological risks, planetary scale, and long-term consequences. This review examines the literature on solar geoengineering governance since 2020, identifying three strands of debate. The first explores emerging research governance and institutional arrangements for potential future deployment. The second, critical, strand focuses instead on institutional pathways to prohibit such technologies. A third strand analyzes the debate itself, including through the lens of science and technology studies. While proposals for solar geoengineering are developed by small groups of researchers in industrialized countries, opposition is growing in the Global South, with governments from Africa and the Pacific calling for an international nonuse agreement. This growing resistance from vulnerable nations raises pressing moral and political questions about continuing research amid mounting global dissent.

Other Effective Area-Based Conservation Measures (OECMs) in Global Area-Based Conservation

Catherine M. Wensink, Daniel J. Ingram, Awantha Dissanayake, Nicola Weber, Julia E. Fa

Full text
Other Effective Area-Based Conservation Measures (OECMs) are an emerging area-based conservation mechanism aspiring to achieve long-term and effective in situ biodiversity conservation outside traditional protected areas. Contributing toward several global biodiversity targets, they combine protection with sustainable land use and human development. This review analyzes spatial and temporal trends in OECM site designation (2010–2024), exploring governance frameworks, management, and ecological effectiveness in reducing biodiversity loss, addressing environmental change, and promoting sustainable development. Emphasis is placed on inclusivity of OECMs, particularly engagement of local communities and Indigenous Peoples in stewardship and decision-making, thereby enhancing social equity and cultural relevance. Academic and policy attention has centered on two areas: ( a ) refining the OECM framework, its definition, characteristics, and alignment with international goals (Section 3), and ( b ) evaluating governance, effectiveness, and national/subnational implementation (Section 4). While OECMs offer potential to expand global conservation, issues around governance and site-level outcomes remain underexplored and warrant attention (Section 5).

Worlds Made Extractive: Black, Decolonial, and Feminist Geographies of Extractivism and Resistance

A. Murrey

Full text
Over the past two decades, scholars have reconceptualized extraction as ( a ) embedded within racial capitalism's systems of global, uneven accumulation and ( b ) a constitutive organizing principle of contemporary capitalism. Extractivism refers to contested discursive and material practices, governance technologies, and terrains of struggle rooted in racial capitalism which shape how people perceive and relate to the world. Extractive logics describes the ontological and epistemic structures through which extractivism renders differentiated subjects legible, governable, and exploitable. Extractive capitalism fosters dissent, consent, and acquiescence and by foregrounding the micropolitics of extractivism—labor, emotions, embodiment, endurance, care and domestic labor, and life-making practices—social scientists working differently within and across Marxist, Black, feminist, and Indigenous traditions show how people and more-than-human life inhabit and unsettle extractive logics. This review draws from transdisciplinary research in political economy-ecology that reinterprets slow dissent, refusal, and resurgence as world-making practices which reclaim time, land, emotion, and relationality against extractive capitalism.

Expanded Urban Innovation for the Twenty-Second Century: From Cutting Emissions to Enabling Flourishing Lives on a Thriving Planet

Dennis Pamlin, Pourya Salehi, Massamba Thioye, Sascha Nick, Felix Creutzig, Ganesh Das, Jay Hennessy

Full text
Cities are central to the climate challenge, but much research on urban climate action still focuses on emissions reductions within existing systems. This literature review assesses existing research and literature, including work on human-need-driven innovation for global sustainability and the export of globally sustainable solutions. Rather than limiting urban climate action to measures aimed at reaching zero emissions, the review examines an expanded urban innovation agenda in which cities are studied as incubators and exporters of innovations that support flourishing lives for all on a flourishing planet. The review synthesizes insights from sustainability transitions, sociotechnical systems, well-being economics, artificial intelligence, and brain science. Case studies from different cities illustrate how some urban innovation agendas are already shifting. The comparison highlights how a focus on flourishing lives can mobilize start-ups, youth, and public institutions, while addressing equity and positioning cities as actors in the next generation of urban climate action.

Social Energy Futures in the Global South: Geographies, Concepts, and Praxis

Sofía Ávila-Calero, Ksenija Hanaček, Marcela Torres Wong, Tristan Partridge, Brototi Roy, Bruno Fornillo, Arnim Scheidel

Full text
Socioenvironmental research highlights the key role of communities and organizations in promoting sustainable and democratic energy systems. However, most of this emphasis has focused on modern industrialized societies. This article reviews research on energy alternatives conceptualized and practiced by movements and communities across the Global South. Based on the surveyed literature, we propose the concept of social energy futures (SEF) as an open-ended category to reflect on the drivers and features of these alternatives. As outlined, SEF are diverse and territorially situated, commonly encompassing three components: ( a ) they are largely needs-oriented, tending to prioritize use value over exchange value; ( b ) they involve broad political processes, defining how much energy should be harvested, through which technologies, by which actors, and for which purposes; and ( c ) they are part of broader identity struggles, reflecting how social actors actively organize to counteract colonial and corporate relations amid the energy and climate crisis. Taking these findings together, we propose an SEF analytical toolkit and outline future research themes to continue developing across diverse academic, cultural, and geographical settings.

Metric Selection Framework: A Method for Optimizing Multidimensional Value Assessment of Resource Recovery Systems

Eleni Iacovidou, Spyridoula Gerassimidou, Philip Purnell, Norman Ebner

Full text
This article introduces the metric selection framework (MSF), developed as part of the CVORR+ methodology. CVORR+ is a systems-based evaluation toolkit designed to support complex sustainability assessment and decision-making. Within CVORR+, the MSF translates system analysis into decision-oriented, multidimensional metrics for monitoring, assessment, and evaluation of progress toward sustainability targets. Grounded in a value-focused decision-making approach, the framework combines expert elicitation with stakeholder engagement to ensure that metrics are system grounded, context specific, and practically relevant. Applied to England's plastic packaging system, the MSF reveals systemic inefficiencies, identifies leverage points for intervention, and informs policy design to support systemic value recovery. The case study demonstrates the heuristic and practical value of the MSF in capturing interdependencies across environmental, economic, social, and technical domains. Future developments will streamline the translation from system conceptualization to metric development, ensuring that metrics are responsive to long-term monitoring and policy-making, remain aligned with technological and regulatory shifts, and enhance the robustness and transferability of CVORR+.

International Migration Review

Migration Studies and International Business Scholarship: Toward Mutual Renewal in the Study of High-Skilled Migration

Aida Hajro, Milda Žilinskaitė, Francisco D. Bretones

Full text
This article introduces a special issue of International Migration Review , aimed at bringing migration studies and international business (IB) scholarship into dialogue. Although both interdisciplinary fields examine processes that are fundamentally interconnected—the movement of people and the movement of firms—they have largely developed in parallel. In this introduction, we review the most relevant IB scholarship, consider the extent to which it has drawn on migration-related insights, and reflect on why it has nonetheless remained relatively detached from broader migration studies. We then examine what IB can contribute to research on high-skilled migration, what migration studies can offer IB in return, and outline four pathways for building stronger conceptual bridges between the two fields. Collectively, the articles in this special issue illustrate that potential by illuminating the multiple intersections between high-skilled migration and IB.

Fragile Transnationalism: Rethinking Migrant Transnationalism for a Post-Neoliberal World

Daniel Nehring, Yang Hu

Full text
In this article, we develop the concept of fragile transnationalism to conceptualize the re-infrastructuring of migrant transnationalism under conditions of post-neoliberal globalization. Since the 2008 Great Recession, the world has witnessed the demise of neoliberal globalization, amid an ongoing political, socioeconomic, and environmental polycrisis, and the consequent emergence of a post-neoliberal world order. We argue that the attendant fragmentation of global political order, the resurgence of authoritarian nationalism worldwide, and the societal implications of climate change and emergencies have reconfigured the basic conditions for migrant transnationalism. Our analysis points to a far-reaching reassertion of the nation-state in migration governance. In response, we retheorize migration infrastructure as a conceptual lens for analyzing migrant transnationalism. We reveal how an assemblage of weak infrastructures, particularly the fragility within and between its constituent components, has reshaped the logic, practice, and experiences of transnational migration and lives.

Journal of Refugee Studies

Navigating conflicts in refugee shelters: decolonial everyday peace and humanitarian constraints

Fabricio B Carrijo

Full text
Refugee shelters often become sites of conflict and violence. Grounded in refugees’ lived experiences and situated knowledges, this article introduces the framework of Decolonial Everyday Peace in Refugee Shelters to examine how residents prevent and respond to conflicts while navigating and contesting coloniality in micro-level peacebuilding. Drawing on a 6-month ethnographic fieldwork with Ukrainian refugees in a Warsaw shelter, the article discusses five everyday peace practices developed through the research: avoidance, strategic distraction, engagement, support seeking, and grounded resignation. The analysis shows how they unfold within, rather than beyond, the tensions of shelter life, embodying agency while remaining constrained by coloniality, including the shelter’s top-down peacebuilding approach, which limits possibilities for everyday peace. Yet, refugees also create spaces of coexistence, unsettling, however temporarily, colonial hierarchies. Decolonial everyday peace thus emerges not as an idealized end-state, but as a plural, situated, and unfinished process, continually constructed and reshaped through refugees’ everyday interactions.

Population, Space and Place

Between Family and Community: Intergenerational Interactions and Pathways to Active Ageing in Urban China

Jiayao Zhou, Geoffrey C. Chen

Full text
This article examines how intergenerational interactions intersect with community resources to shape pathways to active ageing among urban older adults in China. Drawing on intergenerational solidarity theory, it conceptualises family and community as an integrated configuration in which care responsibilities and moral expectations are redistributed rather than simply shifted. Based on 40 semi‐structured interviews conducted in a subdistrict of Health Commission of Changning District Shanghai (2024), the analysis compares three residential arrangements: three‐generation co‐residence, empty‐nest households with distant children, and proximate but non‐co‐resident families (“a bowl of soup's distance”). It identifies three pathways: facilitation, where stable family support enables community participation; substitution, where community services replace routine functional support under distance; and complementarity, where proximity and community embeddedness jointly sustain autonomy. Across pathways, active ageing emerges as a moralised achievement sustained through self‐discipline, selective disclosure, and restrained help‐seeking to avoid “burdening” adult children.

Divergent Paths of US Counties: Multiscale Drivers and Spatial Dynamics of Population Growth and Decline (2010−2020)

Mohammadreza Gholami Gowhareh

Full text
Population change across US counties has taken sharply different paths, with some areas experiencing steady growth while others face persistent decline. These trends are not random; they reflect deeper shifts in the economy and uneven regional development. This study contributes to demographic and regional research by treating population growth and decline as two distinct demographic regimes, each influenced by its own set of factors. We examine which factors are associated with county‐level population change in the United States from 2010 to 2020, using data from 3130 counties with complete records across all explanatory variables, compiled from the American Community Survey, the Bureau of Economic Analysis and fiscal records. To capture both overall patterns and local variations, we combine standard regression methods (OLS, spatial lag and spatial error models) with Multiscale Geographically Weighted Regression (MGWR). Counties are explicitly divided into growing ( N = 1548) and shrinking ( N = 1582) groups, with models estimated separately for each. The findings show a moderate but consistent asymmetry between growth and decline. Declining counties tend to cluster together and are associated with consistent factors such as aging populations, weak labour markets, reliance on agriculture and, to a more limited extent, housing tenure. Growth, on the other hand, is more spatially variable and localized. It is associated with an agricultural employment structure operating at a broad scale, while natural increase and tax burden exhibit highly localized associations, and housing tenure shows a broader but uneven association. MGWR provides a substantially better fit than global models in both cases ( R 2 = 0.546 for growing counties and 0.595 for shrinking counties, compared with 0.043 and 0.150 under standard OLS), highlighting that population change is a complex, multiscale process. Understanding these spatial patterns is crucial for developing policies and planning strategies that are sensitive to local conditions.

International Migration

Welcome Home? The Role of Stayees in Return Migration

Małgorzata Dziekońska

Full text
While most research on return migration focuses on return migrants' experiences, this article highlights the perspectives of stayee family members. Based on in‐depth interviews with 32 Polish stayees, it examines how they prepare for returnees' arrival and how they re‐adapt to their renewed presence, thereby supporting their reintegration into the society of origin. The analysis distinguishes two stages of receiving returnees back: the welcome stage, which is primarily concerned with practical arrangements and emotional preparedness, and the reception stage, in which returnees are further accompanied and assisted—or not—after arrival. The reception stage reveals two analytically distinct but often overlapping dimensions of stayees' practices: domestic and family inclusion, and socio‐economic inclusion. These forms of engagement vary with the strength of relationships and stayees' family positions. The findings show that return migration is a relational process involving both returnees and stayees, shaped by different understandings of responsibility and support.

Voices of Change: Low‐Skilled Migrant Workers' Perspectives on Labour Law Reforms in Qatar

Šemsia Jatić, Abdoulaye Diop, Fahmida Naheen, Engi El‐Maghraby, Haneen Al‐Qassass, Buthaina Al Khelaifi

Full text
In the years preceding the 2022 FIFA World Cup, Qatar introduced a series of labour reforms aimed at strengthening migrant workers' rights, including the abolition of exit permit requirements, the removal of the No Objection Certificate requirement for job changes and the introduction of a universal minimum wage. Drawing on semi‐structured interviews with 30 low‐skilled male migrant workers conducted in October 2023, this study examines how workers perceived and experienced these reforms in their everyday lives. The findings reveal a nuanced picture that resists binary assessments of success or failure. Many participants described meaningful improvements in job mobility, freedom of movement and living conditions, particularly those who had spent many years in Qatar and were able to compare conditions before and after the reforms. However, the translation of legal change into everyday experience was uneven. Workers continued to navigate their rights through informal information networks, relationships with employers and unequal social and financial resources that often predate migration itself. By centring migrant workers' own voices, this study contributes to a more grounded understanding of labour reform as a socially embedded process shaped by relationships, unequal resources and the everyday contexts through which rights obtain meaning.

Naiveté or Strategic Ambiguity? Examining Economic, Demographic and Security Narratives in India's Refugee Policy

Setu Gupta, Swati Singh Parmar

Full text
The decadal transition in the Indian politico‐legal stance on refugees, from a human rights tilt to national security concerns, invites a close examination. India maintains a theoretical safe distance from the international legal framework on refugees by not acceding to the 1951 Refugee Convention, while on the ground, it extensively engages—politically and judicially—with refugee‐related domestic legal issues. In the absence of a dedicated legal framework, the refugees, categorised based on temporalities and spatialities, face a highly unpredictable executive and judicial discretion. The present work critically examines the validity of three purported claims that India frames as ‘obstacles’ in enacting refugee‐specific legislation, using a mixed method design—combining doctrinal analysis of Indian jurisprudence, Parliamentary questions, statutory law and Executive Policy Rules along with a review of demographic data and securitization aspects to draw some hints as real‐world inferences on the actual impact caused by refugees.

Relationality and Temporality of Return Migration: Overseas Chinese Students During the COVID ‐19 Pandemic

Yinni Peng

Full text
This study applies the perspectives of relationality and temporality to investigate how overseas Chinese students made their return decisions during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Analysis of qualitative data obtained from 56 returning Chinese students during the pandemic indicates that their return decisions were relational and temporal: embedded in their familial and romantic relations and intertwined with their life trajectories of migration–education–employment. Identifying three types of return, namely hurried, predetermined, and thoughtful, during the pandemic, the study reveals the nuanced relational–temporal dynamics of the risks, opportunities, and emotions negotiated and contested between the students and their families and intimate partners during the crisis.

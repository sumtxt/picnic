Public Service Decline and Support for the Populist Right: Evidence from England’s National Health Service

ZACHARY P. DICKSON, SARA B. HOBOLT, CATHERINE DE VRIES, SIMONE CREMASCHI

Full text
The rise of populist right parties is well studied, but relatively little attention has been given to how public service performance influences voter support. Given that public services are often the primary means through which citizens interact with the state, we argue that declining public services can create grievances that increase the appeal of populist right parties. Focusing on England’s National Health Service, we combine administrative data on local health facility closures with panel data on public preferences and voting intentions. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design, we find that closures reduce satisfaction with public services and increase support for populist right parties. These effects are moderated by migrant registrations at local health practices, highlighting the interaction between public service performance and immigration concerns in fueling populist right support. Our findings underscore the role of public service decline as a driver of support for populist parties, especially in areas undergoing demographic change.

The Synthetic Imputation Approach: Using LLMs to Generate Synthetic Texts for Underrepresented Categories in Supervised Classification Tasks

Joan C. Timoneda

Full text
Annotated text data are central to many empirical applications in political science, yet many annotation tasks involve categories that are sparsely populated. These low- N categories pose problems for both encoder-only and decoder-only (generative) Transformer large language models (LLMs), leading to low performance, biased evaluation and measurement error in downstream analyses. This article introduces the synthetic imputation approach, a targeted method for expanding sparse categories while remaining consistent with the category-defining content of the human-coded examples. The approach uses a generative LLM to produce additional texts for underrepresented categories by conditioning on small, rotating subsets of human-coded examples. Rather than paraphrasing or replacing the original data, synthetic imputation adds informative variation only where data are thin. I evaluate the approach using both encoder-only and decoder-only LLM classifiers across multiple political science applications. The results show that synthetic imputation substantially improves performance and evaluation stability in sparse settings, recovering full-sample performance once a modest number of true observations is available, with limited and predictable overfitting at very low levels of N .

Spatiality of preemptive coercion: How autocrats demolish informal settlements for political control

Shiqi Ma

Full text
Why do authorities demolish informal settlements in metropolises? The social sciences have a long tradition of addressing this issue through the lens of economic development or welfare provision. I instead propose a theory of political control. Autocrats adopt demolition as a preemptive and coercive tool to remove communities substantively and symbolically less legible to them, thereby ensuring societal compliance and restoring visual order. Empirically, I compile a unique dataset of 320 demolished and undemolished informal settlements in Beijing between 2017 and 2018, based on satellite imagery, digital street views, and fieldwork. The results indicate that the low legibility of informal settlements to authorities correlates with a greater likelihood of demolition. This paper contributes to the social science literature by connecting legibility politics with urban governance. It also introduces a spatial perspective and leverages novel data sources to deepen our understanding of preemptive coercion under authoritarianism.

Comparative Political Studies

Divergent Legacies: How the Local Instantiation of National Conflict Conditions Postwar Political Engagement

Joseph Lasky

Full text
Under what conditions does violence generate legacies of political [dis]engagement? Victimization is frequently linked to higher rates of political engagement – voting, protesting, attending meetings, etc. Often, however, victims withdraw from politics. These initial responses are evident generations later. I reconcile these disparate trends by analyzing the materialization of national conflict at the local level. When a community experiences violence perpetrated by non-local factions, victimization is more likely to politicize. By contrast, when perpetrated by members of one’s community against other local members, violence is more likely to depoliticize. To test these arguments, I leverage an original survey, archival material, and interviews from the understudied case of Cameroon. From 1956 to 1971, the Union des Populations du Cameroun (UPC) fought against French and Cameroonian state and paramilitary forces. Civilians were heavily targeted by the state, militias, and UPC guerillas. Today, variation in those local experiences is associated with differences in political behavior and socialization practices.

Perspectives on Politics

Revisiting the Carbon Lock-In: Outlining an Agenda on How Affective Polarization Constrains Climate Policy Making

Evan Drake

Full text
Carbon lock-in, the self-reinforcing inertia that perpetuates fossil fuel dependence, emerges from interdependent technological, institutional, economic, and behavioral factors. As climate policy has become increasingly polarized across advanced democracies, the role of affective polarization in reinforcing these barriers remains underexplored. This research agenda asks the following question: How and to what extent does affective polarization sustain the carbon lock-in? Drawing on social identity theory and the carbon lock-in literature, I propose a framework in which affective polarization operates as a cross-cutting process strengthening existing lock-in mechanisms through three pathways: (1) elite framing that activates both institutional distrust and out-group animosity toward pro-climate groups; (2) trust erosion linking vertical (elite–mass) and horizontal (mass–mass) polarization to sustained policy resistance; and (3) self-reinforcing feedback loops between polarizing rhetoric and mass resistance that persist across electoral cycles. Partisan identity sorting can transform climate policies into identity markers, turning otherwise reversible constraints into path-dependent barriers that persist even when policies are economically beneficial or technically effective. The agenda applies specifically to high-polarization contexts and calls for empirical investigation of these mechanisms and of depolarization interventions.

European Journal of Political Research

Strategic alienation: The dynamics of group appeals in negative campaigning

Lena Maria Huber, Martin Haselmayer

Full text
This paper examines how political parties use social group appeals in negative campaigning. We argue that parties strategically target opponents with group-based attacks to portray them as failing key social groups. Using a novel dataset of Facebook and Twitter posts by parties in the UK (2016–2024), linked to voter perceptions of group representation from the British Election Study, we test whether such attacks are more likely when the sender party enjoys strong group-based credibility, or the target party is seen as a weak representative of the respective group, or both. We find no evidence for the former but strong support for the latter: parties disproportionately target rivals with poor reputations for representing specific groups, and especially so when the sender holds a reputational advantage. These findings advance our understanding of identity-driven party competition, showing that group-based attacks exploit opponents’ representational vulnerabilities rather than reinforce attackers’ own strength.

Authoritarian orientations and the contestation of liberal self-determination: Cross-national evidence from 30 countries

Heiko Giebler, Lukas Antoine, Rasmus Ollroge

Full text
In contrast to expectations of global liberal convergence, liberal values and institutions are increasingly contested. This study investigates whether core liberal conditions for self-determination are likewise subject to contestation and how this varies across societal contexts. Drawing from liberal theory, we identify five enabling conditions for self-determination: physical well-being, basic economic security, learning and personal growth, moral equality, and influence on political decision-making. These conditions differ in their degree of ‘liberal uniqueness’, with the latter two representing distinctly liberal ideals. Using original survey data from over 45,000 respondents across 30 countries, we examine how authoritarian orientations – conceptualised as preferences for authority, conformity, order, and security – relate to support for these conditions. Such orientations are consistently and negatively associated with uniquely liberal conditions, especially moral equality and political influence. In contrast, there is no meaningful relationship with basic economic security and a positive association with physical well-being. Preferences for learning and personal growth fall in between. These associations vary with levels of societal modernisation; in more modernised societies, the gap between respondents with stronger and weaker authoritarian orientations grows wider, with the strongest widening observed for the uniquely liberal conditions. Our findings suggest that authoritarian orientations are linked to different understandings of self-determination. These competing visions of what it means to live freely are more polarised in modern societies. Recognising this divergence helps to better understand the normative foundations of contemporary societal conflicts across diverse countries.

Digital turnout mobilization: Enhancing voter turnout with digital messaging

Kasper M. Hansen, Esther Ejrnæs Bastholm

Full text
Voter turnout is declining across Europe, raising concerns about democratic legitimacy and prompting a search for effective mobilization strategies. The new digital era has transformed the landscape for political communication, making it possible to mobilize voters through direct digital messages. These messages have proven to be a low-cost and easily scalable tool, capable of reaching millions of voters efficiently and with broad coverage. We synthesize existing evidence through a comprehensive meta-analysis of voter mobilization experiments from the past decade that apply digital messages as a Get-Out-The-Vote (GOTV) tool. The analysis assesses the overall effectiveness of digital messaging in increasing turnout, with particular attention to its potential for engaging marginalized groups and promoting participatory equality. Second, we present results from a new population-wide GOTV experiment conducted during the 2024 European Parliament election in Denmark – the first to apply digital messages to the entire electorate. The intervention increased turnout by 0.5 percentage points. By using year-of-birth bins to estimate effects across all age cohorts and disaggregating the immigrant category into Western and non-Western subpopulations, we can examine previously unexplored in-depth heterogeneous effects. We find that digital messages consistently have positive effects on turnout, including among marginalized voters.

Voice After Exit: the Prioritization of Homeland-Facing Foreign Policy Among the Hong Kong Diaspora

Maggie Shum, Eddy S. F. Yeung, Ka Ming Chan, Sanho Chung, Athena Tong, Sam Yip

Full text
Citizens around the world have migrated under the shadow of authoritarianism, and many are increasingly acquiring political and voting rights in their host countries. Yet we know little about the content of their foreign-policy preferences or the salience with which they prioritize homeland-facing issues relative to domestic concerns. We argue that experiences of authoritarian rule prior to migration generate anti-regime sentiments among diasporas, inclining them toward hawkish foreign-policy positions toward the homeland regime. These preferences heighten the salience of homeland-facing foreign policy, even relative to highly consequential domestic issues. We test this argument with harmonized candidate-choice experiments administered to an original sample of Hong Kong diasporas in four host countries (Australia, Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States). We find that respondents strongly preferred candidates advocating hawkish economic and geopolitical policies against China. These foreign-policy positions were consistently prioritized over salient domestic issues such as healthcare and the economy, and this structure of preferences held across subgroups with varying degrees of host-country integration and homeland ties. Our findings provide novel experimental evidence that diasporas continue to care intensely about and voice on behalf of their homeland even after exit.

Relational Clientelism, Information Processing, and Accountability

Miquel Pellicer, Eva Wegner

Full text
Research shows that providing information on incumbent programmatic performance often has limited impact on citizen attitudes towards incumbents, even if citizens tend to care strongly about corruption and service delivery. We use a survey experiment in South Africa and Tunisia to study if relational clientelism, a type of clientelism often valued by clients, plays a role in this puzzle. We present respondents with a radiocast that varies the profile of the politician (clientelistic vs. neutral) and his performance in service delivery and corruption (good vs. bad). We find that, when clientelistic politicians are valued, performance information on them matters less than for neutral politicians. The effect appears to operate via motivated reasoning: Respondents are less likely to process negative performance information when exposed to the clientelistic profile. Our findings suggest that, in certain contexts, relational clientelism is a valued candidate characteristic that can offset bad programmatic performance akin to co-partisanship or co-ethnicity.

Daughters and Political Preferences of Politicians: A Preregistered Prospective Study on Swedish Politicians

Anna Dreber, Magnus Johannesson, Elin Naurin, Patrik Öhberg

Full text
Important studies have suggested that politicians become more liberal by having daughters, but more recent work has been unable to replicate this result. It is therefore essential to further clarify how politicians’ family situations affect their policy making in different contexts, and in particular when it comes to gender-related policies. We provide a unique test based on prospective data collection on Swedish politicians based on a pre-analysis plan strictly followed in all analyses and tests. Data is collected in two surveys implemented after the 2022 general parliamentary election in Sweden on members of parliament and candidates in the parliamentary election (n=1,147 complete responders are included in our analyses). In our primary hypotheses, we test whether having daughters is associated with more left-leaning political preferences, a stronger preference for female-favoring policies, and a weaker preference for male-favoring policies. We find no evidence of an effect on left-leaning political preferences or female-favoring policies. These results do not support the original work on this topic but are in line with the more recent null results. We also find suggestive evidence (p<0.05) that having daughters is associated with a weaker preference for male-favoring policies. As the effects of having daughters on male-favoring policies have not been explored earlier, this opens up an interesting avenue to investigate in future studies.

Love of variety? An experimental study of heterogeneous responses to foreign brands in the marketplace

Valentina Gonzalez-Rostani, Jude C. Hays

Full text
Do everyday encounters with foreign brands shape political attitudes? We argue that brand origin cues activate national identities, producing different responses among nationalists and cosmopolitans. In an online shopping experiment, U.S. participants were randomly assigned to compare American (control), European, or Asian (treatment) sport utility vehicles without explicit mention of brand origin. Exposure to foreign brands increased opposition to trade and immigration and support for Donald Trump among nationalists. Cosmopolitans generally showed little change. These effects are not reducible to partisanship, revealing within-coalition divides among Republicans and among Democrats and Independents. Our findings show that subtle consumer experiences spill over into politics, creating a bottom-up channel of polarization that links nationalism and populism to support for neomercantilist and nativist policies.

Political Psychology

The partisan dilemma: How people evaluate sacrificial dilemma decisions by politicians across the political divide

Silvana D'Ottone, Matilde Tumino, Paul Conway

Full text
Politicians often face policy decisions where sacrificing some targets benefits others. As motivations behind such decisions can be ambiguous, partisans may ascribe nobler motives to politicians who make decisions they approve of and whose party they identify with. Consequently, evaluations of politicians can be shaped by the politician's decision, party affiliation, the domain of sacrifice, and the evaluator's political orientation. Across three studies, the US and South American participants ( N = 1595) evaluated liberal/left‐wing and conservative/right‐wing politicians who accepted or rejected educational and economic sacrifices. We examined how participants' political orientation influenced judgments of sacrificial decisions. Conservative/Right‐wing political orientation frequently (though not always) predicted higher ratings of conservative/right‐wing politicians and lower ratings of liberal/left‐wing politicians across decision acceptability, vote‐worthiness, warmth, and competence—even when both politicians made the same choice. Study 2 demonstrated that the direction of bias depends on the policy domain, with liberal‐leaning participants less accepting of educational sacrifices and more accepting of economic sacrifices than conservative‐leaning participants. Study 2 further suggested that inferences about politicians' underlying motivations partly drive partisan bias. These findings demonstrate that judgments of realistic sacrificial dilemmas are significantly influenced by the social context—specifically, who is evaluating whom and over which issues.

The boundaries of global concern: How socioeconomic context shapes the link between global consciousness and environmental attitudes

Ayu Okvitawanli, Moh. Abdul Hakim, Reginapta Nitya Prasetyani, Takeshi Hamamura

Full text
Climate change requires unprecedented international cooperation; yet growing protectionist sentiment poses a significant barrier to collective action. Global Consciousness (GC), a psychological orientation characterized by a connection to humanity and the world as a whole, has been theorized as a key driver of benevolent behaviors, including pro‐environmental attitudes. However, empirical evidence linking GC to environmentalism remains largely confined to specific contexts, leaving the generalizability of this relationship across diverse societies unclear. Leveraging data from the European Values Survey (EVS) and the World Values Survey (WVS), this study examines the link between GC and indicators of environmentalism: confidence, active membership in environmental organizations, and prioritizing environmental protection over economic growth. We further examine whether socioeconomic context moderates this relationship, hypothesizing that the effect of GC on environmental attitudes will be stronger in wealthier nations, as measured by the Human Development Index (HDI). Multilevel models revealed that, across 35 countries in Study 1 ( N = 40,259) and 86 countries in Study 2 ( N = 123,280), GC is consistently and positively associated with environmentalism. Crucially, this relationship is significantly moderated by HDI. These results indicate that the positive effect of GC on environmentalism is stronger in countries with higher socioeconomic progress, suggesting that GC more readily translates into actions when supported by macro‐level structural resources.

From misogyny to mobilization: Hostile sexism and conflictual online discussions indirectly predict men's rights activism via heightened identification as a men's rights supporter

Morgana Lizzio‐Wilson, Emma F. Thomas, Cassandra M. Quarisa, Anna C. Barron, Danny Osborne

Full text
Although sexism may serve as a gateway into the men's rights movement, few studies have examined group processes in online environments that fuel this reactionary movement. We address this gap by examining whether interacting with like‐minded others fosters an emergent social identity as a “men's rights supporter” among sexist men; and whether identification predicts willingness to engage in reactionary actions undermining gender equality. Across two studies ( N s = 443, 755), men completed a hostile sexism measure and read about the perceived impacts of feminism on men. Participants then either discussed whether feminism negatively impacts men in an online chatroom (group deliberation) or reflected individually (individual deliberation). Hostile sexism predicted identification as a men's rights supporter, and this link was stronger in the group deliberation condition. In turn, identification predicted greater reactionary action intentions. Unexpectedly, these effects emerged only among men who indicated that their group was unsure about the impact of feminism after the discussion. A post‐hoc content analysis of the pooled chat logs revealed that these men engaged in discussions marked by conflict and disagreement, suggesting that identification with the movement emerged through dissent rather than consensus. These findings highlight complex social dynamics underpinning engagement with the men's rights movement.

Egotropic and sociotropic negativity bias in social policy reform preferences

Tijs Laenen, Elise Aerts

Full text
Although negativity bias is widely documented in political psychology, evidence is far more mixed when it comes to voters' preferences for social policy reform, particularly when they are confronted with trade‐offs between personal and societal outcomes. Building on existing theories of political communication, this article examines how voters evaluate policy reforms when considering realistic information about potential losses and gains. Empirically, we analyze a simu‐survey experiment examining how voters update their reform preferences in response to information derived from microsimulation modeling about the predicted effects of policy reform on their pocketbook and on the poverty level. The results show that negative information weighs more heavily than positive information in shaping reform preferences at both the egotropic and sociotropic levels. In the case of competing information, the effects are asymmetric: Positive pocketbook information tends to be neutralized by negative poverty information, whereas negative pocketbook information outweighs positive poverty information. However, egotropic negativity bias is most pronounced for smaller pocketbook losses, which are more likely to violate voters' prior expectations than larger losses. Consistent with this pattern, expectation violations—rather than differences in recall of, or trust in, information—seems the primary mechanism underlying negativity bias in policy preferences.

Philosophy & Public Affairs

Evidentiary Interpretation, Discursive Injustice, and Rap on Trial

K. M. Crandall

Full text
The submission of rap lyrics as inculpatory evidence (Rap on Trial) is an active prosecution tactic deployed in United States criminal trials. In this paper, I argue that interpretive ambiguity in rap lyrics is exploited in Rap on Trial cases, constituting a novel form of discursive injustice: extractive discursive injustice. Rap, a historically Black American artform, has core oral‐traditional elements such as its usage of Signifyin(g) that render it interpretably ambiguous. Utilizing the concept of hermeneutical impasses, I explain how a pernicious interpretation of rap lyrics nonetheless receives evidentiary uptake, revealing a crucial junction where injustice seeps into the courtroom. I argue that two speech act‐theoretic sites of injustice are present in Rap on Trial cases, detailing how one strain—discursive injustice—occurs. In doing so, I theorize novel forms of constitutive refusal and discursive injustice that occur when an utterance is extracted from one conversational context and deposited into another. I argue these are distinct from traditional forms in the method of arisal, though congruent with their function and display. I conclude by addressing an objection questioning the denial of truth‐telling value within rap lyrics and considering how a valid evidential use of rap lyrics might occur.

Integrated Explanations of Well‐Being

Paal Fredrik S. Kvarberg

Full text
According to three plausible assumptions about well‐being, a person's welfare depends on (i) the valence of her experiences, (ii) the degree to which her life resonates with her, and (iii) the extent to which she fulfills her potential. Theories of well‐being have struggled to accommodate all three in a determinate manner. The difficulty can be framed as a dilemma: either jettison considered convictions, or sacrifice the clarity required for measurement and practical guidance. The aim of this article is to show that the three most influential theories are impaled by one of the horns in the dilemma, and diagnose the source of the problem. In doing so, I draw attention to an under‐theorized form of explanation that integrates phenomenological, conative and functional elements in a unified account that might overcome it.

Research & Politics

Big Tech, big risk? How education and ideology shape public attitudes toward Big Tech companies

Tobias Tober, Marius R. Busemeyer, Sebastian Koos

Full text
This paper examines public attitudes toward Big Tech and their relationship to policy preferences in six OECD countries. Drawing on original survey data collected in 2022 from 19,800 respondents in Germany, Japan, Poland, Spain, Sweden, and the United States, we analyze how education and political ideology shape evaluations of large technology firms such as Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft. Using six survey items on the perceived economic, social, and democratic effects of Big Tech, we identify two weakly correlated attitudinal dimensions: positive and negative evaluations. A cluster analysis reveals five groups of respondents—supporters, skeptics, ambivalent, dismissive, and neutral—highlighting that public opinion on Big Tech is not organized along a single continuum. Bayesian multinomial regression models show that higher education is associated less with outright support than with greater ambivalence, while ideology structures attitudes in distinct ways: skepticism is more pronounced on the left, whereas ambivalence rises especially among highly educated respondents on the right. These attitudinal clusters are closely linked to preferences for regulation and government support. The findings suggest that public opinion toward Big Tech is complex, politically structured, and consequential for policy debates.

How Do (Mis-)Perceptions of Refugee Gender Composition Shape Attitudes? Evidence from a Survey Experiment

Andrea Blasco, Michal Krawczyk, Hannah Nohlen

Full text
People’s perceptions of others, particularly those from other countries, can shape their attitudes and willingness to offer solidarity. We study public beliefs about the gender composition of forcibly displaced populations in a large online survey experiment. Participants ( n = 13,008) representative of the adult population of six EU countries, consistently underestimated the share of women among the people displaced by the Russian war on Ukraine. We show that a belief-correction intervention effectively prompted updates in public beliefs, aligning them more closely with actual numbers. The source of information matters: updates were greater when the intervention cites official statistics rather than other people’s opinions. Although female migrants are typically preferred, the intervention did not change perceptions regarding current refugees’ impact on crime and economy or policy preferences (family reunification). While all these measures correlate with beliefs about gender composition, the intervention did not significantly affect any of them.

Using Television Drama to Promote Electoral Corruption Reporting: An Evaluation in Northern Nigeria

Dan Brown, Ariel Lee Bothen, Michelle Krieger, Fatima Turaki, Mansur Kurugu, Maji Peterx, Catherine Angai, Ummi Yakubu, Vanessa Alli

Full text

Covariate Adjustment in Political Science Experiments: Progress and Recommendations

Matthew H. Graham, Binh Hoang

Full text
Covariate adjustment can reduce the standard error of experimental treatment effect estimates without increasing the sample size but is vulnerable to abuse unless a specific procedure is preregistered. We assess adjustment practices in ten political science journals in 2013, 2018, and 2023–24. Encouragingly, preregistration and covariate adjustment have each been rapidly adopted. Preregistration is also a good predictor of what practices are used in articles. However, just 12% of articles published in 2023–24 preregister a specific covariate adjustment procedure and use adjustment for the main estimate. Although preregistering an adjusted estimator is not always advisable (when covariates are unavailable or preregistration is not possible), there is substantial room for improvement. We recommend that when feasible, researchers preregister a specific covariate adjustment procedure and use it for their preferred estimate regardless of covariate balance. This may require a shift in preregistration culture away from predicting results and toward constraining analysis.

European Political Science Review

Professionalizing deliberation: a Social Network Analysis of UK climate mini-publics

Grace Cooper, Kate Long

Full text
Deliberative mini-publics have become an increasingly prominent feature of climate governance across advanced democracies. In the United Kingdom, their rapid expansion has been accompanied by the emergence of a professional deliberative field composed of facilitators, recruitment agencies, advisory bodies, expert witnesses, and network organizations. While existing scholarship debates whether professionalization enhances deliberative quality or introduces risks of marketization and elite capture, there is limited empirical evidence on how influence, standards, and authority are structured within this field. This article addresses that gap by mapping the actors involved in the design and delivery of UK climate mini-publics using qualitative social network analysis. The analysis examines how professional ties shape the circulation of expertise, standards, and backstage decision-making. The findings identify important and influential actors within the network, and their place within the wider industry. Here, we highlight the unequal concentration and clustering within the network. Rather than finding clear evidence of elite capture, the analysis reveals a form of structural dependency. Responsibility and authority are frequently transferred from commissioning bodies to facilitators, concentrating influence in a small number of actors. While these actors often act as safeguards of deliberative integrity, this reliance also introduces fragility, risks of stagnation, and uneven standards across the field. The article concludes that professionalization is neither inherently corrosive nor unproblematic. By foregrounding the networked architecture of deliberation, the study contributes a new perspective on how democratic innovations are governed, stabilized, and constrained in practice.

Centripetal narratives under crisis? Media framing and the Europeanization of economic discourse in Portugal, Spain, and Ireland

Susana Rogeiro Nina, João Moniz

Full text
This article examines whether and how national media in Portugal, Spain, and Ireland converged in their interpretation of European economic issues during the Eurozone crisis. Building on research on the European Public Sphere, it analyses how national media located political authority, responsibility, and problem-solving capacity under conditions of intensified EU involvement in domestic economic governance. Conceptually, the article distinguishes between relational convergence, referring to cross-national similarity in media narratives, and directional convergence, capturing within-country shifts in narrative orientation along the national–EU axis. Empirically, the analysis draws on a comparative framing study of 6,070 economic news articles published in six quality newspapers between 2002 and 2015. Narrative interpretation across five framing dimensions—Problem, Cause, Responsibility, Consequences, and Solution—is operationalised through an original Convergence Index capturing the balance between EU-centred and nationally centred narratives. The findings show no generalised relational convergence following the onset of the crisis. Instead, cross-national dispersion in narrative orientation increases in the post-crisis period. Directional convergence towards EU-centred narratives occurs selectively: Spanish and Irish media display modest centripetal shifts, whereas Portuguese coverage follows a contrasting trajectory of systematic re-nationalisation. Overall, these results qualify crisis-driven expectations of communicative integration. While the Eurozone crisis intensified diagnostic and accountability-oriented framing across national media, it did not generate stable or cumulative convergence in narrative interpretation. Media convergence thus emerges as a differentiated and non-linear process, with important implications for how the communicative foundations of a European Public Sphere should be assessed under conditions of systemic stress.

Can multi-dimensional descriptive representation increase policy priority alignment? Evidence from South African municipalities

Evan Lieberman, Sarah Lockwood, Philip Martin, Vinothan Naidoo

Full text
Does an increase in the number of shared identity traits between citizens and their public representatives increase the likelihood that they will agree on policy priorities? The identities of citizens and politicians are complex and multi-dimensional, but we know little about how the combination of multiple shared identity traits affects the convergence of attitudes on government policy. In this paper, we analyze two waves of original survey data on local government councillors in conjunction with several surveys of citizens in South Africa. We find that both within and across political parties, greater degrees of trait similarity between citizens and politicians – even beyond the one or two traits considered in most studies of descriptive representation – are associated with modest but consistent increases in alignment on policy priorities, especially when citizens and councillors share traits that reinforce a historically marginalized identity. We also show that trait similarity increases policy priority alignment to a greater degree when politicians have roles that require frequent contact with constituents, suggesting that communication channels may strengthen the substantive impact of descriptive representation.

Does politics drive cultural preferences? Voting behavior in the Eurovision Song Contest

Recent scholarship argues that crises can create opportunities for women's political leadership.

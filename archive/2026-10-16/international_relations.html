The dissent score: using events data to measure dissent when media scrutiny varies

Carlisle Rainey, Harley Roe, Qing Wang, Nick Dietrich

Full text
Researchers commonly use measures of dissent or protest in their analyses. Yet a standardized, public, ready-to-use measure does not exist. In this article, we introduce such a measure—what we call the “dissent score.” To create the dissent score, we follow the standard practice in the literature and use the number of dissent events reported in events data sets. However, we make two improvements. First, we adjust for variation in media scrutiny by using the rate of dissent events—the number of dissent events compared to the total number of events reported in the data set. This is important because the total number of events explains about 50% of the variation in the number of dissent events. Second, we use a hierarchical model to partially pool these rates within and across countries. This is important because many places and times have relatively few events recorded; partially pooling reduces the measurement error. We validate the dissent score by showing that it correlates strongly with Fariss’ latent human rights protection score and substantially improves out-of-sample predictions in a reanalysis of a comprehensive model of repression. Further, while raw event counts show no support for the “more dissent in the middle” hypothesis, regime type and the dissent score have the expected inverted-U relationship. We supply the country-year dissent score for 195 countries from 1990 to 2004 in an easy-to-use format. Perhaps more importantly, we also describe our method and supply the computer code so that researchers can extend our method, make adjustments, compute the dissent score for alternative place-times, or apply the idea to new concepts. We also raise important considerations for creators of events data sets; although we focus on dissent, our approach applies broadly to any event type coded from media reports, including international conflict events and terrorism.

Belonging, violence, and natural resource governance: experimental evidence from Mali and Niger

Alma Bezares Calderón, Simon Mercer, Ifeoluwa M Olawole, Matthew K Ribar

Full text
The presence of non-state armed groups in the Sahel complicates effective natural resource governance, which is critical for conflict resolution, economic growth, and climate resilience. In response, African governments and donor organizations have alternatively implemented both community-led and state-led interventions to strengthen natural resource governance institutions. It is unclear, however, what governance strategies will be successful in the face of these challenges. We use a vignette survey experiment administered to 3,607 rural household heads in Mali and Niger to show that (1) respondents consistently believe that a community-led natural resource governance system would be more effective, more equitable, and more resilient to climate change than either the status quo or a state-led system; (2) that members of local ethnic outgroups have greater confidence in community-led and state-led regimes relative to the status quo; and (3) exposure to violence reduces confidence in all proposed systems. This article advances our understanding of natural resource governance under institutional pluralism, explores the endogenous relationship between state capacity and armed conflict, and identifies potential limits on the extent to which strengthening local institutions can substitute for low state capacity.

How does political fragmentation within dominant ethnic groups shape whether challenger organizations use electoral politics, nonviolence, or violence? Evidence from Sri Lanka

Agatha Skierkowski Hultquist

Full text
How does political fragmentation within dominant ethnic groups—i.e., politically relevant ethnic groups that control the executive branch of government—impact whether challenger organizations representing politically disadvantaged ethnic groups use violence, nonviolence, or electoral politics to achieve their political goals? We know that fragmentation within politically disadvantaged groups who advocate for self-determination affects the violent and nonviolent tactics that challenger organizations use. However, ethnic mobilization is not unique to politically disadvantaged groups, and politically dominant groups may be just as fragmented as the disadvantaged groups over whom they govern. I argue that fragmentation within politically dominant ethnic groups conditions the political opportunity structures in which challenger organizations operate. As political fragmentation in the dominant group increases, creating elite divisions and leverage points, challenger organizations are more likely to use nonviolence or electoral politics, while violence becomes a tactic of last resort when these divisions are lacking. Using an original measure of political fragmentation based on legislative roll call votes in Sri Lanka, I find support for the theory. These results demonstrate that unlike fragmentation within politically disadvantaged groups, which has been found to increase the use of violence against the state, fragmentation within politically dominant ethnic groups incentivizes challenger organizations to realize their goals through nonviolent channels, lending additional insights to the literature on tactical choice in conflicts and elite divisions in social movements and nonviolent resistance.

Beyond coercion: how female political representation shapes sanctions termination

Hoan La

Full text
What explains variation in the duration of international sanctions and the mechanisms through which they are terminated? While the existing literature on sanctions termination focuses on strategic and structural factors, this study introduces a gender-based perspective, arguing that higher female political representation in the target state increases the likelihood of sanctions termination, particularly for sanctions with significant human costs. Two main pathways link women's representation to sanctions outcomes. First, women in leadership positions foster compliance with sender demands by increasing government responsiveness to humanitarian suffering and facilitating collaborative compliance processes. Second, female officeholders influence senders’ strategic calculations by signaling reforms and creating humanitarian pressure, giving senders a reason to lift sanctions. Using event history analysis on panel data of 366 sanctions cases from 1990 to 2018, the study finds that women in executive and legislative roles in target states predict shorter sanction durations, primarily through faster target compliance, while evidence for sender capitulation is more limited.

The legacy of rebel taxation on post-conflict fiscal capacity

Andrea Arroyo Petro, Vincenzo Bove, Jessica Di Salvatore, Roberto Nisticò

Full text
Civil wars often erode state sovereignty, as institutions are replaced or co-opted by armed groups that establish informal governance structures. Among these, rebel taxation emerges as a key tool for financing wartime activities but also for administering territories. In this article, we explore whether wartime rebel taxation shapes post-conflict governments’ fiscal capacity, drawing on cross-national data from post-conflict states in Africa between 2000 and 2015. We emphasize the dual nature of rebel taxation. On the one hand, rebel taxation creates institutions and routines that may support post-conflict enforcement and compliance, especially when rebel groups are part of or co-opted into post-conflict politics. On the other hand, rebel taxation can undermine state authority by entrenching informal systems and introducing an alternative fiscal contract between citizens and non-state actors that may be hard to replace. Our analysis supports the first argument, finding a positive association between rebel taxation and post-conflict tax revenues. This result is especially pronounced in conflicts that ended without decisive military victories, suggesting that complete capitulation of rebels or governments hinders the integration of wartime taxation in post-conflict fiscal bureaucracy. Our findings highlight the enduring impact of rebel taxation on state capacity in post-conflict environments.

Pave the Way to Diversion: Increased Salience of Foreign Policy at the Times of Economic Hardships

Burak Kazim Yilmaz, Efe Tokdemir

Full text
Most research on the diversionary use of force focuses on timing of conflict initiation and target selection, often treating the public as passive observers ready to take the bait. This article challenges this assumption by examining how leaders strategically elevate foreign policy issues in public discourse before resorting to the use of force. We argue that economic downturns lead to anticipatory rhetorical agenda-setting, through which leaders increase the salience of foreign policy in shaping the conditions under which military actions become feasible. Using a large language model, we measure foreign policy salience in 29,286 US presidential addresses and remarks between 1945 and 2019. Combining these data with monthly inflation and US military interventions, we examine whether patterns in presidential rhetoric are consistent with a rhetorical pathway linking economic distress to the use of force. The results are consistent with our theoretical expectation. Higher inflation is associated with increased foreign policy salience in presidential speeches and heightened salience is, in turn, associated with a greater likelihood of military intervention, while inflation itself exhibits no robust direct relationship with the use of force. Our findings align with the expectation that diversionary dynamics may unfold as a mediated process in which leaders place greater rhetorical emphasis on foreign policy issues prior to the use of force. By highlighting the role of anticipatory agenda-setting, this study offers a nuanced perspective on diversionary conflict and highlights the importance of presidential rhetoric as a key component of foreign policy outcome.

The Social Legacies of Repression: Family, Friends, and Political Participation

Alexandra D. Blackman, Nicholas J. Lotito, Elizabeth R. Nugent, Daniel L. Tavana

Full text
Existing scholarship on the legacies of state violence treats families as the primary vehicles through which repression shapes political behavior. Yet individuals are embedded in multiple social groups; a robust literature shows that both kin and non-kin relationships are consequential for political socialization. This paper examines the comparative effects of direct repression and the repression of family members, friends, and community members on subsequent political behavior. We draw on an original nationally representative survey of 1,200 adult Tunisians, supplemented by a targeted sample of 175 sibling pairs. We find that individuals who reported themselves or family members as repressed under the Ben Ali and Bourguiba dictatorships (1956–2011) are significantly more likely to report protest participation. Those who reported repressed friends and community members are more likely to report both recent protest attendance and electoral turnout. Analyses of the sibling pairs show that the relationship between repression of friends and communities and protest participation remains significant when controlling for unobservable family characteristics. Additional analyses indicate that political discussion with friends is a key mechanism through which repression’s behavioral effects are transmitted. These findings suggest that scholars who focus on familial networks risk underestimating the broader reach of repression’s political legacies.

Symbolism and polarization in American public opinion: The case of border security

Beth A. Simmons, Lauren E. Pinson

Full text
We explore the effect of political symbols on mass attitudes and partisan identities under conditions of political polarization. Politicians are motivated to select visible, simple, and salient symbols to influence a target audience. Some of the most potent symbols may further polarize attitudes and affect. We test our expectations using the case of border security. Two decades of polls, national panel surveys, and original conjoint experiments in the United States suggest that border barriers have become increasingly partisan over time. A final survey experiment suggests a likely mechanism: exposure to a key symbol (here, a border wall) enhances strong Republican identifiers’ nationalist attitudes. Moreover, increasing the border wall’s salience bolsters ingroup feelings among moderate Republicans and reduces strong Republicans’ warmth for Democrats (the outgroup). Political symbols that divide respondents along salient identity lines contribute to polarization, sometimes creating sharp disagreements alongside consensus in American public opinion.

Diffusing norms, facing friction: International non-governmental organisations and the struggle of global governance efforts

Germán Bidegain, Fernanda Odilla

Full text
International non-governmental organisations rely on nationally embedded affiliates to advance global norms, yet relationships between headquarters and national chapters sometimes end in organisational rupture. Why do nationally embedded affiliates shift from voice to exit? This article addresses this question through a comparative study of Transparência Brasil and Uruguay Transparente, two former chapters of Transparency International (TI). Drawing on interviews, document analysis and a Most Different Systems Design, it shows that despite operating in markedly different contexts, both chapters followed a similar trajectory: persistent tensions over organisational priorities, modes of action and internal governance gradually eroded loyalty, while the absence of effective mechanisms to mediate these differences led to the disaffiliation of both chapters from TI’s network. These findings contribute to debates on the governance of international non-governmental organisations by showing how unresolved tensions between global coordination and local autonomy can lead nationally embedded affiliates to shift from voice to exit.

What’s the point of ideal theory?

Frank Lovett

Full text
Critics of ideal theory contend that ideal theory cannot guide present political action: from a practical point of view, they claim, social ideals are redundant at best and a positive hindrance at worst. Often the defenders of ideal theory concede these points but go on to respond that ideal theory need not be practical to have value. Both sides, however, assume that to have practical value, social ideals must be able to guide political action by serving as long-range goals or targets. I argue that this assumption is false. The point of ideal theory is not to provide guidance but rather to overcome in an ethically responsible manner the practical doubt that arises in a context of reasonable pluralism.

From reinvasion shock to realignment? How context and pragmatism shape far-right voting on Russia

Jakub Wondreys, Neil S. Williams

Full text
This article provides a novel analysis of drivers of the heterogeneity in positions of European far-right parties on Russia. Concentrating on roll-call votes in the European Parliament, we theorize that due to pragmatic constraints, far-right members of European Parliament from larger domestic parties and those in government will be less likely to explicitly support Russia, expectations that are largely supported by our results. Furthermore, given that our data range from a year before the reinvasion of Ukraine in 2022 to more than 2 years after, we chart and test changes in voting over time for far-right members of European Parliament. Our theorizing and results demonstrate how electoral viability and strategic calculations constrain far-right manoeuvrers and position-taking in much the same manner as other types of political parties, therefore adding to our knowledge of far-right behaviour not simply concerning Russia but in other policy areas as well.

The time is now: Why talking to our children about injustice cannot wait

Rosa Terlazzo

Full text
In this article, I argue that white parents have an obligation to have early conversations with their children about racial injustice. First, I appeal to combined considerations of fairness and solidarity. It is unfair that racial injustice exists, that Black parents cannot shield their children from knowledge of that injustice, and that acquiring that knowledge is more painful for Black children. This gives white parents solidarity-based reasons to have early injustice conversations, as a way of engaging in fate sharing with Black parents and of developing their own commitments to racial justice. Second, I argue that early conversations are required to allow white children to fulfill short-term duties to avoid causing unjust harm, avoid risking unjust harm, and stand up against injustice. Finally, I argue that beginning with conversations about justice and saving conversations about injustice for later makes it harder for children to fulfill long-term duties of justice.

Expanding Access, Preserving Judgment: Large Language Models in Social Science Data Collection

Chun-Young Park, Matthew Rains

Full text
What role should large language models (LLMs) play in social science research? The rapid rise of LLMs has generated both excitement and trepidation about their functional and ethical implications. This study evaluates their role in political science data production through the Universal Periodic Review Other Stakeholders’ Engagement (UPROSE) Dataset Project. The UPROSE project uses Universal Periodic Review reports from Non-Governmental Organizations on human rights conditions to build data on human rights and NGO engagement with international organizations. We compare LLM performance to human coders across multiple extraction tasks using established reliability metrics. LLMs perform comparably to humans for well-structured, surface-level variables but fall short in tasks requiring contextual inference or interpretive judgment. These results suggest that LLMs are best used as conditional tools that, when paired with subject-matter expertise, can expand access to high-quality data and reduce structural barriers. We conclude with best practices for hybrid workflows that enhance efficiency while maintaining interpretive quality in social science research.

Strategic divergences: Comparing national approaches to critical raw materials

Andrea Prontera, Claudio C. Passalacqua

Full text
Critical raw materials have become central in policy debates due to their importance for economic growth and national security, as well as the vulnerabilities arising from the geographic concentration of their supply chains. Alongside intensifying geopolitical tensions, these factors have prompted many countries to adopt national strategies aimed at mitigating supply-chain risks or securing a position in the expanding global market. Although these strategies tend to share similar objectives, they differ across several important dimensions, depending on the priorities emphasized by policymakers during their formulation. In this article, we introduce an analytical framework to map and explain such divergences. We apply this framework to examine national strategies on critical raw materials developed in 23 jurisdictions worldwide since 2008. Combining qualitative content analysis with descriptive statistics, we investigate the evolution of these strategies over time and identify the main drivers behind variation in national approaches to critical raw materials governance.

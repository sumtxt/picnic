Mind over Paradigm: Political Psychology in International Relations

Rose McDermott

Full text
Rose McDermott traces the rise of political psychology in international relations and argues that psychological concepts and methods have become central to the study of international security, even as their influence is often overlooked. For the fiftieth anniversary of International Security, McDermott analyzes how, unlike approaches centered primarily on systems, institutions, or states, political psychology emphasizes individual agency, including leaders’ perceptions, emotions, identities, biases, and decision-making. The article surveys how political psychology has reshaped debates over deterrence, signaling, the security dilemma, leadership, intelligence failure, status, reputation, credibility, emotion, and evolutionary behavior. Psychological research shows how misperception can destabilize deterrence, how leaders’ beliefs and personal characteristics influence intervention and escalation, and how status, pride, shame, fear, anger, and overconfidence can intensify conflict. McDermott also highlights newer work that uses experiments, neuroscience, genetics, and evolutionary theory. Political psychology remains theoretically fragmented and limited in predictive power because human behavior is complex, but its pluralism has generated valuable insights and questions for addressing war, cooperation, and global threats.

The Liberal Balance of Power and the Four Horsemen of Its Apocalypse

Jack Snyder

Full text
Jack Snyder argues that liberalism’s historical success rests on four competitive mechanisms: free markets, democratic elections, an open marketplace of ideas, and a distinctively liberal approach to the balance of power. These mechanisms’ effectiveness depends on a “visible hand” of laws, norms, expertise, institutions, and social practices that regulates competition and guards against instability. For the fiftieth anniversary of International Security, Snyder applies this framework to the contemporary crisis of the liberal order and the rise of illiberal nationalism that stems in part from deregulation, economic inequality, political polarization, media fragmentation, and attacks on democratic guardrails. He argues that the post-1945 liberal order worked better than previous illiberal and laissez-faire liberal alternatives because market competition was embedded within welfare states, Bretton Woods institutions, the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, and democratic accountability. In international politics, liberal powers have outperformed authoritarian challengers because liberal institutions enable more effective strategies, alliance cooperation, learning, and self-correction. By contrast, authoritarian “late developers” often respond to economic and security vulnerabilities through self-destructive strategies of militarized autarky and imperial overexpansion. Snyder nevertheless acknowledges liberal overreach, especially in peripheral “forever wars.” He recommends renewing democratic alliances, reforming the defense industrial base, steering clear of peripheral military interventions, rebuilding a constructive media environment, and developing socially embedded liberal institutions capable of addressing contemporary economic and technological problems.

Defending the Liberal International Order

G. John Ikenberry

Full text
Realists accounts of the U.S.-led postwar order cast skepticism on its liberal foundations or functions. G. John Ikenberry offers a different reading of the origins, logic, and character of this order. Theoretically and historically, there is more—not less—to this liberal international order than meets the eye, certainly the realist eye. While not emerging at a single order-building moment or from a unitary strategic vision, it evolved over the decades into arguably the most formidable and dynamic political formation in world history: a far-flung and complex system of economic, political, and security relations binding together the advanced industrial democracies; an order organized around bargains, institutions, and shared values and interests; and an order infused with principles of consent, reciprocity, and mutual gain. Theoretically, it deserves to be seen as a genuinely new type of international order. It is an order that established an array of commitments and restraints that, when combined, offered a sort of “third way” between two extremes: order based on anarchy and the balance of power, and order based on empire and domination.

Shore Thing?: Why the Stopping Power of Water Is Misconceived

Charles Miller

Full text
Charles Miller challenges the conventional wisdom about amphibious landings, arguing that most amphibious landings succeed, and that they are no less likely to succeed than ground offensives. In fact, successful amphibious landings can provide more than a tactical advantage. An amphibious landing can be strategically successful if it ties into a broader plan that reduces the defender’s chances of prevailing in the war. The strategic utility of any military operation lies in its ability to undermine the adversary’s theory of victory, reducing its belief in the likelihood of a favorable outcome if the fighting continues. Amphibious landings can accomplish this in two ways— by gaining access to new land masses that are contiguous with the enemy’s core territory or sit astride key supply lines, or by outflanking the enemy in key theaters. The high-stakes Battle of Saipan during World War II, when the successful U.S. amphibious landings constituted a turning point in the thinking of many Japanese decision-makers about the outcome of the war, provides an example. Important policy issues hinge on this question. The difficulties of amphibious landings are one reason why some analysts argue that a Chinese invasion of Taiwan would be unlikely to succeed.

The Malacca Myth: Why the United States Should Be Wary of a Distant Blockade Against China

Hugo Bromley, Eyck Freymann

Full text
Hugo Bromley and Eyck Freymann argue that the United States should be cautious about threatening or imposing a distant naval blockade against China through the Strait of Malacca. Although such a blockade might restrict China’s oil imports and create short-term economic pressure, its broader political and economic effects could undermine U.S. strategic interests. The authors situate blockades in a broader typology of economic coercion and develop a theory centered on “arbitrage”: blockades create price differences that encourage neutral states and private firms to reroute, reflag, transship, or smuggle goods through or around the blockade. The more economically significant the neutral community, the more costly and difficult enforcement becomes for the blockader. Historical studies of British blockades against France during the revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars and against Germany in the two world wars support this argument. Britain repeatedly had to compromise with neutral powers because aggressive enforcement threatened diplomatic coalitions and sometimes provoked conflict with important states. A Malacca blockade would confront an even larger and more economically integrated neutral community than Britain faced. China could also rely on stockpiles, domestic production, overland routes, substitution, and food reserves to withstand the blockade. The authors conclude that Washington should favor economic measures that encourage cooperation with neutral actors rather than coercive blockade enforcement, which could fracture the global economy and strengthen China diplomatically.

Review of International Organizations

Forum shopping by non-state actors in international organizations

Gino Pauselli, Rachel J. Schoner

Full text
Non-state actors filing human rights complaints can often choose between international organizations. How does institution design shape these choices? We consider strategic forum shopping, arguing that individuals and NGOs select venues based on two key institutional features: whether decisions are legally binding and how long decisions take. We test this argument using original data on over 3,400 petitions filed in the Inter-American Human Rights System (IAHRS) and the UN Human Rights Committee (CCPR) over a period of forty years (1980–2020). We find that when states recognize the jurisdiction of an international court, making legally binding decisions possible, petitions to the IAHRS increase and petitions to the CCPR decrease. The two features trade off differently across actor types: NGOs are willing to tolerate lengthy processes in exchange for the prospect of binding, precedent-setting decisions, while individual victims are more sensitive to decision time and prefer the faster UN system. These findings show that overlapping institutions produce systematic sorting with important implications for the design, resilience, and enforcement capacity of the international human rights regime.

The second Trump administration and the Anglo-American special relationship: A test of alliance persistence

Ruike Xu, Wyn Rees

Full text
The Anglo-American ‘Special Relationship’ has entered a new and uncertain era under the second Trump administration. Drawing on the theoretical framework of alliance persistence, this article analyses how President Trump’s ‘America First’ policies have reshaped the foundations and functions of the UK–US partnership. It argues that the revival of ‘America First’ nationalism has intensified the structural tension between bilateral cooperation and the multilateral commitments that both states share within organisations such as NATO. While the enduring institutionalisation of intelligence sharing, nuclear collaboration and military interoperability continues to anchor the alliance, these aspects have increasingly been conditioned by Washington’s transactional approach to burden-sharing and its scepticism towards multilateral frameworks. The article demonstrates that the second Trump administration’s foreign policy has eroded confidence in the partnership’s multilateral dimension, compelling the United Kingdom to assume greater responsibility for sustaining the special relationship.

Regime penalty and capability dividend: European public support for security cooperation

Caner Şimşek, Faruk Aksoy

Full text
How does a potential partner’s regime type affect European public support for security cooperation and does military technology moderate this effect? We investigate how the European public navigates the conflict between political reliability and strategic utility. Using a pre-registered survey experiment with 15,400 participants across seven European countries, we manipulate the potential partner’s political regime and technological capacity in the context of a missile development project between the European Union and a non-member partner country. Ordinary least squares models show that democratic partners enjoy higher public support than both authoritarian and backsliding regimes, while technological sophistication independently increases support. Backsliding democracies receive more support than autocracies. High technology does not erase the regime penalty. The democracy advantage remains larger than the capability dividend. Structural equation model results suggest that regime effects operate through perceived reliability and perceived reputational cost with the larger indirect path running through trust. We contribute by empirically examining the tension between capacity and regime type in shaping public support.

‘The party of workers’? The class politics of Reform UK candidates

Vladimir Bortun, Aaron Reeves

Full text
Far-right populist parties’ claim to be on the side of ‘the people’ sits at the core of their appeal. Yet, this claim has seen surprisingly little empirical scrutiny. This paper examines Reform UK’s claim to be ‘the party of workers’ by unpacking the classed self-presentations, actual class locations and economic orientations of Reform candidates in the 2024 general election. Based on quantitative and qualitative text analysis, the paper finds a discrepancy across all three levels. First , to position themselves as credible alternatives to established elites, candidates emphasise their upward mobility rather than working-classness. Second , their actual class profiles reveal a high share of people from professional-managerial and petty bourgeois backgrounds. Third , their economic positions are more consistently pro-business than pro-worker. Together, these findings cast doubt on Reform’s claim to represent workers and highlight the need for scholars of far-right populism to focus more on the class politics of party elites.

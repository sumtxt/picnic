A natural field experiment on the effectiveness of economic sanctions before and after the Russian invasion of Ukraine

Michael G. Findley, Daniel L. Nielson, J. C. Sharman

Full text
Here, using two rounds of natural field experiments based on an audit-study approach, we investigated whether sanctioned Russian government officials retain access to the international financial system. Researchers sent 7,081 email solicitations to intermediary firms in 202 countries and jurisdictions to establish shell companies and set up corporate bank accounts. Responses to sanctioned names (middle initial modified) are compared with equivalent solicitations from non-sanctioned placebo names. Round 1 results in December 2020 reveal no statistically significant difference in financial access for the sanctioned names compared with placebo. Round 2 findings in May 2022, after the Russian invasion of Ukraine, indicate that sanctioned individuals’ access to international corporate services and banking was significantly reduced (drop in non-compliant response rates: 4.0 percentage points, 95% confidence interval 1.2–6.8, P = 0.006, Cohen’s d = 0.207). The results suggest that factors related to the war and not sanctions law proved decisive in constricting sanctioned individuals’ access to the global financial system.

Evidence and measures of the digital usage divide from 40 million Windows devices

Mayana Pereira, Shane Greenstein, Raffaella Sadun, Prasanna Tambe, Lucia Ronchi Darre, Tammy Glazer, Allen Kim, Rahul Dodhia, Juan Lavista Ferres

Full text
Digital infrastructure is crucial for economic opportunities, social inclusion, and civic participation, but benefits remain unevenly distributed. Existing measures, such as computer ownership and broadband adoption, do not capture the depth and variety of digital use. We develop measures using Microsoft telemetry collected during operating system updates from 40 million Windows devices in U.S. households. These measures provide greater coverage and granularity than existing commercial or government surveys. Device representations at the ZIP-code level reveal substantial variation in usage, including an urban-rural divide and disparities within narrowly defined Metropolitan Statistical Areas. Income and education correlate with these differences. These effects are large and suggest that digital usage gaps extend beyond the local availability of essential IT infrastructure. The findings call for interventions that complement infrastructure access with support for technology use and skills development. The resulting indices are publicly available to support future research on digital inequality.

Temporal dynamics of premenstrual dysphoric disorder revealed through predictive symptom networks

Ella Schleimann-Jensen, Inger Sundström-Poromaa, Johanna M. Hoppe, Sinha Engel, Jeff Kiesner, Erika Comasco

Full text
Temporal dynamics of psychiatric symptoms challenge researchers and clinicians due to their inherent unpredictability. The cyclical on–off nature of symptoms linked to physiological rhythms, such as the menstrual cycle, provides a unique window to apply temporal and dynamic modeling of psychiatric symptoms. This can be studied in premenstrual dysphoric disorder, a female hormone-triggered condition characterized by cyclical symptoms. Yet intra-individual temporal patterns and dynamics of these symptoms remain unknown. Here we show that individuals with premenstrual dysphoric disorder differ widely in symptom timing, severity, and persistence, clustering into distinct temporal symptom profiles. Dynamic modelling uncovers temporal order among core psychiatric symptoms, with anxiety emerging as a central symptom in the unfolding of affective dysregulation. Depression shows the strongest predictive associations with subsequent severity of the other core symptoms, while irritability predicts the severity of depression at the longest time-interval. These temporal symptom dynamics reveal mechanistic psychoneuroendocrine pathways and inform precision, time-sensitive interventions for the mental health of individuals with premenstrual dysphoric disorder.

Zoe Renee Finiasz, Tamar Kushnir

Full text
Shared beliefs can bring people together but may motivate prioritizing group affiliation over facts supported by evidence. Indeed, affiliation with a group whose membership is tied to shared beliefs (i.e., ideology-based groups) has led adults to endorse and maintain false beliefs. We demonstrate that this resistance to revise beliefs that form the basis of group membership has its origins in childhood. In three studies, 4- to 5-y-old children ( N = 192 ) endorse a claim about a novel causal system and subsequently join a group that shares their endorsement. Children randomly assigned to a focal Belief-contingent condition learn that their membership in the group is based on this claim, framed as a shared belief. Only when group membership was contingent on the shared belief did children hold on to (and act on—Experiment 3) their initial incorrect endorsement. These findings suggest that the affiliative power of ideology has developmental roots; even in our earliest years, affiliative motives override truth-seeking and rationality. Thus, our early-emerging motive to identify with groups may underlie later cases of evidence denial and polarization.

Multimodal Czech online handwriting and cognitive data from children with and without handwriting disabilities

K. Zvončáková, J. Mekyska, A. Klocek, J. Mucha, Z. Galáž

Full text
Handwriting is a fundamental skill that significantly influences academic achievement and cognitive development. Despite its importance, open-access datasets combining online handwriting data with comprehensive cognitive profiles are scarce, particularly for children with handwriting disabilities. We present a multimodal dataset consisting of online handwriting and cognitive assessment data from 276 Czech children (ages 8–12), including 161 individuals with handwriting disabilities. The data acquisition utilized a Wacom Cintiq 16 tablet and a digital pen to record 16 graphomotor and writing tasks, capturing high-resolution x-y coordinates, pressure, azimuth, and pen tilt at an average frequency of 167 Hz. Alongside these raw data, the dataset includes scores from standardized assessments of cognitive abilities, visuospatial skills, phonological awareness, and self-reported handwriting proficiency. We provide raw data in SVC format and integrated, preprocessed records in JSON and RDS formats, supported by specialized Python and R processing scripts. This resource is intended to facilitate research in graphonomics, machine learning, and educational psychology, providing a benchmark for the development and comparison of computational methods for analyzing children’s handwriting.

Big Tech’s power resources: four eras of corporate influence in the EU

Matti Ylönen, Laura Nordström

Full text
The political clout of Big Tech firms has gained growing attention, as the tech industry has emerged as the largest lobbying force in Brussels. Drawing on a case study based on an extensive round of interviews in Brussels and other research, we argue that Big Tech’s penetration of European politics has proceeded in four overlapping yet distinct phases: harnessing an economic style of reasoning (1990s–2000s), leveraging platform power (2000s–2010s), championing European values (2010s–2020s), and exploiting dependence on American digital infrastructures (late 2020s). Advancing recent theoretical and empirical literature on this front, we show how each period relies on a distinct bundle of power resources. At one end are conventional lobbying techniques and a push for a deregulatory hegemony, while at the other are lobbying and hegemony-building strategies that utilize digital power resources. The findings invite a reassessment of established theories in this field. Policy implications also abound.

Income inequality, individual income, trust in government, and long-term individual concerns

Juan J Fernández, Tobias Wiß, Hayley James

Full text
Humans frequently worry about their future, yet most research focuses on only one area of concern, limiting our understanding of general long-term worry. This manuscript addresses this gap through a cross-national analysis of 27 high-income societies. Using representative surveys conducted in 2022 and 2024 and multilevel models, we examine seven domains of worry spanning financial security, housing, and health. We identify two underlying forms of concern—financial and health-related—and argue that their prevalence is linked to country conditions. In countries with higher inequality and lower trust in government, citizens report higher levels of long-term concern overall. We attribute the role of income inequality in relation to worry to the influence of perceived instability and status-maintenance anxiety, and the role of trust in government to the presence of credible institutions. Policies that reduce inequality and enhance trust in government may mitigate material insecurity and psychological burden about the future.

When Insurers Exit: Climate Losses, Fragile Insurers, and Mortgage Markets

Parinitha Sastry, Ishita Sen, Ana-Maria Tenekedjieva

Full text
This paper uses a range of natural experiments to show how mispricing of climate risk in mortgages and property insurance creates large taxpayer exposures and leads to excess credit flows to risky areas. Our central finding is that the government-sponsored enterprises’ (GSEs) policies for evaluating property insurers are a crucial source of this mispricing. We assemble a comprehensive new dataset on both mortgages and insurance to analyze these dynamics in Florida from 2009-2018. We begin by documenting a breakdown in the quality of insurance provision, with new under-capitalized and under-diversified insurers dominating insurance markets. These fragile insurers have high rates of insolvency, which we show causally increases mortgage defaults after natural disasters. We find that the GSEs’ reliance on third-party ratings of insurers leads them to accept insurance from companies at high risk of insolvency without pricing for it. This creates large taxpayer exposures, with an estimated 25% of the GSEs’ expected losses in Florida coming from insurance fragility. Most starkly, we show that private lenders strategically respond to the GSE mispricing. Mortgage denial rates are sensitive to insurance quality in the jumbo segment where loans are retained, but not in the conforming segment where loans are offloaded to the GSEs. This suggests that, all else equal, fewer conforming mortgages would have been originated if lenders screened insurance fragility as they do in jumbos.

Channeled Attention and Stable Errors

Tristan Gagnon-Bartsch, Matthew Rabin, Joshua Schwartzstein

Full text
People persist in mistaken beliefs even in settings with high stakes, abundant data, and repeated opportunities to learn—settings where standard arguments imply they should correct their errors. We develop an attention-based framework for understanding why errors persist. When a person channels his attention through the lens of his potentially misspecified model, exposure to data is not the same as tracking the statistics necessary to tell him his model is wrong. A model is attentionally stable when the data the person deems relevant will, in the long run, fail to make the model look implausible. Models that erroneously rule things out—e.g., beliefs that a variable doesn’t matter or that a distinction doesn’t exist—tend to be attentionally stable because they divert attention away from data that would refute the models. Conversely, models that erroneously rule things in are less likely to be stable. Whether an error is discovered depends on the structure of the decision problem but is not tightly linked to its level of harm; arbitrarily costly errors can persist while trivially inexpensive ones may be detected. Nevertheless, no error is robust both to changes in the decision environment and to shocks that expand attention: every channeled-attention error is vulnerable to some perturbation that would reveal it. We use the framework to analyze the stability of psychological biases and empirical misconceptions, as well as to explain why fresh eyes catch mistakes, why people use overly coarse rather than overly fine categorizations, and why people recognize errors in others that they miss in themselves.

Labor Market Tightness and the Reversal of Inequality Trends in Germany

Christian Dustmann, Martin Friedrich, Carl Gergs, Uta Schönberg

Full text
Germany experienced a sharp rise in wage inequality over the two decades leading up to the Great Recession. However, a remarkable trend reversal occurred in 2010—well before the introduction of the minimum wage in 2015—when wages at the bottom of the distribution began to grow more rapidly than those higher up. This paper attributes the reversal to an increasingly tight labor market after the Great Recession, supported by strong domestic demand, and operating through several interrelated and mutually reinforcing channels. Consistent with this hypothesis, job-to-job transition rates and between-job wage growth rose markedly after the recession, especially for workers at the lower rungs of the job ladder. Concurrently, workers increasingly moved from low-paying to higher-paying firms, compressing the worker-weighted distribution of firm wage premia. Tighter labor markets also reduced assortative matching between workers and firms, lowered the skill premium, and weakened firms’ monopsony power.

Review of Economic Studies

Competition in Markups: A Theory of the Lerner Index in Networks and General Equilibrium

Lingxuan Wu

Full text
What determines demand elasticities and markups in production networks, where firms' demand and pricing are interdependent? I propose competition in markups, an equilibrium concept in which firms choose markups, taking other firms' markups as given. In partial equilibrium with vertical structures, it links upstream demand elasticities to downstream characteristics without backward induction and ties pass-through to the curvature of final demand. In general equilibrium with arbitrary industry networks, it microfounds firms' demand elasticities and markups through their within-industry market shares and four industry-level network statistics---cycle, substitution in production, substitution in consumption, and consumer-price impact. Empirical analysis of Compustat firms from 1997 to 2019 confirms the predicted variation of industry demand elasticities and firm markups. Relative to an exogenous-markup benchmark, endogenous markups dampen the aggregate TFP impact of a productivity shock to an industry's largest firm by 6% for the median industry, and by up to 48% across industries.

Optimal Policy Rules in HANK

Alisdair McKay, Christian K Wolf

Full text
We characterize optimal policy rules in a business-cycle model with nominal rigidities and heterogeneous households. The policymaker has access to two instruments: the short-term nominal rate, and lump-sum transfer payments. A policymaker with a traditional dual mandate that targets aggregates uses the same policy rule as is optimal in the textbook New Keynesian model. We then consider a policymaker with a distributional objective. The optimal policy rule now contains an additional term, reflecting the effects of the policymaker’s instruments on consumption inequality. Since, in our model, monetary policy only has very limited distributional effects, this additional term does not materially change optimal monetary policy. Fiscal stimulus payments, on the other hand, have strongly progressive effects and are thus well-suited to cushion the distributional effects of cyclical fluctuations.

Fertility and Family Leave Policies in Germany: Optimal Policy Design in a Dynamic Framework

Hanna Wang

Full text
I develop and estimate a life-cycle discrete-choice model of fertility and female labour supply to study the optimal design of a range of child-related policies. First, I examine two German reforms that introduced wage-contingent parental leave payments and expanded access to low-cost public childcare. I find that both reforms raised completed fertility, with the parental leave reform having a particularly strong impact on highly educated women. Second, I solve for a budget-neutral optimal policy portfolio that maximizes either aggregate welfare or fertility, while ensuring that welfare and fertility do not decline for any education group. I consider four prominent child subsidies as well as the degree of tax jointness. My results show that optimal policy has the potential to increase welfare by 0.5% or fertility by 5.7%. While the solutions are qualitatively similar, they prioritize different policy instruments depending on the specific objective being targeted.

The Why and How of Foreign Aid

Dean Karlan

Full text
Foreign aid has become a melting pot of motivations and policies, where differently motivated actors often converge on recommended policies while the similarly motivated diverge. Breaking down the “why” and the “how” can illuminate real tensions and reveal false ones. The “why” asks which problems to tackle and who benefits: the recipient, the donor through economic, political, or soft-power returns, or everyone through global public goods. The “how” asks which strategy most cost-effectively addresses a chosen problem. Evidence is central to the “how”: Use it where it exists, produce it where it does not. In particular, approaches that improve state capacity to use and generate evidence can satisfy multiple objectives: strengthen recipient countries, benefit donors, and provide a global public good of knowledge. Ultimately, the “how” is not a linear process, but rather an iterative one of learning, adopting, adapting, repeating, and then sharing so others can learn as well.

The Role of Private Debt in the Financial Ecosystem

Victoria Ivashina

Full text
This paper provides an introduction to the modern private debt market and examines its rapid expansion following the 2007–2009 global financial crisis. Traditionally, economists distinguished between public debt markets and private debt in the form of bank lending. In contemporary markets, however, “private debt” refers to credit originated and held by nonbank financial intermediaries, including private credit funds and business development companies. The paper reviews the key drivers of the sector’s growth; analyzes the distinctive features of private lending arrangements and funding structures; and discusses the implications for traditional banking, financial intermediation, and financial stability. While the sector’s rapid growth and increasing complexity warrant careful monitoring, the paper argues that private debt represents an important and likely durable component of modern financial markets.

Trust Violations in Research Universities

William G. Howell

Full text
By nearly twice as much as in any other institution that Gallup tracks, public trust in research universities has fallen over the past decade. The causes are not only external. This essay examines two violations of trust that originate within the academy: research that is driven by political advocacy, and a professoriate whose range of ideological viewpoints has narrowed, especially in the humanities and social sciences. The first subordinates the pursuit of truth to political aims; the second erodes the diversity of perspectives that checks individual biases and lets research correct itself. Both break faith with the arguments originally advanced for tenure and academic freedom. Restoring trust demands a renewed commitment to disinterested inquiry and the cultivation of intellectual pluralism—objectives, both, that are best adopted voluntarily, without state censor or interference, and promptly.

The Meritocratic Consensus and Stratification in Higher Education

Zachary Bleemer, Jesse Rothstein

Full text
American colleges and universities are highly stratified by pre-college academic achievement, family background, and institutional resources. We study the meritocratic consensus in American higher education: Colleges that high-testing students (who are generally also from high-income families) attend spend dramatically more on instruction than do those that enroll lower-testing students. Stratification by test scores has been largely stable since the 1960s, but the stratification of instructional resources has risen sharply since 1970 at both private and public institutions. Nonacademic admissions criteria like athletics, legacy, and affirmative action are second-order in determining the allocation of students to universities. Potential economic justifications for the positive association of instructional expenditures with student prior achievement—q-complementarity between achievement and resources, convex social returns to high human capital, and incentives to invest in learning prior to college—have little empirical support. Resource stratification across universities has not increased in the past decade, largely due to increased public funding of universities that enroll lower-testing students through financial aid programs like California's CalGrant, but stratification within institutions is now rising swiftly.

Can Government Support for Innovation Create the Next Silicon Valley?

E. Jason Baron, Shawn Kantor, Alexander Whalley

Full text
Can government support for innovation create the next Silicon Valley? We examine what public research and development can and cannot do to foster regional knowledge hubs. The historical record suggests that many successful hubs owe their origins partly to serendipity and favorable initial conditions. We then review causal evidence from universities, changes in intellectual property rules, new research institutions, wartime R&D, and the space race. Public R&D can raise local innovation, employment, and income, but the effects are heterogeneous, often modest relative to cost, and may partly reflect the relocation of firms and skilled workers from elsewhere. The evidence suggests a central role for complementary local inputs, including entrepreneurs, risk capital, skilled labor, firms, and institutions. Public R&D can help build a hub, but is unlikely to create one on its own.

Private Credit, Balance Sheets, and Financial Stability

Gregor Matvos, Tomasz Piskorski, Amit Seru

Full text
Private credit has become an important source of business financing, raising questions about financial stability. We examine these questions using comprehensive fund- and asset-level data on private credit funds, the dominant form of private credit. Four facts emerge. First, private credit funds are primarily equity financed, with equity accounting for 65–80 percent of assets, compared with about 10 percent for US banks. Second, they engage in little maturity transformation: Fund lives span eight to twelve years, while underlying loans typically mature within two to four years. Third, portfolios are diversified across industries, geographies, and credit strategies. Fourth, equity investors have earned average net annualized returns of about 10 percent after fees, with equity cushions historically absorbing losses before creditors. These findings suggest that private credit funds do not currently replicate balance-sheet structures associated with banking fragility. We conclude by discussing potential vulnerabilities related to liquidity provision, valuation contagion, and loss transmission through investor balance sheets.

The Role of Organization in Firm-Sponsored Training

David J. Deming, Raffaella Sadun, Christopher Stanton

Full text
Some firms are more likely than others to invest in workers’ skills. We document this phenomenon and discuss its implications in light of models of firm-sponsored skills training. The data show that better managed firms, even in the same industries and labor markets, are more likely to train, while training clusters around either managerial or frontline worker development. We argue that organizational differences provide incentives for firms to train beyond traditional explanations that focus on labor market frictions. We illustrate how organizational heterogeneity can generate training investments through a knowledge hierarchy framework where top managers with different skills build bespoke organizations tailored to their unique needs. This, in turn, makes general skill firm specific, allowing firms to capture returns on training investment.

Systemic Cyber Risk: Linking Measurement, Market Incentives, and Policy

Susan Athey, Neil Gandal, Tyler Moore

Full text
Many firms and sectors within the global economy heavily depend on a common set of critical technology products and software services, thereby consolidating risk across a small number of potential failure points. The widespread reliance on a limited set of products and services has given rise to many single points of system vulnerability. Such concentration of risk amplifies the potential fallout from security breaches, especially since attackers have strong incentives to attack such products and services. Despite the abundance of tools available to organizations and consumers for safeguarding their data against loss or compromise, advancements in individual defense measures have been more than counterbalanced by an increased susceptibility to systemic events. In this paper, we apply economic principles to the rising danger of systemic cyber risks. The paper is organized around four central economic lessons and uses case studies, new data, and policy discussion to develop and support them.

In Praise of the Bayesian Bootstrap

Isaiah Andrews, Sarah Moon, Jesse M. Shapiro

Full text
The key takeaways from this article will be the following: (i) Anytime you can bootstrap, you should! (ii) Use the Bayesian bootstrap instead of the nonparametric bootstrap. (iii) If the bootstrap distribution looks different from the “theoretical” (usually, normal) distribution, don’t (only) report a point estimate and standard error. (iv) Instead (or in addition), report the bootstrap distribution with a plot if you have space for it or an interval if you don’t. (v) Give the Bayesian bootstrap a Bayesian interpretation. (vi) You can use a bootstrap even with (multiway) clustering.

Worker‐side discrimination: Beliefs and preferences

Md Moshi Ul Alam, Mehreen Mookerjee, Sanket Roy

Full text
When agents choose among options with unobserved attributes to maximize expected utility, their choices conflate preferences with beliefs about those attributes. We develop a novel theory‐driven identification strategy for such settings, showing that compensating differentials separately identify beliefs from preferences. Noting that workers' preferences and beliefs shape labor market outcomes, yet remain understudied, we apply our method to provide the first evidence on the distribution of workers' preferences over manager gender, and their beliefs about managers' mentoring ability. In the absence of information on manager mentoring ability, workers are indifferent to manager gender. However, upon receiving information on manager mentorship ability, workers prefer to work for female managers, willing to forgo 1.7% of average annual wages. Hence, absent additional information, workers believe female managers are worse mentors (1.6% wage equivalent). Nonparametric estimates of the distributions reveal that 76% prefer female managers with mentoring information, and 67% believe male managers are better mentors absent information. Machine learning algorithms show that individuals with higher education are less likely to hold such beliefs. These beliefs are primarily driven by perceptions that women are less competent. Evidence suggests these beliefs are biased, highlighting scope for information‐based policy interventions.

Finite‐state Markov chain approximations: A hidden Markov approach

Eva F. Janssens, Sean McCrary

Full text
This paper proposes a novel finite‐state Markov chain approximation method for Markov processes with continuous support, providing both an optimal grid and a transition probability matrix. The method can be used for multivariate processes and processes with time‐varying components. It is based on minimizing the information loss between a hidden Markov model and the true data‐generating process. We provide sufficient conditions under which this information loss can be made arbitrarily small, provided enough grid points are used. We compare our method to existing methods through the lens of an asset‐pricing model and a life‐cycle consumption‐savings model. We find that our method can lead to more parsimonious discretizations and more accurate solutions, and that the discretization matters for the welfare costs of risk, marginal propensities to consume, and the amount of wealth inequality a life‐cycle model can generate.

Intrahousehold inequality and savings in retirement

Minsu Chang, Ami Ko

Full text
The distribution of resources between spouses and the extent to which household savings reflect each spouse's motives are fundamental to analyzing married retirees' welfare. We provide one of the first quantitative assessments of intrahousehold inequality in bargaining power and its implications for consumption allocation during marriage, savings, and welfare in old age. To do so, we estimate a dynamic collective model of retired couples with individual‐specific preferences and relative bargaining power, in which spouses jointly choose consumption, saving, care arrangements, and housing in the face of health and mortality shocks. We find that wives' mean consumption shares are close to parity in retirement, although there is substantial dispersion. We also find meaningful gender differences in saving motives and preferences: wives have stronger precautionary motives due to their longer life expectancy and higher long‐term care risk, while men exhibit greater bequest motives toward children. Consequently, our estimated collective model shows that reforms to old‐age social insurance have different welfare effects for wives and husbands, revealing distributional consequences that would be obscured under a unitary framework.

Fertility, child gender, and parental migration decision: Evidence from China

Zibin Huang, Lin Lin, Junsen Zhang

Full text
We study how the number and gender of children affect rural‐to‐urban parental migration in China. Exploiting variation from the “1.5‐child policy,” which allowed a second birth only if the first‐born was a girl, we separately identify the causal effects of fertility and child gender based on a novel semiparametric method. Using the 2010 population census, we find that an additional child increases the likelihood of joint parental migration by 12.1%, while the presence of a son raises it by a further 11.6%. These comparable magnitudes show that gender matters as much as fertility in shaping migration decisions. The results suggest that the higher financial demands of larger families—especially those with boys—outweigh the emotional costs of separation, motivating parents to migrate. Traditional IV estimates would be severely downward biased if only the number of children were considered.

A nonparametric test for diurnal variation in spot correlation processes

Kim Christensen, Ulrich Hounyo, Zhi Liu

Full text
The association between log‐price increments of exchange‐traded equities, as measured by their spot correlation estimated from high‐frequency data, exhibits a pronounced upward‐sloping and almost piecewise linear relationship at the intraday horizon. There is notably lower—on average less positive—correlation in the morning than in the afternoon. We develop a nonparametric testing procedure to detect such variation in a correlation process. The test statistic has a known distribution under the null hypothesis, whereas it diverges under the alternative. We run a Monte Carlo simulation to discover the finite sample properties of the test statistic, which are close to the large sample predictions, even for small sample sizes and realistic levels of diurnal variation. In an application, we implement the test on a high‐frequency dataset covering the stock market over an extended period. The test leads to rejection of the null most of the time. This suggests diurnal variation in the correlation process is a nontrivial effect in practice. We show how conditioning information about macroeconomic news and corporate earnings announcements affects the intraday correlation curve.

Finite‐sample auction inference using transaction prices

David M. Kaplan, Xin Liu

Full text
We first provide inference on the quantile function of the bid distribution in first‐price or descending auctions, and the quantile function of the value distribution in second‐price or ascending auctions, with a known number of bidders with symmetric independent private values. Specifically, we provide finite‐sample, nonparametric, tuning parameter‐free, uniform confidence bands for these quantile functions when observing only the transaction price. We also show how these bands can be used to construct confidence intervals for many counterfactuals and economic quantities that are functionals of the value distribution. Some confidence intervals inherit the same properties of our bands, besides having conservative instead of exact coverage, while others require asymptotic arguments and/or tuning parameters. Even if the observed number of bidders varies across auctions, our bands' finite‐sample coverage is exact, assuming exogenous participation. With a fixed number of bidders, we also derive uniform confidence bands robust to auction‐level unobserved heterogeneity. This includes new partial identification results that bound the bid or value quantile function in terms of the transaction price quantile function. Empirically, our new methodology is applied to timber auction data to examine heterogeneity across appraisal value and number of bidders, which helps assess the combination of symmetric independent private values and exogenous participation, and to provide confidence intervals for the optimal reserve price and the economic benefit of running the auction instead of a random lottery.

School choice, skill measures, and graduation

María Elena Ortega-Hesles, Marco Pariguana

Full text
This paper studies the effects of combining skill measures to construct the priority order in a centralized education market. We use data from Mexico City, where seat rationing relies solely on a one‐shot exam score. We first show that admission to the most oversubscribed schools decreases graduation among marginally admitted students, but this effect is heterogeneous. It decreases with the one‐shot exam score among inframarginal students and is negative for marginal students with low GPAs but null for those with high GPAs. We then use a model of school choice and graduation to study the equity and efficiency of counterfactual priority orders that combine the one‐shot exam score and GPA. Greater weight on GPA increases access to the most oversubscribed schools for girls and low‐SES students. However, assigning roughly equal weight to both skill measures maximizes their graduation rates at these schools.

Decomposing the rise in markups

Has Vlokhoven

Full text
A recent literature argues that the rise in aggregate markups is due to economic activity reallocating toward high‐markup firms rather than an increase in firm‐level markups. I show that standard decomposition methods used to analyze this phenomenon can estimate a nonzero reallocation effect even when the joint distribution of markups and market shares is time‐invariant. This is due to churning in the firm‐level distribution of markups and market shares. Empirically, I find that a large and positive reallocation effect is found by standard decomposition methods when the distribution is time‐invariant. I propose a new decomposition, which gives that the rise in the aggregate markup mainly comes from changes in the markup distribution rather than changes in the market share distribution.

Standard errors when a regressor is randomly assigned

Denis Chetverikov, Jinyong Hahn, Zhipeng Liao, Andres Santos

Full text
We show that the OLS variance formula simplifies when values of the regressor of interest are assigned randomly and independently of other regressors. When the regressor of interest is independent not only of other regressors but also of the error term, the homoscedastic variance formula is valid even if regression errors are correlated. When the error term is heteroscedastic with respect to the regressor of interest, the variance formula has to be adjusted not only for heteroscedasticity but also for correlation structure of the error term. However, even in the latter case, some simplifications are possible.

Identification of daily oil price shocks and their uncertainties via conditional heteroskedasticity

Shu Wang

Full text
This paper develops a daily structural VAR for identifying oil price shocks from conditional heteroskedasticity and quantifies the transmission of their time‐varying uncertainty. Identification exploits volatility clustering and spillovers in a structural GARCH specification and the complete identified set is derived. We derive covariance impulse response functions and introduce a time‐varying forecast error variance decomposition (TV‐FEVD) that allows for a decomposition of short‐horizon forecast uncertainty across episodes. We study the asymptotic properties of a two‐step quasi‐maximum likelihood estimator and demonstrate via extensive simulations the robustness of the estimator across a wide range of data‐generating distributions. Empirically, the model identifies three daily shocks with distinct first‐ and second‐order transmission. TV‐FEVD shows that oil‐price forecast uncertainty in early 2020 shifted from aggregate business‐cycle uncertainty, as pandemic news affected expected economic conditions, to oil‐market tightness risk during the Saudi–Russia price war and production cuts.

Tilting at Windmills: Evidence on Momentum and Hot Hands

Adrian Poignant

Full text
Successes and failures often appear to propagate. According to one common belief, the experience of winning or losing can alter the cognitive and emotional state of performers, generating a psychological momentum that helps or harms subsequent performance. This belief is often used to rationalize the so-called hot-hand phenomenon, and has been applied to contexts ranging from horseshoe pitching to stock market trading. However, the existence of a causal effect of winning on subsequent performance remains debated. I test whether wins cause more wins (and losses more losses) using a large natural experiment involving almost a quarter billion online chess games. I find that performance is serially correlated, consistent with the existence of hot hands. Surprisingly, however, I find no evidence of causal effects. Players that were given a small random advantage in one game have no advantage in the next. The null effect is precisely estimated, providing credible evidence against the hypothesis that success breeds success in online chess.

Happy Pills? The Health Consequences of the Dramatic Increase in Antidepressant Use

Giuliano Masiero, Fabrizio Mazzonna, Sandro Steinbach

Full text
This paper examines the health consequences of the rapid increase in antidepressant consumption in Switzerland (2002–2014). Using a shift-share instrumental variable approach, we exploit regional variation in sales driven by product innovation (the shift) and the market power of pharmaceutical firms (the share). Our findings reveal that a one defined daily dose per 1,000 inhabitants increase in antidepressant sales raises hospital admissions for adverse drug reactions by 18% and for depression by 6%. Financial incentives for self-dispensing doctors and reduced access to psychotherapy emerge as key drivers, highlighting concerns about overtreatment and suboptimal prescribing practices.

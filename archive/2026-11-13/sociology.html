Joint Land Titling and the Marital Bond in Southwestern Uganda

Michelle Poulin, Rachael S. Pierotti

Full text
When a randomized intervention in southwestern Uganda offered fully subsidized land titles to agricultural households, the majority of husbands chose to add their wives’ names to the title, a striking result given the persistent gender asymmetry in land ownership across Africa. This study investigates the social dynamics underlying that result, asking: What does a joint land title mean for couples in practice, and do women benefit as intended? We draw on fieldwork observations, conversational interviews with selected couples, and interviews with community leaders. Our findings indicate that women's marital bargaining power increases, consistent with household bargaining models. Critically, we identify an additional channel: a joint title symbolizes a husband's commitment to his spouse and marriage. This symbolic dimension is crucial, as women's property rights are threatened under marital insecurity. Joint titling thus shapes marital dynamics not only by strengthening women's bargaining power but also by encouraging what we term “cooperative power”—a fortified marital bond that inspires investment in the marriage. By applying ethnographic approaches to numerical outcomes, the study shows how qualitative inquiry complements survey research in examining the meanings, motivations, and social logics underlying observed demographic and economic behaviors, offering insight into family processes in contemporary Africa.

Parental Migration During Childhood and Later‐Life Major Depressive Disorder

Emma Labovitz, Emma Banchoff

Full text
The rise of migration in many communities across the Global South raises questions about its consequences for the children of migrants. Previous studies have primarily examined the immediate impact of parental migration on children's well‐being, while its cumulative effects across the life course remain largely unknown. Drawing on retrospective life histories, prospective monthly residency data, and novel diagnostic measures of mental disorders from the longitudinal Chitwan Valley Family Study in Nepal, this study examines the relationship between parental migration during childhood and later‐life onset of major depressive disorder (MDD). Results indicate that parental migration during childhood is associated with a reduced risk of developing MDD across the life course, although this benefit is exclusive to the younger cohort. These findings suggest that in a context where migration is prolific, parental migration may serve as a protective force against adverse mental health outcomes in later adolescence and adulthood, thus revealing a potential source of long‐term inequality in high out‐migration countries such as Nepal.

Assessing the Quality of the Family Interrelationship Variable in Classificatory Kinship Populations

Aurélien Dasré

Full text
The family interrelationship variable is widely used to study household structure, child fostering, and intergenerational support. Yet its measurement validity has not been assessed in populations with classificatory kinship systems—prevalent across most of sub‐Saharan Africa—where kinship terminology groups relatives by generation rather than by direct biological link. This study provides the first empirical assessment of this variable in a West African classificatory context. Using genealogical data from the SLAM (Suivi Longitudinal au Mali ‐ Longitudinal survey in Mali) survey in rural Mali, linked at the individual level to the 2009 national census, we compare census‐reported relationships to the household head with genealogically reconstructed ties among the Bwa, who follow a classificatory terminology. The results reveal systematic and directional discrepancies: nephews, nieces, and stepchildren are frequently reported as biological children of the household head. These findings highlight how the imposition of Western nuclear‐family categories in classificatory kinship settings produces nonrandom biases, leading to inflated counts of household head's children and underestimation of extended family ties.

A Durkheimian Theory Against Empire: Central Australian Festivals and the Making of the Modern World

Romulo Lelis

Full text
It has long been assumed that Durkheimian sociology helped create a sharp distinction between traditional and modern societies, rooted in a Eurocentric bias that treats the latter as a break with the former. However, a close examination of Durkheim’s work may challenge this assumption. Through an in-depth analysis of Durkheim’s posthumously published lectures alongside Les Formes Élémentaires de la Vie Religieuse , this article shows how Durkheim applied the model he developed for studying Aboriginal Australians to a counter-imperial reevaluation of European history. In examining Durkheim’s writings on the genesis of private property in Ancient Rome, the Crusades in the Middle Ages, the French Revolution, and the role of mythological truths in the Modern Era, the article advances a Durkheimian theory against empire, an approach that examines modern society through a theoretical lens drawn from studying Indigenous societies.

Palestinian Sumud: Toward a Southern Theory of Unsettled Times

Fauzia Husain

Full text
Prominent sociological theories of action treat stability as the default and disruption as episodic. Yet for many marginalized communities, unsettledness is endemic. I intervene by “making strange,” a two-pronged strategy that shifts theory’s vantage point to destabilize what the center has naturalized. The first move examines the temporal occlusions that give extant theories of action their appearance of universality. The second centers Palestinian sumud (steadfastness) and Southern and postcolonial approaches to remake theories of action and time. Where extant accounts anticipate hysteresis, sumud reveals an ongoing effort to contest a temporal order designed to fragment and deplete. Because time is not a neutral medium for experience, habitus emerges as a vehicle for temporal intervention rather than merely a repository of accumulated dispositions. The result is a decolonial theory of action in which colonial power structures time without fully determining it.

Net Migration By Age: The Flow-Difference Approach for Estimation and Forecasting

Hana Ševčíková, James Raymer, Adrian E. Raftery

Full text
Highlights We present the flow-difference method to disaggregate total net migration by age. It models the age-specific flows of in-migration and out-migration to derive age-specific net migration, even in the absence of data on such flows. We show that the resulting net migration predictions are greatly improved over existing methods that model only the net migration totals.

The Early Origins of Biological Aging: Maternal Income, Education, and DNA Methylation Patterns at Ages 9–15

Beza Taddess

Full text
Highlights Maternal income at birth predicts children's epigenetic aging more strongly than later income. Children of college-educated mothers show lower epigenetic age acceleration at ages 9 and 15. Socioeconomic gaps differ by epigenetic clock: PhenoAge attenuates, Dunedin-PACE holds, and GrimAge grows by 15. Girls with faster epigenetic aging at age 9 experience menarche earlier. Higher GrimAge at age 9 predicts slower height growth between 9 and 15.

When It Rains: The Dual Impact of Rainfall on Child Survival in Rural Senegal

Hamidou Diallo, Anne-Sophie Robilliard

Full text
Highlights We analyze how rainfall anomalies affect child survival in semiarid rural Senegal. The study uses 50,000 child records over four decades from the Niakhar HDSS, the oldest in Africa. Past rain shortfalls raise child mortality while current rainfall shocks matter less. Rainfall seasonality shapes children’s vulnerability to rainfall anomalies. Food scarcity and disease exposure jointly drive these mortality effects.

Racial Inequalities in Health and Social Policy: Examining the Relationship Between Medicaid Spending and Self-assessed Health Among Poor Single Mothers

Amie Bostic

Full text
Highlights Greater average state Medicaid spending is associated with better self-rated health for poor single mothers. This relationship is stronger for poor, Black single mothers than poor, White or Latina single mothers. Spending variation by state generates substantial spatial inequalities in health. States with larger Black populations spend less, creating a mismatch between effectiveness and implementation.

Racial Disparities in Infant Drug Testing: A Research Note

Mallory Dreyer, Cydney M. McGuire, Jennifer M. Silva, Kosali Simon, Coady Wing, Engy Ziedan

Full text
Highlights Black infants were tested for drug exposure at more than twice the rate of White infants. Among those tested, both groups had similar positive rates—approximately 20%. Clinical risk factors explain only 38% of the racial disparity in testing rates. The remaining 61% may reflect differential treatment or unmeasured clinician observations. These findings raise concerns about fairness and trust in clinical decision-making.

Citizenship and Immigrant Longevity: Evidence From U.S. Census–Linked Social Security Administration Death Records

Jiaxin Shi, Jason M. Fletcher

Full text
Highlights We link full-count 1920–1940 U.S. censuses to death records to study citizenship and longevity. Immigrant men who were citizens by 1930 lived 7.6 months longer than those without first papers. Sons whose fathers were citizens by 1920 lived 4.1 months longer than sons of noncitizens. Earlier naturalization is associated with greater longevity gains in both generations. Adjusting for fathers’ SES and sons’ early-adult SES reduces the fathers’ citizenship effect.

International Journal of Press-Politics

Scripts of Escalation and Restraint: State Messaging and Public Opinion in International Crises

Frances Yaping Wang

Full text
Public opinion can both enable and constrain foreign policy, motivating governments to manipulate media during international crises. This study identifies four recurring communication strategies, one mobilizing and three moderating, each organized around a dominant cue: mobilization relies primarily on emotional arousal to stoke nationalist sentiment and foster assertiveness; censorship centers on the informational restriction of politically sensitive content; delegitimization uses normative appeals to discredit hawkish responses as irresponsible or illegitimate; and posturing employs performative displays of toughness intended to make moderate policies politically acceptable. Using a large-scale survey experiment in China, Vietnam, and the Philippines, the study evaluates how these strategies affect the public’s foreign-policy preferences and tolerance for policy deviation in a simulated fishing dispute in the South China Sea. The results show that mobilization shifts preferences in a more hawkish direction and reduces tolerance for deviation, whereas delegitimization consistently steers the public toward more moderate foreign policy preferences across countries. Censorship tempers policy preferences in all three countries but is strongest in Vietnam; posturing has no significant effect in China or Vietnam and produces a more hawkish response in the Philippines. Although the sources of this cross-national heterogeneity cannot be definitively identified, the findings suggest that propaganda effectiveness may depend partly on how audiences interpret different messages. Explicitly prescriptive strategies, such as mobilization and delegitimization, appear more portable across contexts, whereas informational or performative strategies, such as censorship and posturing, may be more contingent on audience predispositions, including baseline hawkishness, and on broader political and informational environments.

Social Media + Society

Fandom Rupture in the Movement Society: Reactive Collective Participation Through Affective Practices

Zhihong Li

Full text
When an external shock draws heterogeneous actors into a single platform arena before any collective aim emerges, participation persists at movement-like intensity. Existing theories have not specified the cohering mechanism through which this formation sustains. This study conceptualizes this mode as reactive collective participation and specifies how affective publics cohere and persist under reactive conditions at the practice level. A mixed-methods design integrates in-depth interviews with 13 heterogeneously positioned participants (fans, anti-fans, bystanders) and computational analysis of Weibo discourse surrounding the 2023 Cai Xukun scandal (139,795 posts; 205,001 comments). Four affective practices recur across the event: Positioning, Storying, Attunement, and Calibration. These practices shift in relative weight across the event’s life cycle and distribute unevenly across actor positions, producing a stratified ecology in which insulated defense and exposed antagonism sustain each other. The study advances affective publics theory by specifying how affective publics cohere and persist under reactive conditions at practice level, and collective participation theory by conceptualizing reactive collective participation as a mode that reconnects the movement society and fan activism frameworks through a reactive pathway.

Women Sustaining TikTok as Supplementary Information Infrastructure for Flexible Work Arrangements: Gendered Conditions, Community and Surveillance

Xuefei Li, Kiran Ijaz, Marta E. Cecchinato, Naseem Ahmadpour

Full text
Flexible work arrangements (FWAs) create practical and interpretive questions that formal workplace information may not adequately address, particularly around how flexibility is experienced and negotiated in everyday working life. As workers share and encounter FWA-related posts on TikTok, the platform can provide supplementary access to experiential knowledge. However, circulation alone does not explain how such knowledge becomes a durable and usable resource for workers. Drawing on infrastructure theory, we conceptualise TikTok as a supplementary information infrastructure (SII) and use a feminist perspective to examine the gendered conditions under which women contribute to and sustain it. Based on semi-structured interviews with 14 women who post about FWAs on TikTok, we analyse their relational information practices. Participants responded to misleading information with situated knowledge and interpretive resources that made workplace norms more visible and contestable. Sustaining this infrastructure came at a cost: credibility and continued participation were shaped by gendered conditions of recognition and unequal access to time and energy, while trust was challenged by shifting audiences, limited contextualisation and technological risks. Participation also exposed women creators to workplace-facing surveillance. We contribute an infrastructural account of digital workplace knowledge exchange by showing how creators’ repair and maintenance labour helps sustain TikTok as an SII. This extends research on digital workplace knowledge exchange by showing that supplementary workplace knowledge can also matter for workers who remain connected to formal workplace information. The SII’s possibilities for information justice nevertheless remain uneven because they depend on creator labour undertaken under gendered and platformed conditions.

New Media & Society

The science of beauty: Looksmaxxing, self-help, and genetic fatalism in women’s online spaces

Rachel Bergmann, Elizabeth Fetterolf

Full text
This paper examines the emergence of “looksmaxxing” in women’s online spaces. As a specific and underexamined element within what Jilly Boyce Kay analyzes as “reactionary feminism,” essentialist logics about gender and race are operationalized through the aesthetics of science and self-optimization rather than explicitly political rhetoric. Users construct the “science of beauty” through decentralized forms of expertise, from plastic surgery influencers to scientific racism to community-generated aesthetic taxonomies. We identify a thread of individualized and intimate genetic fatalism shaping both aesthetic frameworks and the tactics used to achieve them, mutating the postfeminist promise of self-transformation toward distinctly reactionary goals. This tension between the essentialism and malleability of the body makes the paradigm generative and self-sustaining, absorbing failure and keeping users invested. This case is a call to examine “fringe” and aesthetics-focused cultural formations for insight on how bioessentialist ideas about race and gender circulate today.

Too Much, Too Little, or Just Right? Identity Processes and Motivations Explain Everyday Evaluations of Time Spent on Social Media

Serena Soh, Zhenchao Hu, Sanaz Talaifar, Alan Meca, Noah Vinoya, Rui Pei, Samantha J. Grayson, Sumer S. Vaid, Nilam Ram, Jamil Zaki, ... (+1 authors)

Full text
Emerging adults use social media—and evaluate their use—while navigating the critical task of identity development. We investigate how evaluations of time spent on social media are associated with (a) individual differences in identity development and (b) situational fluctuations in identity-relevant social media motivations. In our longitudinal field study ( N = 2,251; 87,283 observations), emerging adults forming their identity tended to evaluate their social media time as balanced (i.e., the right amount), whereas those evaluating their identity tended to perceive imbalance (i.e., too much or too little time spent). Using social media to connect with others was associated with momentary perceptions of balanced use, whereas doing so to explore personal and professional goals was associated with imbalance. These findings suggest that emerging adults’ evaluations of their time spent on social media may depend on how well use aligns with identity-relevant needs. We discuss implications for psychological and digital well-being.

Seeing and Hearing YouTube: Linking Media Design to Children’s Attention Span and Viewing Control

Minjung Kim, Drew P. Cingel, Allyson L. Snyder

Full text
To examine how audiovisual characteristics of YouTube content are associated with children’s attention span and viewing control, we conducted a two-wave longitudinal survey in 2023 and 2024. The final analytical sample consisted of 195 Korean parents of children aged 4 to 6 whose children reported the same most frequently viewed YouTube channel across both waves. Videos from children’s most frequently viewed YouTube channels were coded for visual (scene changes and special effects) and audio (dialogues and sound effects) features. Structural equation modeling (SEM) results indicated that greater exposure to visual and auditory features in Year 1 content was associated with lower levels of children’s attention span and viewing control one year later. Dialogues, an audio feature, demonstrated the strongest negative association with both children’s attention span and viewing control. These findings highlight the importance of considering the design features of children’s digital media when examining associations between media environments and children's attentional and self-regulatory behaviors.

Foreign Faces in the False: Immigrant-Targeting False Posts on Social Media and Their Impact on Immigrant Perceptions

Mingxiao Sui, Ian Hawkins

Full text
This work investigates three interrelated questions around false information targeting immigrants as a broad social group on U.S. social media (thereafter referred to as immigrant-targeting false SNS claims). Study 1 examines content-level features that potentially attract readers’ attention: a quantitative analysis of PolitiFact data showed that relative to those not specifically about immigrants, immigrant-targeting false SNS claims exhibited a stronger negative tone and were more about race-related issues. Study 2 experimentally investigates the effects of immigrant-targeting SNS claims with distinctive media frames (defined as claims emphasizing either economic or health-related concerns) and that of corrective approach (with vs. without fact-checking flags): participants who viewed immigrant-targeting SNS claims flagged as false reported lower threat and anxiety perceptions and higher positive trait perceptions than those who viewed unflagged claims. Regarding the reference groups—specifically, “legal” versus “illegal” immigrants—participants associated with their perceived anxiety, the effect of fact-checking flags was conditional on the SNS claims’ media frames. Study 2 also uncovered that cognitive reflection and actively open-minded thinking play distinct roles in shaping how people respond to immigrant-targeting misinformation and fact-checking labels.

Cognitive and Emotional Processes Involved in Co-Viewing: A Naturalistic Comparison With Solo Viewers

Jingjing Han, Yuqian Ni

Full text
Video co-viewing is a central part of everyday media use, yet little is known about the cognitive and emotional processes involved in co-viewing. To facilitate theoretical development in understanding message processing in social contexts, this study theorizes four interpersonal factors that shape co-viewers’ psychological processes. As a first step toward this broader theoretical goal, we focused on interaction-free moments (i.e., silent with eyes fixed on the television) during co-viewing and investigated co-viewers’ cognitive and emotional processes by comparing them with solo viewers, within a naturalistic context where conversation is allowed. Compared to solo viewers, co-viewers exhibited (1) more positive and less negative emotions, (2) heightened physiological arousal though not significantly so, and (3) higher levels of respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA). Co-viewers’ higher RSA support the proposed social vigilance effect where the presence of a viewing partner triggers self-regulation processes as co-viewers monitor and anticipate potential interactions during naturalistic co-viewing.

Cost-effective climate change mitigation through land sector contributions

Leon Merfort, Alexander Popp, Nico Bauer, Florian Humpenöder, Gabriel Abrahão, Benjamin Leon Bodirsky, David Meng-Chuen Chen, Laurin Köhler-Schindler, Gunnar Luderer, Rahel Mandaroux, ... (+3 authors)

Full text
Climate change mitigation from Agriculture, Forestry and Other Land Use (AFOLU) comes with severe sustainability risks and faces major implementation challenges. Yet, the consequences of missing out on AFOLU mitigation potential for the transition of the energy sector and the achievability of climate objectives are not well understood. Here, using the Integrated Assessment Modeling framework REMIND–MAgPIE, we derive future mitigation pathways under different AFOLU policy portfolios. We show that, without AFOLU contributions to mitigation, limiting warming to 1.5 °C is out of reach within the twenty-first century and for keeping peak warming below 1.7 °C consumption loss relative to a current-policy baseline increases from 1.3% to 3.5%. A portfolio of options alleviates the burden of energy sector decarbonization and substantially reduces energy price markups, but puts the AFOLU sector under stress. We show that prudent policy measures, such as limiting total bioenergy use to 100 EJ yr −1 and restricting CH 4 and N 2 O pricing to US$75 per tonne of CO 2 equivalent, can largely attenuate this pressure by reducing the need for yield increases and diminishing food price markups without severely compromising the achievability of climate targets. Our findings highlight both the need for effective climate action in the AFOLU sector and the importance of balanced and judicious sectorally aligned policies.

Effective, Credible, and Acceptable: How to Ensure the EU ETS Delivers on Climate Targets

Simone Borghesi, Albert Ferrari, Kai Lessmann

Full text
The European Union opted to rely on an Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) as a cornerstone of climate policy to reach its emissions reduction targets. However, its future hinges on more than just effectiveness in reducing emissions. While the EU ETS is widely recognized as a cost‐effective tool for cutting emissions, recent geopolitical trends and economic concerns among national governments and industries have raised questions about its long‐term viability. Indeed, carbon pricing is often perceived as a barrier to industrial competitiveness, economic growth, and social justice. As a result, the EU ETS faces growing scrutiny, requiring a renewed focus on three essential conditions for its success: effectiveness, credibility, and public acceptability. The EU ETS has successfully reduced greenhouse gas emissions. Its credibility grew thanks to long‐term regulatory certainty, but it remains subject to possible speculative behaviors and market distortions. Moreover, as emissions trading expands to sectors such as road transport and building heating with a separate ETS (EU ETS 2), its direct impact on households risks triggering resistance, making public acceptability a crucial concern. Credibility can be strengthened through clear policy signals and better market oversight. Acceptability could be improved through revenue recycling and complementary measures. The latter could make the EU carbon price even more effective. Effectiveness, credibility, and acceptability are profoundly interconnected and mutually reinforcing. Future climate policies must prioritize credibility and acceptability alongside emissions reductions to succeed. If not, loss of confidence and public resistance may hinder EU ETS expansion and future ambition.

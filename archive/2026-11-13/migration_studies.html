Comparative Migration Studies

Regionalism and the categorization of migrants in Africa – comparing ECOWAS and the SADC

Dorothea Biaback Anong, Khangelani Moyo

Full text
This article examines the relationship between regional free movement frameworks and the political categorization of migrants in Africa through a comparative analysis of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) and the Southern African Development Community (SADC). Drawing on comparative regionalism and constructivist approaches, it explores how regional frameworks both shape and are shaped by the construction of migrants as “wanted” or “unwanted.” Empirically, the study combines document analysis of legislation and policy with semi-structured interviews conducted with policy actors and experts in both regions, focusing on Ghana and Nigeria (ECOWAS) and South Africa (SADC) as illustrative cases. The findings reveal a paradox in ECOWAS: despite an advanced free movement regime and references to a shared West African and pan-African identity, intra-regional migrants are constructed as economic liability and security risks. By contrast, the SADC lacks a coherent free movement framework and a corresponding category of regional citizenship, with migrant categorization primarily structured along skill and racialized notions concerning “African” migration. The comparison shows that free movement frameworks and migrant categorization are intrinsically linked, as constructions of migrants’ desirability along the axes of identity, economic utility, and security both foster and constrain the regionalization of mobility regimes. The article thus underscores the importance of migrant categorization for understanding the possibilities and limits of regional free movement in Africa.

Population, Space and Place

Sedimenting Migration Choices: Political Interpretation, Future Imaginaries and the Decision to Stay or Go Among Syrians Displaced to Turkey

Birgitte S. Holst

Full text
The article promotes a focus on the processes through which potential migrants come to imagine the future in certain ways as a central element of migratory decision‐making. Drawing on empirical material from long‐term fieldwork with Syrians displaced to Turkey, it discusses the dynamics through which future imaginaries emerged in a context marked by profound historical ruptures. To do this, it unfolds how people's contemplations about migration entailed ongoing but uncertain interpretation of politics to foresee what might come to pass in various places. Over time, uncertainty gave way to more confidence as individuals continuously reaffirmed previous interpretations leading to a sedimentation of particular assumptions about politics. Through this process, some futures came to appear more likely than others and this impacted migratory decisions because these decisions were based on potential migrants' perceptions of future possibilities ‘here’ and ‘there’. Accordingly, the article suggests that the question of why some people migrate while others stay put should be answered not only with reference to potential migrants' divergent weighing of possibilities ‘here’ against possibilities ‘there’ but also with reference to the processes through which people come to perceive possibilities ‘here’ or ‘there’ in specific ways.

Migrant Network Opportunities and the Pathways to Social Integration: Evidence From Urban China

Xueying Mu, Junru Cui, Yuanyuan Li, Wenping Xu

Full text
The integration of migrants into urban society has become a critical challenge in China's transition from land‐based to people‐oriented urbanization. Although migrant networks have been widely discussed as a source of migration‐related social capital, less is known about how the local concentration of co‐origin migrants creates opportunities for network formation and shapes migrants' social integration. Using data from the 2017 Chinese Migrants Dynamic Survey (CMDS) and generalized structural equation modeling (GSEM), this study measures co‐origin migrant concentration as the proportion of migrants from the same place of origin in each destination city, and examines its relationship with social integration. The findings show that co‐origin migrant concentration significantly promotes social integration, although the effects vary across birth cohorts: migrants born in the 1960s exhibit a stronger association than those born in the 1970s and 1980s. Housing outcomes and social interactions emerge as key mediating pathways. Migrants living in cities with higher co‐origin migrant concentrations are more likely to achieve homeownership, which in turn enhances integration. The findings also reveal contrasting patterns of social interaction. Greater co‐origin migrant concentration is associated with fewer interactions with local residents, potentially constraining deeper integration, but with more interactions with fellow migrants who have obtained local hukou , which are positively associated with social integration. By distinguishing the contextual opportunities for network formation from migrants' realized social ties, this study advances understanding of how the spatial concentration of co‐origin migrants shapes social integration through housing and social interaction.

Keep or Leave? Income–Happiness Trade‐Offs and Settlement Intentions in Urban China

Feilong Yang, Li Chen, Yunxiao Dang, Wenzhong Zhang

Full text
Population mobility research has long examined how economic opportunity shapes residential trajectories, while subjective well‐being research has often treated happiness as an outcome of income, place or life circumstances. Less attention has been paid to how income and happiness are jointly associated with settlement intentions. Drawing on survey data from 59 Chinese cities between 2020 and 2022, this article examines the conditional relationship between household income, happiness and settlement intentions. Both higher income and greater happiness are associated with stronger intentions to stay, while their interaction reveals a modest conditional pattern consistent with partial compensation. Ordered‐response analyses further identify attenuation in the probability of intending to leave across a substantial subset of income–happiness combinations, with different patterns for staying and uncertainty. Multilevel analyses indicate that the income–happiness interaction is more pronounced in wealthier and more populous cities. These findings advance a relational understanding of mobility and immobility by showing how economic resources and subjective well‐being jointly enter residential evaluations.

International Migration

Ideology and Utopia in the Governance of Migration: The Case of the 2024 Pact on Migration and Asylum and the Common European System for Returns

Valériane Mistiaen, Silvia Almenara‐Niebla

Full text
This article interrogates the 2024 Pact on Migration and Asylum and Migration and the 2025 proposal for a Common European System for Returns (CESR) through the dialectical relationship between ideology and utopia. Drawing on Ricoeur's complementarity of utopia and ideology, Levitas' Utopia as Method and critical discourse analysis, we examine how these instruments construct an idealised narrative of orderly, efficient, rights‐based governance while consolidating intensive control infrastructures. Findings show that (1) a discourse of urgency and novelty pre‐legitimises restrictive measures, (2) promises of operability and efficiency materialise in expanded data exchange and externalisation mechanisms, and (3) procedural clarity and fundamental rights are mobilised as markers of EU moral authority. We argue that the Pact and the CESR exemplify the complementarity of utopia and ideology, revealing how utopian imaginaries of good governance and ideological border control are not opposites but rather mutually reinforcing dimensions of the European border regime.

Emotion and Moral Framing in Migration Aspirations: A Natural Language Processing Study of a U.S. Emigration Reddit Community

Nari Yoo, Jeong Taek Lim, Doohyun Park, Peixuan He, Sou Hyun Jang

Full text
Research on the United States and migration has concentrated on arrival, and much less is known about why people in a wealthy democracy come to want to leave. This study examines 7855 posts written between November 2021 and April 2026 in r/Amerexit, an online community for people in the United States who are considering emigration. Natural language processing identified the themes, destinations, emotions and moral vocabulary of that discourse. Of the 15 themes, four describe conditions in the United States that users give as reasons for leaving, among them healthcare costs, racism and gun violence, and distress about the future; five describe what users expect to find elsewhere; and five concern pathways such as citizenship by descent, remote work and visas. Spain, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany and France were named most often. The moral language overwhelmingly concerned care and fairness and rarely loyalty or authority, so the grievance is stated as harm done to people. Emotion moved with political events, and differently at each: the month after the US Supreme Court's June 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson decision brought more talk of pathways and more hopeful anticipation, while the month after Donald J. Trump's January 2025 presidential inauguration brought higher anxiety and more care‐based moral language. These posts share a judgement that the United States no longer supplies conditions the writer values, in a domain that differs from writer to writer. Where fear about the country's direction turns that judgement into an intention to leave, we call the orientation political‐affective displacement.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Issue Ownership, Immigration, and the Far-Rightness of Two Irish Political Party Websites

Clara Schenk, James Windle, Kevin Sweeney

Full text
Ireland has largely avoided the escalation of far-right populism seen across Europe. There has, however, been an upsurge in far-right politics and activism, underpinned by anti-immigration sentiment. Within this context, this paper aims to determine how two Irish political parties commonly identified by media outlets as far-right present their websites, which issues they seek to own and whether these issues align with far-right ideology. Qualitative thematic analysis was conducted on transcribed screen recordings of every textual and visual component of these websites during a period of routine politics sandwiched between the 2024 European and local elections and the general election. The key finding was that, while the two parties diverged on some issues, both sought to own immigration and exhibited high degrees of nationalism, which often veered into nativism. Immigration was the core issue for both parties and interwoven with other policy positions (Euroscepticism) and issues (housing, COVID-19, the economy).

American Political Science Review

The Seeds of State Capture: Merit and Patrimonialism in the Colonial Bureaucracy

SARAH BRIERLEY, NOAH NATHAN, GEORGE KWAKU OFOSU, TINGXUAN ZHU

Full text
Bureaucracies vary in the extent to which hiring is meritocratic versus patrimonial. Such outcomes are typically seen as the result of politicians’ behavior. However, politicians often delegate hiring to existing bureaucrats. When political leaders are socially distant from communities they seek to govern and these areas are peripheral to politicians’ core interests, bureaucrats’ private preferences can dominate hiring decisions instead. Colonial and other forms of external state-building typify cases where patrimonialism created by bureaucrats, not politicians, can emerge. Digitizing individual-level records across six decades of British rule in Ghana, we find evidence of patrimonialism rather than meritocracy in the hiring of African staff, with large inequities across ethnic groups. Our results demonstrate the underexplored agency of local bureaucrats in state-building and highlight conditions under which bureaucrats’ preferences, rather than only political principals’, shape state institutions. They also challenge conventional wisdom about why, and when, newly-independent African states became “neopatrimonial.”

The Influence of Police Unions in Local Elections: Evidence from U.S. Cities’ Spending and Performance

MARIA CARRERI, EDOARDO TESO, RUI YU

Full text
Despite growing attention to local policies governing public safety issues, and mounting reports of police union spending to local candidates, the role of police unions in local politics remains understudied. We address this gap by assembling a novel, large-scale dataset on campaign finance in U.S. mayoral elections, covering 2,303 races across 1,073 cities from 2001 to 2019. We document that police unions frequently donate to mayoral candidates—especially in mid-sized and large cities—targeting likely winners and incumbents, and without displaying clear partisan bias. Using a within-city design that accounts for heterogeneous treatment effects in the presence of staggered treatment timing, we find that the election of a police-union-backed mayor leads to an expansion in police department size. However, we do not find improvements in police performance in these cities, suggesting that unions’ political involvement, while providing additional resources, might also be associated with reduced incentives for effort.

American Journal of Political Science

America's most “far‐reaching institution”: The work and legacies of the Freedmen's Bureau

Michael Albertus, Jon Rogowski

Full text
Reconstruction was the American state's first sustained peacetime intervention into local communities, and the Freedmen's Bureau was its most important component. What was the social impact of this program? Using an original data set on Freedmen's Bureau operations, we link the distribution and duration of Bureau offices to local socioeconomic outcomes. We find that the Bureau had positive effects for both Black and White residents of counties that hosted offices. Moreover, these effects were accompanied by the development of local administrative capacity and moderated by office longevity. The effects persisted for decades, though the magnitude attenuated for Blacks and increased for Whites as Jim Crow deepened and local government increasingly oriented policy benefits toward Whites. The findings indicate the importance of bureaucratic organization for policy implementation and suggest the 19th‐century federal government played a larger and more successful—albeit unequal—role in citizens’ lives than commonly believed.

Comparative Political Studies

Energy Burden and Satisfaction With Democracy: The Political Consequences of Market Risk in Germany

Laura Kettel, J. Sophia Wang

Full text
Recent years have seen steep increases in energy costs for households. To date, research on the political consequences of this emergent risk is limited. To explore this relationship for the context of Germany, we leverage an instrumental variable design, drawing on zipcode level data on energy prices, household expenditures, and individual political attitudes. We present causal evidence that rising energy costs contribute to decreased satisfaction with democracy (SWD). We argue theoretically and show empirically that the effect is driven by a pocketbook mechanism. By centering the consequences of household energy cost, we contribute to existing lines of research investigating the relationship between perceptions of economic well-being and SWD for the specific context of energy markets. The political implications of rising energy prices will likely become increasingly salient in the future, as governments tackle the challenge of designing socially just energy transition policies.

British Journal of Political Science

Who’s Asking Whom? Gendering in Parliamentary Interactions

Denisa Kostovicova, Tolga Sinmazdemir, Lanabi La Lova, Vesna Popovski

Full text
Male domination is asserted and reproduced through speaking behavior in parliaments. We address the enduring challenge of women’s marginalization by examining the interactive nature of speech. People adjust their speaking behavior based on the gender and status of those with whom they interact. Analyzing over 2,500 question-and-answer sequences in the Croatian Parliament (2004–20), we identify interactional asymmetry: male MPs ask fewer and shorter questions of female ministers than of male ministers, and engage in shorter exchanges with women than with men. Notably, female MPs partially replicate this pattern, posing shorter questions to female ministers. Female ministers, however, use their executive status to assert authority and respond with longer answers than men, despite their marginalization as women. We reveal how non-cooperation through withholding speech and limiting engagement with women operates as a form of power in interactions, reproducing parliaments as masculine institutions whilst affecting the quality of government oversight.

Perspectives on Politics

Dead Men’s Shoes: Diversity, Distributive Justice, and Job Security

Tom Parr, Andrew Walton

Full text
In this article, we make a normative argument for weakening the job security of those in senior positions in the workforce. This would increase turnover in these roles, which would facilitate diversifying those who occupy them in a way that distributes the burdens of doing so fairly. We draw particular attention to the fact that current measures to enhance diversity do not fare so favorably in distributive terms, because they decrease the opportunities of some of those applying to these roles while asking very little of those who currently hold them. Concerns relating to historical injustice, cohort inequality, and diachronic inequality all support distributing the burdens differently, and weakening the job security of those in senior positions is a plausible way to do so.

European Journal of Political Research

Democracy matters twice: How democracy relates to winner-loser gaps in political trust across countries and over time

Marta Kołczyńska, Mariano Torcal, Matías A. Bargsted

Full text
This study explores how non-democratic experiences shape gaps in political trust between electoral winners and losers in contemporary democracies, complementing existing theories that focus solely on citizens’ limited democratic experience within democratic contexts. We argue that democracy matters twice: first through historical legacies of authoritarian rule, and second through contemporary democratic improvements. Using harmonized data from the European Social Survey and Americas Barometer across 40 countries in Europe and Latin America (2002–2018), we demonstrate that individuals with greater personal exposure to autocratic rule display stronger reactions to electoral outcomes, resulting in winner-loser gaps twice as large among those who spent their entire adult lives under autocracy compared to those with no autocratic experience. Our findings also reveal that political trust is not culturally fixed but responds to democratic quality. Short-term improvements in liberal democracy significantly narrow winner-loser gaps, primarily by increasing trust among electoral losers who benefit from enhanced checks on power, free speech, and minority rights. Conversely, democratic backsliding widens these gaps. These results enhance understanding of how historical legacies and current democratic conditions together shape political trust and provide crucial insights for understanding its political polarization (politicization) and democratic challenges in post-authoritarian societies.

Political Behavior

Delayed Date and Motivated Vote: How Election Postponement Shapes Turnout

Jia Li

Full text
Autocracies postpone elections for political control, but we lack an understanding of how voters respond to election postponement in autocracies. This paper argues that election postponement motivates voter turnout across partisan groups, because voters see the postponement as a sign of regime weakness and increased electoral competitiveness. Evidence from a survey experiment in Hong Kong demonstrates that reminder messages about election postponement motivate various partisan groups to turn out, an effect robust to whether and how the message is framed. While the turnout effect is larger when the message’s narrative aligns with the subject’s partisanship—such as the propaganda and intimidation frames for government supporters and the electioneering frame for opposition supporters—all postponement reminder messages have an average turnout effect, with no significant differences across partisan groups. These findings suggest that election postponement is consequential in shaping citizens’ political behavior, with crucial implications for election turnout and authoritarian stability.

Quarterly Journal of Political Science

Learning in a complex world: how multidimensionality affects policymaking

Federica Izzo, Giovanna Invernizzi, Jesse Crosson

Full text
Policymaking inherently involves uncertainty about policy consequences. This uncertainty leads voters to rely on their past experiences when forming beliefs about policies, which ultimately shapes their electoral decisions. Officeholders, in turn, strategically weigh how their policy choices between bold initiatives and incremental options will affect voter learning and thus their electoral prospects. While conventional wisdom suggests leading incumbents always prefer safe policies and trailing ones pursue risky gambles for resurrection, this paper develops a model of policy experimentation showing that this logic often breaks down in multidimensional policy contexts. This study’s central result identifies a strategic substitution effect, whereby trailing incumbents may moderate on certain issues, while leading ones might pursue riskier policies than they otherwise would. This finding yields novel empirical implications regarding issue salience, policy extremism, and the incumbent’s electoral prospects, which this study illustrates through a preliminary plausibility check using U.S. data.

Political Science Research and Methods

Coalition differentiation at scale: measuring intra-coalition rhetorical distinctiveness with supervised classification

Christine Sheldon

Full text
Coalition parties face a unity-differentiation dilemma: they must uphold compromise to govern effectively, while maintaining distinct identities to maximize electoral success. Existing measures of coalition differentiation almost exclusively rely on static proxies to infer behavior, and the few dynamic measures often only cover a limited sample of governments, limiting comparative analysis. This article introduces a comparative, dynamic measure of rhetorical differentiation based on supervised classification of legislative speeches. Higher classification accuracy indicates greater rhetorical distinctiveness between coalition partners, while misclassification indicates greater rhetorical overlap. The measure provides monthly party-level scores for close to 50 coalition governments, across six European democracies, and thereby enables the dynamic analysis of coalition party differentiation in a public-facing arena, for a sizable comparative sample.

Blame attribution in authoritarianism: the public’s response to three safety crises in China

Dehua Sun

Full text
Citizens rely on information to evaluate the government, but their sources are limited in authoritarian regimes. This study shows that citizens can learn from extraordinary events, such as public safety incidents, which are socially salient, highly visible, and clearly attributable. When the state bears causal responsibility for a safety failure, political trust declines under both a controversial and an uncontroversial response. Using survey data that cover three national crises in contemporary China, the analysis compares respondents in the weeks immediately before and after the incidents. Trust in government declined following the 2011 bullet train crash and the 2018 vaccine scandal, but not following the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake. Further analysis finds no increase in perceived corruption, while perceptions of free expression declined after the train crash but not after the earthquake, consistent with dissatisfaction over information control. The findings have implications for authoritarian accountability and regime stability.

Social intolerance as a product of political identities: evidence from a conjoint experiment in five countries

Mariano Torcal, Josep Maria Comellas, Alina Vrânceanu

Full text
Do group identities rooted in salient social and political characteristics foster homophily and social distance? If so, which identities shape social preferences more strongly, and under what conditions? While existing literature on affective polarization and inter-group relations privileges particular social and political identities, we know less about the dynamics that unfold when multiple identities are considered simultaneously. Using conjoint experiments in five multiparty systems (Argentina, Chile, Italy, Portugal, and Spain), we find that political identities are generally more influential than social ones among individuals with partisan affinities. This is especially true among left-wing individuals, who display greater social distance to radical right supporters. Our findings suggest that social distance is rooted in political attachments, which may channel salient social divisions.

Trade liberalization and the welfare state: how subnational geographies shape compensation

Alice Xu

Full text
How globalization reshapes the welfare state in developing democracies remains contested, and evidence on whether trade liberalization expands or contracts social spending is mixed. Using a quasi-natural experiment in Brazil, I first find support for the “compensation hypothesis:” trade integration generates welfare compensation. I then argue that trade’s differential impact across subnational regions helps explain the mixed evidence. Subnational political and economic geographies mediate trade’s effect on social policy. In particular, I argue that the geographic concentration of political identities facilitates collective action. I find that geographically concentrated constituencies exhibit higher voter turnout and more participatory councils advocating for labor rights and welfare. Trade’s positive welfare effect is magnified where political or economic identities are geographically concentrated. The findings spotlight how subnational geography shapes globalization’s redistributive consequences.

Comparing belief certainty formats in the measurement of political knowledge

Robert Vidigal

Full text
Psychological certainty plays a role in how respondents retrieve and express their attitudes, beliefs, and opinions. This study compares two question formats that allow respondents to express certainty about their political knowledge. Using a YouGov sample ( N = 2,161), a single-item certainty-in-knowledge format, where political knowledge and belief certainty are measured jointly, and a branching format, where knowledge is measured first, followed by certainty in a separate question, are compared. The results show that the certainty-in-knowledge format significantly reduces the likelihood of respondents looking up answers online. Including a “Don’t Know” option does not increase gender differences. These findings demonstrate a question format that improves validity in online surveys by reducing measurement artifacts.

Democratic hypocrisy: support for undemocratic responses to protest in Germany

Morgan Le Corre Juratic, Markus Wagner, Daniel Bischof

Full text
Political tolerance is a cornerstone of liberal democracy, yet citizens apply it selectively: intolerance is higher for disliked groups. However, does this extend to democratic hypocrisy, leading to support for undemocratic responses to protest? We address this important question through a vignette survey experiment ( N = 5,000) in Germany, comparing reactions to similar protest tactics used by farmers and climate activists in a realistic, externally valid setting. We show that respondents were not only less willing to grant climate activists key democratic rights but also significantly more likely to support undemocratic responses to climate protesters, including imprisonment without trial. Our findings underline that citizens engage in democratic hypocrisy: their political biases lead them to support undemocratic actions against disliked groups.

Optimal party strategies on niche issues: a computational approach to multiparty competition for position and salience

Anna Sophie Kurella, Lovisa Mundschenk

Full text
How should parties respond to competition from a new challenger on a niche issue? Meguid argues that accommodation is effective, but recent evidence shows it may cost votes. We identify vote-maximizing strategies of established parties facing an extreme challenger using an extended Downsian model with position and salience. Based on empirical data from Germany, our results show that accommodation works only for low-valence parties, and only while the challenger is new. Otherwise, adversarial strategies benefit small parties. Major established parties maximize votes by holding moderate positions and engaging weakly with the issue. Our findings offer a theoretical baseline for understanding optimal strategies in party competition.

Terror across borders, politics at home: Political influencers navigating diasporic threat and political identity on social media

Gabrielle D. Beacken

Full text
Terror attacks occurring in one jurisdiction can shape the perceived political attitudes of populations in a separate jurisdiction—through diasporic ties or the mediation of political discourses via social media. This study examines how left‐ and center‐leaning Jewish influencers addressing American audiences perceived and expressed the impact of the Hamas terrorist attack on October 7, 2023, on their political attitudes and messaging in the context of the 2024 US Presidential election. This study implemented a three‐pronged digital ethnographic approach: digital observation on Instagram, 15 in‐depth interviews, and digital fieldwork analyzing interviewees' political posts in the lead‐up and aftermath of the election. Findings reveal heterogeneity of political attitude and orientation in the face of transnational terror and perceived threat to Jewish life—from preference falsification to single‐issue voting and calls for bipartisanship. Rather than describing a political transformation away from liberal or progressive thought, interviewees described a refocusing of political energy around Jewish safety. This study argues that the transnational threat of October 7, compounded by feelings of political abandonment domestically, was accompanied by a process of attitudinal navigation—complicating expectations of in‐group solidarity, threat‐to‐conservatism pathways, and the strategic communication of political influencers as emergent opinion leaders on social media.

Affective dynamics of welfare chauvinism under different policy funding structures

Duygu Merve Uysal

Full text
This research investigates how distinct emotional responses to immigrants—namely, compassion, anger, and anxiety—are connected to welfare chauvinist attitudes within varying welfare policy funding contexts. Leveraging Turkey's experience with large‐scale immigration and the exogenous nature of EU funding, a population‐representative survey experiment was conducted, manipulating claimant identity (native vs. immigrant) and the funding source of welfare assistance (state vs. EU‐funded). Results show that a “care deficit” operates as a key mechanism linking immigrant identity and lower support for immigrant welfare access, but only within the nation‐bound welfare system. Once compassion is evoked, however, it operates similarly for natives and immigrants. Furthermore, moderation analyses reveal divergent patterns of compassion, anger, and anxiety in shaping welfare chauvinist attitudes across different policy funding structures. Highlighting the crucial interplay between emotions and the policy funding schemes in which welfare assistance is delivered, this research contributes to the debate on what drives welfare chauvinism. Drawing on the political psychology literature, this study offers a perspective on the emotional dynamics associated with the persistent native‐immigrant welfare support gap and discusses potential factors that may contribute to narrowing this disparity.

Philosophy & Public Affairs

The Choiceworthlessness of Transformative Heroism

Meradjuddin Khan Oidermaa, Mac Willners

Full text
History abounds with transformative moral heroes—agents devoting their lives to uprooting entrenched unjust structures. Though philosophy has said much about heroes generally, this distinctive class remains undertheorized. This matters because they are among our most celebrated moral exemplars, and much of our sense of moral progress rests on them. Yet they characteristically act against familiar constraints on responsible deliberation, pursuing apparently infeasible and inefficacious projects while foreclosing tractable alternatives that would secure demonstrable goods. We establish this tension and argue that the most promising attempts to dissolve it fail. At best, this shows that moral theory is structurally conservative, as its standards of responsible deliberation systematically favor mitigation over transformation. At worst, it reveals a deeper instability in moral theory, as it seems to depend for its progress on agents it cannot vindicate.

A Defense of Affirmative Action for Conservatives

Daniel Jacobson

Full text
The suggestion that academia should promote political diversity, implying as it does that the status quo is severely deficient, is commonly rejected on the grounds that to do so would require “affirmative action for conservatives.” That conclusion is typically treated as a reductio ad absurdum even by those who support conventional race‐ and sex‐based affirmative action. Nevertheless, this paper defends affirmative action for conservatives on both negative and positive grounds. It argues that since conservatives are discriminated against throughout academia, policies that benefit them in admissions and hiring would not be unjust to those who bear its burden. And it argues that the advantages of increased political diversity would accrue not just to those with heterodox opinions but would improve the intellectual atmosphere of academia at large. Hence, conservative affirmative action promotes the traditional academic mission and its norms of meritocracy, academic freedom, and the pursuit of knowledge; and it is not unjust to those with orthodox political views.

Government and Opposition

Opposition Strategies and Democratic Transition Failure: Affective Polarisation and Institutional Fragmentation in Post-Gaddafi Libya

Walid Ali, Sergiu Gherghina

Full text
An extensive literature has investigated the importance of governing political elites in the transition from authoritarianism. However, we still know very little about the role of opposition forces, especially in conflict zones within the Global South. This article addresses this research gap by analysing how parliamentary opposition strategies contributed to Libya’s transition outcome. It examines the opposition strategies directed against the Islamic majority within the General National Congress, the country’s first elected parliament after Gaddafi’s fall in 2011. It uses primary data drawn from semi-structured interviews conducted in 2023 with former politicians, academics, political journalists and non-governmental organisation (NGO) activists. The findings illustrate how, although opposition strategies were aimed at countering Islamic influence, they resulted in the destabilising consequences of affective polarisation and institutional fragmentation.

The Winning Formula of the Populist Radical Right: Insider Narratives and the Making of a Representative Claim

Francesco Melito, Mattia Zulianello

Full text
The literature has explained the success of the populist radical right (PRR) through demand-side and supply-side factors, but it has typically adopted an ‘external’ perspective, leaving unexplored how PRR parties themselves interpret their success. This article investigates the winning formula of this party family from an ‘insider’ perspective, drawing on 21 in-depth interviews with representatives of a clear case of PRR success: Brothers of Italy (Fratelli d’Italia, FdI). Rather than testing the causal claims of the literature, we theorise these insider accounts as strategic narratives of success: they show how FdI representatives draw on ideological, organisational and leadership repertoires, selectively appropriate elements of the external environment, and, most distinctively, mobilise valence-inspired self-descriptions (especially coherence) to construct a claim of distinction within the party system. In this context, coherence operates as a strategic device that signals the party’s unity and political consistency – anchored in FdI’s refusal to join broad coalitions – performing discursive work of self-legitimation in PRR normalisation.

Politics & Gender

The Unintended Cost of Care: How Family Policies Impact Intimate Partner Violence in the European Union

Mariel J. Barnes

Full text
Despite being considered the de facto world leader in gender equality, the European Union has a significant problem — its extremely high rates of everyday violence against women (EVAW). Contrary to conventional wisdom about development and gender equality, recent survey research shows the most industrialized countries in Europe also report a higher prevalence of EVAW. In this article, I argue that family policies — particularly those associated with the universal breadwinning model and increased women’s labor market participation — are related to intimate partner violence. Utilizing a nationally representative and comparable dataset on EVAW in the European Union, I find state expenditure on in-kind childcare is associated with a higher likelihood of a woman experiencing intimate partner violence, while more generous parental leave schemes have the opposite relationship. This underscores that while family policies are often designed to rectify gender disparities in formal and caring work, they may also yield unintended yet profoundly negative consequences.

Selective doors: The role of party ideology, candidate selection rules, and Muslim population in the selection of Muslim minority candidates in European countries

Memoona Nadir

Full text
Candidate selection is an integral part of the political process in European democracies as political parties decide who will be on candidate lists. The candidates’ selection process can sometimes disadvantage potential candidates, especially minority candidates, including Muslims. This article has examined the selection of Muslim candidates in Belgium, France, the Netherlands, and the UK in 16 elections (2007-2022) and the factors that affect this. The results show that while Muslims are generally underrepresented in terms of candidates, there is significant variation between countries. A number of party-level and constituency-level factors, such as party ideology, the size of the Muslim population, and candidate selection rules, influence the selection of Muslim candidates.

Reframing moderation: Frame moderation as a radical-right party strategy

Spencer J Corp

Full text
Many radical-right parties across Western Europe have normalized electorally while holding stable, hardline positions on immigration. This paper argues that these parties have moderated the perception of their core policies not through positional moderation, but through frame moderation: they change how they justify a policy without necessarily changing the policy itself, altering the considerations through which voters perceive their position. Conceptually, the paper distinguishes the range of frames a party invokes from the frequency with which it invokes these frames. Empirically, it develops a scalable measure of framing width and of change within frame categories using a novel method of sentence embeddings. Its usefulness is illustrated with the case of the French National Rally’s framing of immigration across manifestos from 1997 to 2022. As the party normalized, it widened its range of frames, increased its framing of immigration in terms of economic considerations, and moved from a cultural frame that emphasized ethnic homogeneity toward one that focuses on the defense of secularism and gender equality. Frame moderation enables stigmatized parties to appear more mainstream without moderating their positions. This suggests that positional measures alone may understate how radical-right parties have changed.

Political Research Quarterly

The Election Monitoring Trilemma: Measuring Rare Elite Threats on Social Media With Large Language Models

Sean T. Norton, Kevin T. Greene, Olivia Leu, Anthony J. DeMattee, Jacob N. Shapiro

Full text
In 2024, elections across more than 70 countries raised fears that social media would incite violence and erode democratic norms. We collected public posts from candidates, parties, journalists, and influencers in South Africa, India, and Germany, coding a random sample for five outcomes using human and LLM annotation. All five are rare on the public platforms we study: incitement to violence appeared in just 0.2% of posts, attacks on democratic norms in 1.9%, and civic duty appeals in 2.3%. While LLMs accurately identified true positives, their precision on rare categories was poor and for the two rarest and highest-stakes categories—incitement and ethnic appeals—the LLMs’ ability to catch true positives rests on a deliberately enriched sample. In addition to the limitations low precision poses to researchers, we further show that this precision shortfall confronts election monitors with a three-way tradeoff among coverage, throughput, and credibility that no single off-the-shelf configuration resolves.

Political Theory

New Materialism, Harm, and Hierarchies in Nature

Samuel Piccolo

Full text
This article is about an internal contradiction within the ascendant philosophy of new materialism, namely that its ethical opposition to harm clashes with its ontology. In the first part, I summarize new materialists’ attention to material entanglements and rejection of hierarchies in nature. In the second section, I argue that diagnosing and addressing harm to ecosystems and individuals (almost) always depend upon a hierarchy of beings within that ecosystem and a set of pre-determined potentials/preferences of how individual entities should be, two principles that new materialists explicitly reject. In the conclusion, I suggest that new materialists’ contradictions surrounding harms mean that they can either maintain a concern over ongoing damage to ecosystems and beings or their opposition to immaterial potentials and a hierarchy among beings—but not both. My preferred option is to retain the former and to accept that we must have some hierarchy among and within beings to address ongoing ecological deterioration around us. I close by outlining how new materialism’s key insights might be put in dialogue with other voices on the relation between humans and the rest of the world.

Comparative Politics

Unforging Autonomy: The Political Subordination of Brazil’s Itamaraty

Katherine Bersch, Gabriela Lotta, Mariana Costa Silveira, Izabela Correa, Kutsal Yesilkagit

Full text
Understanding how bureaucratic autonomy is undone is as important as understanding how it is built. We develop the concept of unforging autonomy to describe the unraveling of supports that sustain bureaucratic independence. Autonomy rests on two pillars: internal institutional arrangements and external constituencies that sustain legitimacy. Drawing on thirty-one interviews and documentary evidence, we examine Brazil’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Itamaraty), long considered a paradigmatic case of bureaucratic autonomy. We show how former President Jair Bolsonaro exploited vulnerabilities in the diplomatic career system by politicizing promotions and postings, weakening professional norms while turning diplomats’ external alliances into political liabilities. The case reveals how interventions targeting seemingly narrow institutional weaknesses can undermine deeply entrenched bureaucratic autonomy, with broader implications for the resilience of established institutions.

PS: Political Science & Politics

The Shadow of 1776: Imperial Republicanism, Technocracy, and the New Gaza

Md Samiul Azim

Full text
This article situates the current governance of Gaza within the contrast between imperial rule and trusteeship and argues that the proposed US-led paradigm is a reimagining of the “Imperial Republic.” The technocratic trusteeship is introduced as a governing trope that promises self-determination yet defers it forever through expert administration. Through critical discourse analysis and legal–historical comparison between policy documents and mandate-era precedents, this study shows that sovereignty is displaced by managerial oversight, capacity building, and security conditionality. The most important discovery is that technocratic trusteeship transforms administrative performance into political freedom, efficiency, and neutrality, thereby annihilating any perception of disempowerment. This model has broader implications for international governance, indeed, unwrought by the legacy of 1776.

Tackling the Lack of Territorial Diversity: Lessons from a Two-Year Supply-Side Intervention in Brazil

Victor Araújo, Malu A. C. Gatto, Magna Inácio

Full text
Political science increasingly acknowledges the importance of diversifying the discipline, yet authors based in the Global South remain underrepresented in top international journals. Addressing this imbalance requires tackling both demand- and supply-side factors. Focusing on supply-side factors, we piloted the Writing Academy, a training and mentorship program for Brazilian political science doctoral students. Drawing on survey data from applicants, we examine the main barriers to internationalizing their research. We find that a lack of knowledge of the rules for international publications—not language—is the primary barrier to submissions to international journals. Pre- and post-intervention survey data indicate that the Writing Academy reduced unfamiliarity with academic journals and increased participants’ confidence in publishing their research in international journals. These findings suggest that targeted training and mentorship can reduce supply-side inequalities in global scholarly participation.

Policy Studies Journal

When More Is More: Committee Overlap and the Diversity of Information in Problem Definition

Connor Dye

Full text
Congress relies on a fragmented bureaucracy to monitor complex problems, raising concerns over whether it can aggregate the diverse information needed for effective policy‐making. I argue that competition among committees with different institutional ties to agencies should produce informational diversity without any coordinating body. Analyzing 92,375 instances of agency testimony across 131 agencies from 1973 to 2021, I find that a greater number of committees examining a problem is consistently associated with a broader and less concentrated set of agencies supplying information. I find little evidence that this association is systematically moderated by public salience.

Volatility in Evidence Use: A Computational Analysis of Punctuated Equilibrium Theory

Stefani Langehennig

Full text
How does the use of evidence in policy documents change over time, and what explains differences between stable, incremental, and abrupt shifts in its use? This study introduces a computational framework to evaluate change in evidence use across Congressional Research Service (CRS) reports. Using a combination of large language models and supervised machine learning, each document is scored on its alignment with evidence‐based policymaking principles and aggregated to the policy‐topic level. The results show that most policy areas exhibit gradual change, while punctuated shifts are less common and highly variable. Public salience is associated with punctuated evidence‐use trajectories, while media and executive attention are associated primarily with gradual change; partisan control plays a limited role once attention and institutional demand are taken into account. Substantial variation remains across policy topics, with areas such as Macroeconomics and Government Operations exhibiting relatively high dispersion in evidence‐use scores. These findings demonstrate that evidence use in policymaking is dynamic and attention‐sensitive over time.

Policy and Politics

Policy feedback and media framing of crime victims

Holly L. Peterson, Ryan J. Williams, Sarah Koon-Magnin, Logan Berthelot

Full text
Drawing on framing theory and the social construction of target populations, this study examines whether and how landmark legislation is associated with shifts in media framing of the populations it targets. We examine this question through the Crime Victims’ Rights Act of 2004 (CVRA), landmark legislation that significantly changed the way the legal process understood victims. By expanding victims’ participatory rights in the prosecution of those who harmed them, it designated victims as deserving a voice in the process and institutionalised the fact that the victim’s experience mattered for the fair provision of justice. Treating the CVRA as a chronological anchor, we conduct a theory-driven framing analysis of 315 articles from four major US newspapers across the four years surrounding the CVRA’s passage to examine how media frames of crime victims shifted across this threshold. We document an asymmetric pattern: frames of victim power and agency shifted measurably in the direction a policy-feedback account would predict, while frames of victim complexity (indirect harm, secondary victimisation, and non-ideal representation), predicated by victimology, did not. We offer this pattern as one plausible instance of arena-refracted feedback effects , in which policy feedback is refracted by institutional carriers, such as the media, through which it travels to reach mass publics.

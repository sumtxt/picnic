Longitudinal impacts of family conflict and school environment on youth mental health: An ABCD study

Kayleigh Esparza, Robin Aupperle, Dalia El-Shafie, Herry Patel, Wes Thompson, Martin Paulus

Full text
Background: Adolescence is a critical period for social and emotional development during which these dynamics start to shift, placing them at an increased risk for poor mental health outcomes. Bioecological theory posits that various levels of one’s environment, including family and school, can impact youth development and mental health outcomes. Therefore, the current study aimed to explore the longitudinal relationships between family and school environment and youth mental health. Methods: The study used data collected as part of the longitudinal and multisite Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development Study (ABCD® Study). Baseline sample included 11,868 youth aged 9-10. The study used data from follow up year 1-year 3, as these time points had data for all variables. We examined family conflict, school environment, and youth and caregiver reports of youth internalizing and externalizing symptoms in a three-wave random intercept cross-lagged panel model. Results: Results indicated that family conflict impacted youth externalizing symptoms from follow-up year 2 to year 3. Youth internalizing and externalizing symptoms impacted family conflict from follow-up year 1 to year 2 and from follow-up year 2 to year 3. Moreover, school environment impacted youth internalizing and externalizing symptoms from follow-up year 1 to year 2. Youth externalizing symptoms impacted the school environment from follow-up year 1 to year 2 and from follow-up year 2 to year 3, while youth internalizing symptoms impacted the school environment from follow-up year 2 to year 3. Conclusions: Results support bidirectional relationships between family and school environments and youth mental health during varying developmental periods. Early detection of internalizing and externalizing symptoms is important for interrupting mutually reinforcing cycles of symptom escalation and environmental strain.

Fake–Real gaps in observed spread and detectability differ between health misinformation datasets built in different ways: a comparison of CoAID and FakeHealth

Ji Zhao, Jesús Valdiviezo

Full text
Health misinformation datasets don't reach a common ground about what misinformation is. FakeHealth labels mainstream health news by expert-rated reporting quality, while CoAID compares fact-checked COVID-19 claims with material from health agencies and health information publishers. We compared model-standardized Fake/Real ratios of observed social media posts, including items with no observed posts, between the CoAID News and FakeHealth HealthStory subsets, and evaluated leakage-controlled text classifiers within, across and beyond the two datasets. Fake items drew fewer observed posts than Real items in both datasets, but the ratio was 0.253 in CoAID and 0.840 in FakeHealth (ratio-of-ratios 0.301; 95% interval 0.154–0.645). The CoAID gap lay entirely in whether an item was posted at all and was concentrated among Fake items with no identifiable publisher, which points to limited findability as well as limited spread. In two FakeNewsNet collections outside health, Fake items drew more observed posts in GossipCop (ratio 2.144), and PolitiFact's interval included parity. Text showed a parallel split: Fake and Real were highly separable within CoAID (macro F1 0.926) and modestly within FakeHealth (0.675), transfer was poor in both directions, and word–label associations were uncorrelated across datasets. Fake items were shorter in both datasets, and length alone ranked transferred items and independent COVID rumors better than the word-based models. The distance between Fake and Real items, in language and in observed engagement, differed between datasets that define and source misinformation differently; these are comparisons between dataset contexts, not estimates of a pandemic effect. Engagement studies and detection benchmarks should report how labels, sources and collection were defined, check for source–label entanglement, shared-post dependence and simple length baselines, and test beyond a single dataset.

Age differences in entry into parenthood among siblings and cousins in Dutch and Finnish kinship networks

Vera de Bel, Alyona Artamonova, Takayuki Hiraoka, Eszter Bokányi, Mirkka Danielsbacka, Marijtje A.J. van Duijn, Antti O Tanskanen

Full text
With fewer siblings and cousins sharing longer lifespans, their relationships may become increasingly important sources of social influence and support in ageing societies. This study makes an empirical and methodological contribution by comparing and analyzing the association of horizontal kin’s timing of entry into parenthood in two European countries: the Netherlands and Finland. We investigate whether horizontal kin being closer in degree of relatedness, share maternal lineage and have the same gender, are more likely to have their first child at approximately the same age. Our results show that cousins are more similar to full siblings in their age at first birth than we expected, whereas half-siblings have the largest age difference. In a handful of Western European countries, a new data source has recently become available that allows empirical analysis of kinship networks across the entire population. To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply statistical methods to analyze and compare horizontal kinship networks across different European contexts.

Has the Motherhood Wage Penalty Declined? Changing Selection into Motherhood Across Cohorts in Japan

Ryota Mugiyama

Full text
Whether the motherhood wage penalty has declined amid family policy expansion and shifting gender norms has attracted attention. However, a change in the penalty over time could reflect both changing effects of motherhood and changing selection into motherhood, in particular selection on wage growth, which can produce spurious trends. Using nationally representative panel data from Japan, this study examines the wage consequences of motherhood over the ten years following first birth in an earlier (1960–1974) and a later (1975–1989) cohort, using fixed effects (FE) models and fixed effects individual slopes (FEIS) models, which account for individual differences in wage growth. Averaged over the ten years following first birth, the penalty was 10.1% and 3.6% in the earlier and later cohorts under the FE model, but 8.4% and 11.9% with no significant cohort difference under the FEIS model. Mothers in the later cohort also had steeper underlying wage growth than non-mothers, which is consistent with changing selection into motherhood on wage growth accounting for the apparent decline under the FE model. These findings provide no evidence that the motherhood wage penalty in Japan declined across cohorts, despite decades of family policy expansion and shifting gender norms.

The Ottoman Shadow: Federal Bypass Capture, Emerging-Donor Concessions, and Contested Development in Turkey’s Horn of Africa Diplomacy

Ismail H Warsame

Full text
Turkey’s engagement with Somalia since 2011 is usually presented as the renewal of an Ottoman-era partnership and as an example of emerging-donor engagement free of the conditionality attached to Western aid. This article asks what that engagement does to authority and revenue inside a fragile federation. It argues that Turkish agreements over Somali state assets and natural resources have been concluded almost entirely with the federal government, including over resources that the Provisional Constitution assigns to negotiation between the federal government and the member states. It names this pattern federal bypass capture and specifies three observable implications against which it can be tested. Process tracing of Turkish port, airport, defence, fisheries and hydrocarbon arrangements from 2011 to 2026 finds the first and third implications supported and the second partly supported, and shows that the resulting opposition, centred on Puntland, has been framed in constitutional terms. The Ottoman connection invoked in official discourse is real but narrow and discontinuous. Plausibility probes of Emirati port concessions in Puntland and Somaliland, and of Kurdish oil exports from northern Iraq through Turkey, suggest that the mechanism belongs to a wider pattern of tier selection. The article closes with four hypotheses for comparative testing. For development scholarship, the case suggests that the effect of emerging-donor engagement on a fragile federation depends on which tier of the federation the donor negotiates with.

NISBO Elderly Advisory Services as a Bridge between Older Migrants and Welfare Institutions: Historical Lessons from the Dutch Experience

Cemal YILMAZ

Full text
This article examines the historical development and institutional significance of elderly advisory services for older migrants in the Netherlands, focusing on the Netherlands Islamic Elderly Union (NISBO) and its predecessor, the Turkish Islamic Elderly Union (TISBO). Employing a qualitative historical case-study approach, the research analyses TISBO project and preparatory documents from the mid-1990s, a NISBO planning text from the 1996 transition period, and NISBO reports from 1999 and 2001, supplemented by governmental records and academic literature. The study traces the advisory initiative from its establishment within TISBO in 1994 through a gradual institutional transition completed by 1997. Historical records document 697 advisory requests between January and May 1998, addressing income, housing, healthcare and welfare-related concerns. The analysis develops an institutional bridge framework encompassing five interconnected functions: informational accessibility, communication and mediation, trust and individual support, institutional coordination, and representation and organisational learning. The findings illustrate how migrant-led advisory initiatives sought to connect individual needs with mainstream welfare institutions through accessible guidance, mediation and organisational cooperation. Although the available documentation does not establish long-term service effectiveness, the historical NISBO experience offers relevant insights into culturally responsive assistance, institutional accessibility and community-based support within increasingly diverse ageing societies.

Making ascriptions of harmfulness

Mark Wood

Full text
Criminologists and zemiologists regularly ascribe harmfulness to a range of social phenomena, from beliefs and motifs to social structures and subjectivities. But what does it mean – and what do we need – to assert that such phenomena are harmful to someone or something? In this article, I argue that an account of harmfulness is equally important to an account of harm, and that arriving at the latter does not necessarily provide us with guidance on what is harmful to someone or something. Moreover, I address several recently expressed worries about relativism about harm and argue that through properly accounting for both harm and harmfulness we can – and should – accept a certain type of relativism about harm, given that discourses, practices, social structures, and other social objects are not harmful simpliciter but harmful to someone, usually under certain conditions. I argue that social harm scholars and criminologists concerned with particularly freewheeling forms of subjectivism or relativism about social harm can allay some of these concerns by distinguishing between what is conditionally and unconditionally harmful to someone.

The Conditional Relevance of Value Orientations in Intergenerational Care: Institutional Context, Gender and Direction of Care in SHARE

Zuzana Talašová, Martin Lakomý

Full text
Population ageing has intensified Europe’s reliance on family care, while long-term processes of individualisation are widely assumed to weaken family obligations. This study examines the conditions under which individual value orientations predict participation in intergenerational care among older adults in 27 European countries. Using wave 8 of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE, 2019–2020), it tests whether four indicators of value orientations (interpersonal trust, religiosity, risk aversion and planning horizon) are associated with care provided to grandchildren and to ageing parents. Grandparental childcare is associated with religiosity and, non-monotonically, with risk aversion; care for parents is associated only with risk aversion, in the same non-monotonic form. Contrary to the expectation that values matter more where discretion is greater, the associations do not differ significantly between the two directions of care, and gender does not moderate them. Institutional context does: the association of trust with caregiving is negative within post-communist Central and Eastern Europe and not negative, or weakly positive, outside it, in both directions. A supplementary analysis of at least weekly care shows that this moderation concerns participation only, while religiosity predicts regular care in both directions. The behavioural relevance of value orientations thus varies less with the direction of care or the caregiver’s gender than with the institutional context in which family care is decided.

Thick and thin accounts of social harm

Mark Wood

Full text
Social harm is a concept on the rise in criminology and zemiology, yet one that remains under-theorised. This article argues that existing accounts misclassify certain harms, are anthropocentric, and struggle to struggle to account for variance in what makes someone or something’s life go best. I argue that these issues can be addressed though distinguishing between ‘thin’ and ‘thick’ accounts of social harm. Thin accounts provide us with formal statements about what is affected when someone or something is harmed (harm’s medium), what the baseline is for assessing whether this thing has been impacted (harm’s structure), and what makes a harm social (harm’s social ontology). Thick accounts add a final, crucial, ingredient: a subject-dependent substantive theory of whatever we take to be harm’s medium (harm’s currency). I then offer a thin account of social harm that avoids the challenges faced by existing accounts by treating wellbeing as harm’s medium, events which negatively affect someone’s wellbeing as harm’s structure, and human actions as structuring causes as its social ontology.

Critical Theory of AI: The Alienation of the Human Intellect

Geoff M Boucher

Full text
In this article, I present some fundamentals of a critique of AI influenced by the Critical Theory of the Frankfurt School. From this perspective, artificial intelligence represents a kind of apotheosis of instrumental rationality, an alienation of the human intellect, framed by economic calculations, geopolitical strategies, the reduction of reason to cognition, perception to classification and knowledge to an inventory. The article begins by defining machine-learning-based forms of artificial intelligence, distinguishing between different types of foundation model (LLM-based and non-LLM-based), describing how LLM-based GenAI works and discussing the realistic prospects for the emergence of AGI in the near future, especially when this is anticipated from increases in compute scale of GenAI. Then, it outlines the fundamental critical position of Frankfurt School Critical Theory, which is that human knowledge cannot be reduced to an inventory of items any more than the human intellect can be reduced to instrumental rationality. Against this background, it critiques the application of AI as a business machine and a warfare technology, arguing that it is crucial, in opposing corporate and military applications, not to uncritically accept the reduction of the human intellect to mental labor. Nonetheless, the implications of AI for the automation of mental labor are critically important, and so the article turns next to the microeconomics of AI implementation, inferring from the discourse of liberal economics that the disruptive structural transformation of the middle class through polarization and casualization is a real possibility. In conclusion, the article discusses the implications of this dialectical critique, not just in terms of the potential for a political convulsion, but also in terms of the urgent need for AI research to acknowledge (and address) the problems and limitations of current foundation models.

Substance Over Label: Semantic Drift and Zionist Self-Identification among Canadian Jews

David R. Mandel, Irina Levit

Full text
A 2024 survey found that 94 percent of Canadian Jews affirm Israel’s right to exist as a Jewish state, yet only 51 percent call themselves Zionists. Brym attributed the gap to semantic drift: the word Zionism has acquired a pejorative connotation, so many Jews who hold Zionist commitments nevertheless decline the label. If so, substantive markers of Zionism should predict attitudes toward Canadian policies that impose costs on Israel, and the label should add little once those markers are accounted for. Using weighted data from the same survey (N = 550), we find that emotional attachment to Israel and belief in its right to exist as a Jewish state explain 23 percent of the variance in opposition to sanctions on violent settlers, an arms embargo, and recognition of a Palestinian state, beyond self-identification. Self-identification adds less than 1 percent beyond those markers, a nonsignificant increment. Among respondents very attached to Israel, non-Zionists and self-identified Zionists differ by only 0.02 points on a five-point scale. The results add converging evidence for the semantic drift hypothesis and show that, among Canadian Jews, calling oneself a Zionist does not mark a distinct position on Canadian policy toward Israel once attachment is considered.

Finja Mailin Schneider

Full text
This paper evaluates the impact of Mexico’s Gender Violence Alert (AVGM) on violence against women and girls. AVGM constitutes a key institutional response to violence against women and girls (VAWG) in Mexico, yet causal evidence on its effects remains limited. I employ a staggered difference-in-differences analysis to estimate the effects of AVGM declarations across Mexican municipalities. The analysis draws on longitudinal mortality data as well as survey data. Beyond femicides and self-reported violence, I study two intermediate outcomes: reporting behaviour and attitudes towards abortion as an indicator of gender norms. Heat-constrained potential accessibility to outdoor sport facilities in France: A dynamic supply framework for spatial planning

Dimitri Defrance

Full text
Spatial accessibility indicators assume that facilities within reach are available whenever residents wish to use them. For outdoor sport facilities, heat breaks this assumption. We develop heat-constrained potential accessibility, in which the supply of each facility is scaled by its hourly thermal availability. Availability is derived from regional wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT; ERA5-Land, 1991–2020) modified by facility attributes: cover, tree canopy and surface type. We apply the model to 97,816 public outdoor facilities and 62.6 million residents of metropolitan France, at 200-m resolution, with a 15-min walking catchment and a 26 °C WBGT threshold. On July afternoons, heat removes on average 25% of potential accessibility, and at least 47% in Occitanie, Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur and Corsica. Served residents spend 139 h per season without any heat-available facility within reach, and 156 h in 2006–2025. Magnitudes depend strongly on the threshold (11% peak loss at 28 °C), whereas spatial rankings are stable. A regional WBGT without facility attributes underestimates mean losses by 12–15%. After regional standardisation, peak losses differ little across income groups, but desert exposure increases with income, in association mainly with the number of facilities within reach and secondarily with their peak availability. Under idealised complete shading, shading 5% of facilities chosen to maximise coverage removes 68% of heat-attributable desert person-hours (50% with half shade), against 21% when the hottest facilities are shaded first. Heat-constrained accessibility turns climate exposure into a property of supply that planners can map, compare and target.

Open to Interpretation: How Frontier AI Developers Practice Openness through Licensing

Scott Robert Patterson, Jason Tucker

Full text
Openness is a central but contested category in global artificial intelligence (AI) governance, yet policy debates often treat it as a binary property of developers, or even of states. This article asks how leading foundation model developers practice openness through release and licensing decisions, and what this implies for how openness is governed globally. We analyze an original longitudinal dataset of 1,313 model releases by twelve developers in the United States (US), China, France and Canada between January 2023 and October 2026. We find that openness is a property of model portfolios rather than of developers. We distinguish four configurations, defined by whether, and on what terms, a developer releases its most capable model: permissive, conditional, or non-commercial open flagship, or closed flagship with permissively licensed secondary models. Secondary models have converged on generic permissive licenses, while open flagships have moved towards bespoke conditions that target revenue rather than users. The sharpest division concerns whether third parties can inspect a developer’s most capable model without permission: all US developers now withhold this, while developers elsewhere, except Baidu, allow it. Drawing on the notion of a "polysemous compromise” and on practice-based accounts of norm formation, we argue that the meaning of openness is being settled not by states but in license files, largely outside the public eye.

Ismail H Warsame

Full text
Abstract Somalia’s political crisis is usually explained through clan competition, external intervention, and the weakness of its institutions. This paper points to a factor that gets less attention but helps sustain these failures: a pattern of political silence among elites and officials, paired with a growing public disregard for the country’s future. The paper traces this silence through the colonial period, the Siad Barre era, the state collapse of 1991, and the federal settlement of the past two decades. It draws on Somali customary values and conflict-resolution practice, including xeer, diya-paying groups, the guurti and shir, and the tradition of oblique speech, to show that Somali society has long possessed effective means of naming wrongs and restraining violence. The paper argues that the decline of these mechanisms at the national level, and their replacement by a political culture in which naming a violation carries a high personal price, is a central cause of the federal breakdown now under way. It also addresses the counterargument that silence has often been a rational strategy for survival, and it distinguishes strategic silence from the civic silence that erodes the conditions for agreement. Scholarly contributions. This paper makes four contributions to the study of Somali politics and African federalism. First, it separates strategic silence, a temporary and purposive restraint tied to negotiation, from civic silence, a durable failure to name violations of the public good, and it offers a test for distinguishing the two. Second, it defines political silence as an observable phenomenon with three dimensions and extends Hirschman’s exit, voice, and loyalty framework to a setting in which exit runs through clan and diaspora channels. Third, it treats customary restraint as both a resource and a liability: the discretion that settles disputes between kin can conceal violations when the cost falls on those excluded from the negotiation. Fourth, it brings xeer, the diya-paying group, and the guurti into federal analysis as inputs to institutional design. It takes Puntland, which the paper treats as the first pillar of Somali federalism, as its central case for testing when customary legitimacy supports a durable federal settlement, and it uses the Borama process in Somaliland as a comparison.

You Talking to Me? The Diverging Effects of Organizational Communication on the Gender Ambition Gap

Gulay Dogan, Aykut Öztürk

Full text
Women's underrepresentation in civic life is accompanied by a persistent ambition gap. Research attributes this gap partly to gendered socialization and unequal encouragement. How does organizational communication affect this gap, and can organizations narrow it by addressing women directly? We examine these questions through a pre-registered survey experiment with more than 7,000 members of a major Turkish public sector trade union confederation. Women and men were randomly assigned to a gender-neutral mobilization video, a video that addressed women directly and exclusively, or a placebo. Forced digitalisation, the withdrawal or downgrading of offline channels, makes going online an obligation that many older adults do not experience as a choice. During the lockdowns, family contact also required going online. Research on proxy internet use and non-use shows that many older adults meet this obligation through others, but says little about how their stance towards it relates to their use, or how that relation varies across situations. We classify how older adults respond to that obligation. We draw on 44 interviews with older adults at day centres in Chile's Metropolitan Region, analysed thematically and coded by episode: one response to the obligation in one situation. Full text

Choices made in dangerous environments accumulate into a range of political outcomes, from refugee crises and mass riots to civilian resilience during conflict. I argue that decision-makers' perceptions of control and certainty influence preferences for dealing with violence, and that those perceptions can carry over from unrelated sources. Sean Gailmard, Andrew T. Little, Carlo Prato

Full text
We study how electoral institutions affect incentives for elected elites to resist democratic backsliding by a chief executive or party leader. Conventional wisdom suggests that party-centered systems, such as list PR with centralized candidate selection, should weaken co-partisan resistance by making legislators more vulnerable to the leader’s retaliation. Using a formal model, we show that this logic is incomplete: legislators do not solely respond to the electoral cost of resisting but also to the leader’s capacity to retaliate after successful resistance. In candidate-centered systems such as single-member districts with primaries, leaders may still be able to punish dissenters after removal. In party-centered systems, punishment is more severe if the leader survives but weaker if resistance succeeds, in which case the leader loses control over the party. So, party-centered systems produce stronger complementarities in the incentive to resist. We show that equilibrium resistance decreases with the effectiveness of punishment (highest under closed-list PR), but increases with its contingency on the leader’s own survival (highest under centralized candidate selection). As a result, party-centered institutions can generate more co-partisan resistance and offer stronger protection against backsliding.

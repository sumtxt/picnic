A better nudge definition for behavioral public policy

Avishalom Tor

Full text
Despite their increasing deployment worldwide, behavioral interventions—frequently referred to as nudges—remain ill-defined. Thaler and Sunstein’s original definition has been more honored in the breach than the observance, not only by its authors but also by much of the expansive literature that followed. Exasperated by the ongoing proliferation of nudge usages, behavioral public policy (BPP) researchers have offered substitute or complementary terms that have experienced varying degrees of adoption but ultimately only increased BPP’s conceptual confusion. So much so, in fact, that one leading scholar has recently advocated abandoning such definitions altogether. The popularity of the nudge terminology is unlikely to abate any time soon, however, and clear definitions are necessary for coherent usage and analysis. Therefore, this paper advances a better definition that detaches nudges from their convoluted libertarian paternalistic origins. This straightforward, descriptive, definition offers conceptual clarity and a more functional basis for the systematic analysis and implementation of actual behavioral interventions. It does so by emphasizing what differentiates nudges from all other interventions—namely, that they appear primarily to use behavioral processes to advance their policy goals. This definition sharply distinguishes nudges from other regulatory interventions, even when the latter are non-coercive (like traditional information disclosures) or behaviorally informed (e.g., “sin” taxes). By delineating nudges based exclusively on their behavioral mechanisms, this definition also highlights what unifies them as a distinct instrument class, irrespective of nudges’ diverse policy goals and regardless of whether they comport with or violate any normative standard.

Self-interest within the Advocacy Coalition Framework: how material beliefs affect change in German munitions policy

Alexander Pechmann, Jochen Hinkel

Full text
We use the concept of material beliefs to integrate self-interest into the Advocacy Coalition Framework (ACF), providing new insights into collaboration between actors with material and purposive beliefs and transitions between subsystem types. Focusing on policy regarding the 1.6 million tons of sea-dumped munitions from the Second World War in German seas, which pose significant environmental and safety challenges, we applied a qualitative mixed-methods approach, combining interviews with desk reviews. Our findings reveal that material beliefs, such as financial profit and political power, initially sustained policy stasis within a unitary subsystem. A decade later, material beliefs enabled policy change, when aligned with purposive beliefs like environmental protection and long-term societal benefits. Contrary to the ACF hypothesis that purposive actors are more constrained in expressing beliefs, we find that both actor types are more constrained when expressing beliefs of their belief type and more flexible when expressing beliefs of the other. We demonstrate that entrepreneurs leveraged the differing incentives, rooted in actors’ deep core beliefs, to align policy core beliefs and foster collaboration. The alignment reflected asymmetric policy learning: material coalitions revised their policy core beliefs in response to shifting opportunities, while the purposive coalition adjusted secondary beliefs, suggesting greater stability but lower flexibility. Our findings challenge the ACF assumption that collaborative subsystems develop only when opposing coalitions collaborate with each other. We demonstrate an alternative theoretical pathway, in which collaboration emerges through the dissolution of one coalition, the participation of new actors, and the alignment of beliefs across coalitions.

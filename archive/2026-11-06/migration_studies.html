Bridging the Migration-Climate Divide: Toward Climate (Im-)Mobility Research 3.0

Andréas Litsegård, Karsten Paerregaard, Ibrahim Wahab, Elizabeth Carper, Gyanu Maskey, Truphena Mukuna, Deliang Chen, Julia Curio, Xiaoye Yang, Anders Burman

Full text
Over the past decade, a growing body of empirical and theoretical research has examined the relationship between climate change and human (im-)mobility. Yet this literature remains fragmented geographically, methodologically, and theoretically, particularly in how it conceptualizes and integrates the multiple drivers shaping mobility outcomes. Despite growing recognition of the multicausal nature of climate-related (im-)mobility, few studies have developed genuinely integrative interdisciplinary approaches that bridge climate research and the social sciences. This article addresses this gap by advancing a research agenda capable of analyzing how climatic and non-climatic drivers interact to shape people's livelihoods and decisions to move or stay in a changing climate. Its contribution is twofold. First, it critically reviews existing analytical frameworks to assess how they conceptualize interactions among mobility drivers, their relationship to climate change, and the ways in which climate change reshapes mobility dynamics. Second, building on this review, it advances an interdisciplinary climate (im-)mobility research agenda 3.0 that gives equal analytical attention to mobility and immobility and integrates natural and social science perspectives, methods, and data. As a first step towards operationalizing this agenda, the article proposes a novel application of Factors Constellation Analysis (FCA), an approach originally developed in health sciences. Adapted to climate (im-)mobility research, FCA provides a flexible analytical framework for systematic examination of how various constellations of climatic, environmental, demographic, social, cultural, political, and economic factors interact to produce different (im-)mobility outcomes.

Humanitarian Migrants’ Non-Metropolitan Settlement and Subsequent Internal Migration

Cho Yat Wong, Aude Bernard, Tomasz Zając, Charles Siriban, Jing Wu, Francisco Perales

Full text
To accommodate the influx of humanitarian migrants and address rural depopulation, many countries promote non-metropolitan settlement amongst incoming humanitarian migrants. Yet many humanitarian migrants subsequently leave their initial non-metropolitan settlement locations, which raises questions about the effectiveness of non-metropolitan settlement policies and the factors influencing humanitarian migrants’ relocation decisions. Within this context, this research note leverages Australian population-level administrative data from the Person Level Integrated Data Asset (PLIDA) to generate novel insights. Specifically, we analyse aggregate data comprising 3.4 million annual bilateral internal migration flows between 87 local labour markets for humanitarian migrants from different origin countries over the 2011–2019 period, comparing their patterns to those for the general Australian population and other migrant groups. Our findings indicate that non-metropolitan areas experience net losses of humanitarian migrants to metropolitan areas, and that humanitarian migrants relocate to regions with greater employment opportunities, better education options, and larger co-ethnic networks. Importantly, the patterns and determinants of internal migration for humanitarian migrants often differ to those observed for other migrant groups as humanitarian migrants are less responsive to labour- and housing-market conditions, but more responsive to co-ethnic networks. Altogether, our results highlight the difficulty of stimulating non-metropolitan population growth via humanitarian migration, with implications for other receiving countries.

Journal of Refugee Studies

‘We sleep with the head inside, but the brain outside’: local integration in resettlement’s shadow

Manya Oriel Kagan, Maria Nagawa, Noa Rubinstein

Full text
How do refugees perceive resettlement policy, and how do these perceptions influence local integration outcomes? In this article, we argue that refugees experience UNHCR’s three durable solutions—integration, resettlement, and repatriation—as interdependent rather than separate pathways. We investigate our argument in Uganda, drawing on in-depth qualitative data from twenty key informant interviews and five focus group discussions with thirty-four participants conducted in Kampala and two refugee settlements. Our findings reveal that refugees navigate resettlement and local integration policy domains concurrently and that their perceptions of one solution shape their engagement with others. Moreover, even though refugees cannot apply for resettlement, they proactively pursue resettlement and make decisions they believe might help their cases. This dynamic is fueled by an information environment where unclear eligibility criteria and limited transparency result in a reliance on informal information networks. The findings not only highlight the costs of siloing refugee protection solutions but also demonstrate how refugees proactively navigate policy spaces.

Population, Space and Place

Spatial Restructuring, Labour Reallocation, and Household Income: Evidence From Poverty Alleviation Resettlement in China

Wei Ouyang, Xin Qi, Mengmin Sun, Ziyang Chen, Yixiao Zhu, Yang Zhou

Full text
China's poverty alleviation resettlement (PAR) programme provides an opportunity to examine how state‐led population redistribution reshapes rural household livelihoods. Drawing on 186,705 household‐year observations from Fuping County eastern China, between 2014 and 2022, we estimate a two‐way fixed‐effects difference‐in‐differences model and conduct robustness checks that account for staggered treatment timing. PAR improves access to several public services but increases the distance between homes and cultivated land. It is associated with an approximately 1.1% increase in household per‐capita net income. Mechanism‐consistent estimates indicate that PAR increases non‐agricultural employment, with the employment response concentrated within the resettlement township rather than at more distant locations. PAR also increases the annual duration of non‐agricultural employment. Income gains are larger among female‐headed households and households with skilled workers, but smaller among households with higher shares of older members. Income outcomes also vary by resettlement destination and type of post‐relocation support. By distinguishing sectoral labour reallocation from geographical labour mobility, the study shows how state‐led population redistribution can facilitate economic mobility through highly localised labour‐market integration without necessarily increasing long‐distance migration. It contributes to population geography by demonstrating that the developmental consequences of relocation depend on how access to employment, services and productive land is reorganised, as well as on households’ differing capacities to take advantage of these opportunities. Our findings demonstrate that PAR achieves spatial restructuring, labor reallocation, livelihood strategy reshaping, and livelihood sustainability, and represents an important initiative for eliminating long‐term (chronic) poverty resulting from geo‐environmental constraints.

International Migration

Navigating Social Sustainability in Refugee Camps Through Transect Walks

Anwar Kwaylih, Lara Alshawawreh

Full text
This paper provides a comparative study of the Zaatari and Azraq refugee camps in Jordan, examining how current planning of the built form, layout and design influences social sustainability indicators within the camps. The aim is to examine and identify key socio‐spatial factors that either support or undermine social sustainability in both camps using transect walks. Generally, results suggest that the macro‐scale, characterized by low camp homogeneity and a chaotic path system, was associated with higher fear of crime and lower perception of safety, but was perceived to support higher access to services, amenities and facilities. At the meso‐scale, the urban form corresponds with weaker community networking and social interactions but facilitates social participation and community engagement. At the micro‐scale, characterized by larger shelter areas resulting from appropriation schemes, it promotes a higher sense of pride and place attachment and supports higher levels of everyday agency and a sense of permanence. The findings aim to inform architects, urban planners and inter‐governmental agencies on how spatial strategies can promote social sustainability in the context of planned refugee camps, advocating a comprehensive approach that creates empowering living environments for refugees.

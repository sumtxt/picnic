American Political Science Review

Trade Interdependence in the Modern Global Economy

MICHAEL-DAVID MANGINI, STEPHEN CHAUDOIN

Full text
Analyzing global trade interdependence requires an approach that incorporates fundamental features of the modern economy: intense trade in intermediates and the changing availability of alternative partners and substitute products. We use theoretical models of structural gravity to construct measures of dyadic dependence that directly incorporate these features. The measures describe (1) how much trade interruptions damage a country’s welfare and (2) how much additional trade it would take from their existing trade partners to compensate. We then show three important findings. First, the new measures differ from traditional measures based on aggregate trade statistics. Second, they show how, contrary to much common wisdom, the world has not marched inexorably toward greater interdependence. Third, we use China’s foreign assistance, as exemplified by the Belt and Road Initiative, to assess the consequences of one major government policy. Chinese assistance has increased partners’ dependence on China, without a reciprocal increase in Chinese dependence.

Councils and Indirect Rule in British Africa

JUTTA BOLT, LEIGH GARDNER, JENNIFER KÖHLER, JACK PAINE, JAMES A. ROBINSON

Full text
Did Western colonial rule transform African political institutions? Despite extensive research on indirect rule, we have little systematic evidence about how colonizers’ aims interacted with the structure of indigenous institutions to shape local governance. We explain why colonizers faced incentives to delegate authority to traditionally legitimate institutions, even in historically decentralized areas. Empirically, we analyze originally compiled data on African political institutions in the precolonial and colonial periods for more than 450 subnational units across British Africa. We focus on councils as a form of executive constraints. Subnational councils were widespread, exhibited strong correlations with precolonial institutions and patterns of socioeconomic development, and exerted meaningful decision-making powers. These indirect-rule institutions reflected reforms to replace ineffective installed agents. Pressure from below prompted British officials to reintroduce systems of executive constraints based on precolonial models. Our findings reject widespread claims that colonizers could unilaterally implement indirect-rule institutions while disregarding precolonial precedents.

When Business Speaks, Legislators Listen: Experimental and Qualitative Evidence from Brazil

FERNANDO B. MELLO

Full text
The influence of interest groups on policy making is a central concern in representative democracies, yet identifying causal effects remains a significant challenge. This article argues that legislators respond to informational cues about the economic importance and coalition breadth of interest groups. A field experiment in Brazil, which randomized the receipt of printed and WhatsApp messages from a pro-reform business organization, reveals that legislators are more likely to vote in favor of the group’s position when its membership size, economic importance, and sectoral reach are emphasized. A survey experiment with lobbyists supports this mechanism, showing that messages highlighting group characteristics increase legislators’ expected engagement and support. In-depth interviews with key political figures—including a former president, ministers, and deputies—further corroborate these findings. This study contributes to the literature on informational lobbying and cue-based persuasion by employing realistic experimental treatments and mixed-methods evidence.

Retrieval-Augmented Surveys

Yamil Velez, Alec Ewig

Full text
We introduce retrieval-augmented surveys, a novel survey approach that links respondents’ open-ended priorities to concrete policy proposals in institutional databases, enabling personalized measures of how citizens’ concerns map onto formal agendas. Our pipeline elicits and clarifies each respondent’s top issue concern, summarizes items in a policy database and retrieves semantically relevant candidates (“nearest neighbors”) for inclusion in a respondent-tailored survey. We illustrate the approach with an application to political representation in Congress, evaluating performance across four metrics. First, large majorities of respondents judge the retrieved policy items as mostly or fully addressing their concerns. Second, a hand-coding audit corroborates the alignment of retrieved items with respondent issue stances. Third, support for retrieved items is stable over a short-term panel, with 82% of respondents at the top of the support scale remaining there three weeks later. Fourth, in a tailored conjoint, alignment on the nearest neighbor item has a larger estimated effect on candidate choice than benchmark policies. The framework is modular and portable to other settings, such as rulemaking, municipal agendas, and public budgets, enabling scholars to identify where representation succeeds or fails in the institutional pipeline.

Euclidean Ideal Point Estimation from Roll-Call Data via Distance-Based Bipartite Network Models

Seungju Lee, In Kyun Kim, Jong Hee Park, Ick Hoon Jin

Full text
Conventional ideal point models rely on Gaussian or quadratic utility functions that violate the triangle inequality, producing non-metric distances that complicate geometric interpretation and undermine clustering and dispersion-based analyses. We introduce a distance-based alternative that adapts the latent space item response model (LSIRM) to roll-call data, treating legislators and bills as nodes in a bipartite network jointly embedded in a Euclidean metric space. Through controlled simulations, Euclidean LSIRM consistently recovers latent coalition structure with superior cluster separation relative to existing methods. Applied to the 118th U.S. House, the model provides competitive predictive performance while yielding bill embeddings that clarify cross-cutting issue alignments. The results show that restoring metric structure to ideal point estimation provides a clearer and more coherent inference about party cohesion, factional divisions and multidimensional legislative behavior.

How Accurate Are Survey Measurements of Objective Phenomena?

Lisanne Wichgers, Linchiat Chang, David Chapman, Elizabeth Elder, Jon Krosnick, Matt Berent, Amanda Scott, Elaine Albertson, Elizabeth Quinlan

Full text
How accurate are survey measurements of phenomena that can be objectively verified? This article reports a review of various types of evidence assessing accuracy (vs. benchmarks not derived from self-reports) across a wide spectrum of content areas, including demographics, healthcare, labor force statistics, substance use and more. The vast majority of measurements were accurate, according to three assessment methods. Individual respondents’ self-reports closely matched objective records of the same phenomena for the same individual. Small aggregate differences were observed between survey sample means and aggregate means derived from objective records. Self-reports correlated strongly with measures of the same attributes of those individuals from secondary data not based on self-reports. Taken together, the evidence suggests that survey measurements of objective phenomena are highly accurate.

An Image Is Worth K Topics: A Visual Structural Topic Model with Pretrained Image Embeddings

Matías Piqueras, Alexandra Segerberg, Matteo Magnani, Måns Magnusson, Nataša Sladoje

Full text
Political scientists are increasingly interested in analyzing visual content at scale. However, the existing computational toolbox is still in need of methods and models attuned to the specific challenges and goals of social and political inquiry. In this article, we introduce a visual structural topic model (vSTM) that combines continuous image embeddings with a structural topic model. This has important advantages compared to existing approaches. First, continuous embeddings allow the model to capture the semantic complexity of images, which is relevant to the study of politics. A consequence of using continuous embeddings is that we can directly use off-the-shelf pretrained deep learning models, offering state-of-the-art performance in image analysis tasks while saving time, cost and resources. Second, the structural topic model provides the ability to analyze how topics and covariates are related, while maintaining a nuanced representation of images as a mixture of multiple topics. In our experiments, we show that the vSTM is able to identify topics that are interpretable, coherent and relevant to the study of online political communication.

American Journal of Political Science

Women and the double standard of ambition

Geertje J. Bol

Full text
Few human qualities are as morally unstable as ambition: We seem unable to decide whether to admire or condemn the desire for self‐advancement. This instability guarantees double standards—and none has been more enduring than that of women's ambition. This article traces the emergence of this double standard in early modern England, when ambition came to be widely accepted as natural and even virtuous in men, yet remained unnatural and vicious in women. It then turns to four seventeenth‐century feminists—Rachel Speght, Margaret Cavendish, Mary Astell, and Judith Drake—who challenged this emerging double standard. They reimagined ambition as a feminist tool, using it to argue for women's equality and even superiority. Unearthing this neglected strand of ambitious feminism not only contributes to the history of political thought but also points the way to a reconciliation between feminism and ambition that avoids the pitfalls identified by feminist theorists today.

Can citizen election observation increase public confidence in US elections?

Jennie Barker, Oren Samet, Susan D. Hyde

Full text
Nonpartisan citizen election observation plays an important role in promoting the credibility of electoral processes around the world. This article assesses whether nonpartisan observation can shape attitudes among the US public, randomizing information about nonpartisan domestic observers in a series of survey experiments fielded to nearly 10,000 respondents. These included both hypothetical vignettes and information about a real‐world nonpartisan domestic observation mission undertaken by The Carter Center during the 2022 midterm elections in Fulton County, Georgia. The results show that information about positive assessments by nonpartisan observers of the hypothetical and Fulton County elections increased respondents' confidence in electoral results and processes, and also made respondents more likely to identify the officially announced winner over the candidate they favored as the candidate who won. Alternative ways of framing observers were largely inconsequential.

When Can Political Parties Credibly Change Their Policies?

Love Christensen, Pablo Fernandez-Vazquez

Full text
When can a party credibly change its policy on an issue? No institution forces parties to keep their promises after being elected. Voters may thus view platform shifts as mere pandering, discounting their credibility. We propose that voters use the direction of a platform shift as a signal of the party’s motives and policy commitment. Shifting to an unpopular position is a costly signal of principled motives, while moving toward a popular one suggests opportunistic ones. Two preregistered survey experiments, one evaluating a general setting and another focusing on a real case of mainstream party accommodation to the radical right, show that voters infer party motives and commitment from platform shifts in line with the predictions of our argument. Our study provides important insights into the electoral persistence of radical right parties, party system stability, and why platform shifts often fail to affect voter perceptions of party positions.

British Journal of Political Science

Youth Representation and Democratic Legitimacy

Aksel Sundström, Charles T. McClean, Daniel Stockemer

Full text
How does the age composition of democratic institutions shape public evaluations of their legitimacy? While most institutions are dominated by older politicians, we argue that incorporating younger representatives can enhance perceptions of legitimacy. To test this claim, we conduct a survey experiment in the United States that varies the age composition of a bipartisan state legislative committee and its decisions. We find that including younger members increases perceptions of procedural fairness and institutional trust, and improves evaluations of decisions on lower-salience policies. These effects are stronger among young people and Democrats. Evidence on mechanisms suggests these legitimacy gains reflect beliefs that younger politicians are more attuned to young people’s experiences and concerns. Observational evidence from US state legislatures and a replication experiment in the United Kingdom yield consistent patterns, suggesting that these findings extend beyond the original experimental context. This suggests that expanding youth representation can meaningfully strengthen democratic legitimacy.

Embodying Hope: Choreographies of Presence and Reiterative Endurance in Latin American Feminist Protest

Camila Páez-Bernal, Maša Mrovlje, Jennet Kirkpatrick

Full text
How is hope sustained under conditions of violence, loss, and political disappointment? Recent feminist and critical theorists have reimagined hope as entangled with grief and failure, yet this work remains largely focused on texts and representation. In this article, we develop a feminist theory of embodied hope grounded in Latin American feminist protest. Drawing on interviews and ethnographic and visual documentation from Colombia and Mexico (2022–23), we show how activists enact hope through what we call choreographies of presence: collective acts of public visibility, such as mourning, dance, mutual aid, and bodily exposure. We argue that these practices reframe feminist hope as a collective, democratic capacity for endurance grounded in return, relationality, and a refusal of closure. Rather than deferring hope to a future horizon, these movements sustain political life by returning to sites of loss and enduring together.

Comparative Politics and the New Area Studies

Jordan Gans-Morse, Daniel W. Gingerich, Thomas B. Pepinsky

Full text
In the 1990s, a sharp divide emerged between area studies advocates and methodologically oriented political scientists. We revisit that debate, arguing that tensions between political science and area studies are neither intrinsic nor static but instead evolve in tandem with theoretical and methodological trends, as well as with broader political and technological developments. Drawing on an original survey of American Political Science Association members and an analysis of roughly 4,500 comparative politics articles in leading journals, we identify four shifts in the discipline: from a theoretical to an empirical orientation, from cross-national datasets to country- and region-specific studies, from macro- to micro-level analyses, and from descriptive to causal inference. We also document evolving patterns in language training, fieldwork, methods use, and data collection. Our findings suggest that political science and area studies are experiencing a reconciliation, but that the state of area studies is fragile and the subdiscipline of comparative politics must support it.

Learning democracy: The lasting effects of political education on democratic support

Anja Neundorf, Roman-Gabriel Olar, Johannes Gerschewski, Rosalind Shorrocks, Natasha Lindstaedt

Full text
Do citizens need to grow up in a democracy to value it? We examine how school education – democratic or authoritarian – shapes long-term support for democracy. Leveraging a quasi-natural experiment in the former German Democratic Republic, where rigid school entry rules and abrupt reforms after reunification created exogenous variation in exposure to socialist schooling, we show that an additional year of such education significantly reduced democratic support in adulthood. To test generalizability, we analyze harmonized survey data from 106 countries alongside new expert-coded measures of educational content and state effort. The results reveal that democratic attitudes are shaped not only by the negative imprint of authoritarian schooling but crucially by the positive imprint of democratic civic education, especially when democracies invest heavily in it. Our findings demonstrate that democracy must be learned to be valued, underscoring the central role of education in democratic consolidation and resilience.

Distinction Without a Difference: American Public Opinion on Immigrants and Refugees

Alexander Tripp, Cindy D. Kam

Full text
Do Americans hold refugees in higher regard than immigrants? If so, why? We theorize that refugees activate humanitarian concerns and perceptions of deservingness, while immigrants trigger threat perceptions and competition, leading to divergent mental associations toward each group. We investigate these questions with a nationally representative online survey experiment fielded in 2024 that contrasts Americans’ attitudes toward refugees and immigrants using both open- and close-ended questions. Open-ended mentions reveal that refugees trigger themes of involuntariness, conflict, and humanitarianism, while immigrants elicit themes of domestic politics, fairness, and threat. Close-ended responses indicate that Americans view immigrants as more competent and more agentic than refugees, and they associate refugees and immigrants with different reasons for coming to the United States. Despite these differences in beliefs, respondents report no significant differences on warmth stereotypes, group affect, or policy preferences. Thus, while Americans do possess some divergent beliefs about refugees and immigrants, ultimately these are distinctions without a difference when it comes to group affect and policy opinions.

Cult of Personality or Lack of Competition? Determining Trump’s Sway Over Republican Partisans on a Critical Case

Love Christensen, Mattias Agerberg

Full text
It is well established that partisans follow their leaders. While this potentially has important implications for political accountability, studies of opinion leadership rarely examine accountability directly. We test whether partisans are able to hold a dominant leader accountable under favorable conditions using a critical case: Trump’s position on the Epstein files. Two preregistered experiments, fielded before the release of the Epstein files, show that partisans follow Trump and do not hold him accountable when he is unopposed or contested by the Democrats. However, when Trump faces opposition from within his own bloc—especially countermessaging from right-coded media elites—both issue-specific and overall approval decline substantially, and following is halved. Our results have two important implications. First, opinion leadership does not imply the absence of political accountability. Second, meaningful political competition in polarized societies may need to originate from within ideological blocs. While Trump is powerful, credible opposition can make him accountable.

Partisan responsiveness in real time: causal evidence from Trump’s military attack on Iran

Stuart J. Turnbull-Dugarte, Peter Luca Versteegen, Daniel Devine, Owen Winter

Full text
Elite cues signal citizens how to interpret political events through a partisan lens. Do these effects occur in real time? We exploit President Trump’s unexpected announcement of US military strikes against Iran during a nationally representative survey to estimate the causal effect of elite cues on public opinion. Leveraging minute-level variation in exposure, we find that partisan divergence emerges almost immediately: support for military intervention increases among Republicans but declines among Democrats. These large effects remain robust across 28,672 model specifications and 57,344 partisan-specific treatment effects. Our quasi-experimental evidence demonstrates that elite cues can polarize public opinion within minutes, even before media framing and partisan commentary have diffused, highlighting the speed of partisan opinion formation and its implications for democratic accountability.

Government ideology and gender equality in public sector career progression

Lasse Aaskoven, Benjamin C.K. Egerod, Frederik K. Kjøller

Full text
Why are women underrepresented in civil service management across rich democracies? We argue that government ideology matters for this question, and that women bureaucrats’ careers stagnate under right-wing governments, since they do not suppress gender-inequality-reinforcing promotion patterns. We examine this using administrative occupation data on all Danish central government employees, observed quarterly from 1998 to 2019. The evidence is consistent with our argument: Under left-wing governments, men and women in the civil service progress similarly, while women progress more slowly than men under right-wing governments. The gender gap in career progression widens at the higher echelons of the bureaucracy, where political attention is greater, and this gap is not reduced by the presence of women ministers.

Is alliance credibility interdependent?

Joshua Alley, Brian Blankenship

Full text
How might changes in the credibility of one alliance relate to the credibility of other alliance commitments? Debate between competing views on this question spans decades in part because systematic tests are difficult. We contribute systematic evidence with a new measure of credibility in U.S. alliances and novel technique to estimate interdependence. Our measure draws on expert ratings of allied beliefs over U.S. credibility and our analysis employs Gaussian process regression to analyze credibility. We find strong correlations in allied credibility perceptions within regions and little correlation across regions. Supplementary analyses suggest that these correlations reflect common unobservables and a small impact of shared perception of past U.S. behavior.

Educational polarization: evidence from the White House staff

Kenneth Lowande, Saloni Singh, Abigail Paegert, Alan Andrade

Full text
American political polarization has mapped onto educational pedigree, with credentialed elites increasingly supporting Democrats. We examine the implications of this trend in the most powerful office in American politics: the White House. From 1995 to 2024, we show Democratic presidents employed staffs with higher educational attainment, and that Republicans were more likely to attend public institutions, those without R1 status, and those ranked below the top 50%. We then show that universities whose alumni disproportionately went on to work for Democratic presidents were more likely to face investigations, enforcement actions, and funding cuts after President Trump took office in 2025. Our findings suggest educational pedigree among staff who operate key political institutions warrant further investigation.

Partisan social cognition and biased experiences in a polarized world: The neural processing of identity‐based group membership

Adam Moore, Sujin Hong, Cyril R. Pernet, Alexa M. Morcom, Neil Roberts, Agamemnon Krasoulis, Laura Cram

Full text
Pairing neuroimaging and behavioral testing, we offer new insights into affective polarization, hyper‐partisanship, and their effects. Our findings introduce a temporal dimension to existing theoretical frameworks concerning the impact of social identity on political perceptions. We demonstrate that group‐identity shapes in‐the‐moment neural processing of real‐world experiences in a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) protocol and Cyberball game where participants experience varying levels of inclusion/exclusion from in/out‐group members. In other words, people don't simply rely upon group membership (e.g., partisanship, national identity) to post‐hoc rationalize an experience, but instead that group membership also actively biases neural processing of otherwise objectively identical experiences, shaping (and distorting) political perception, with significant behavioral effects.

The rhythm of persuasion: Media channels, psychological priming, and voting decisions through political music in Nigeria

Ukamaka C. M. Ozioko, Success Esomchi Obi, Anibueze U. Anselm, Agaba Joseph David, Chinasa Emelda Nnanyelugo, Charles Chukwudi Eze, Jude Chukwudi Agbo, Gever Verlumun Celestine

Full text
The objective of this study was to determine the effect of exposure to political music on voting decisions among Nigerian voters. Data were collected from a multistage sample of 388 participants, and logistic and linear regression models were applied to evaluate the hypotheses. The findings revealed that an individual's overall exposure to political music significantly predicts their likelihood of voting, demonstrating a small positive effect size (Odds Ratio = 1.08). Rather than operating through indirect moderation pathways, exposure media and geographic location emerged as the strongest direct predictors of persuasive influence. Specifically, direct exposure via local radio ( β = .21) and shared audio files ( β = .12) significantly predicted a voter's influence score. Furthermore, rural voters reported being significantly more influenced by political music than their urban counterparts, with geographic location operating as the strongest overall direct baseline predictor ( β = −.48). The study concludes that the psychological effectiveness of political music lies not in generalized, blanket exposure, but in a targeted strategic focus that leverages specific media channels and subcultural demographic segments to maximize persuasive impact on voter behavior.

The critical role of political trust in the energy transition

Jordana W. Composto, Sofía Silvosa, Anandita Sabherwal, Elke U. Weber

Full text
Achieving net‐zero CO 2 emissions requires sustained collaboration between civil society and the private and public sectors across time and geographies, but low trust in and among these actors impedes coordinated action. This multidisciplinary review examines how different forms of trust, in particular political trust (i.e., trust between actors with differential power), shape the energy transition, addressing four core questions: How does trust impact energy project development and outcomes? How do the processes of building, maintaining, eroding, and repairing trust differ? What are the asymmetries in trust gain and loss? How does a project's history and stage determine which process is at play and whether trust repair is possible? A narrative literature review and analysis of fifteen case studies spanning diverse energy contexts identifies key trust dyads: government‐community, developer‐community, regulator‐developer. Mapping these trust dyads on project outcomes reveals when trust functions as either a barrier or a pathway to success. We complement our literature review and case analysis with primary data from stakeholder surveys and expert workshops that validate the real‐world relevance of trust research insights and identify the largest trust deficits and effective trust‐building strategies perceived by expert practitioners. Our findings across sources of evidence converge to show that political trust operates through distinct mechanisms across different actor relationships, with direct implications for project design, stakeholder engagement, and policy implementation. The review concludes by outlining future research directions and opportunities for cross‐disciplinary collaboration to address trust challenges in the energy transition.

Unrequited: Zero‐sum asymmetry in the political domain fuels affective polarization

Martino Ongis, David Kidd

Full text
Zero‐sum beliefs about political relationships—the conviction that one party's gains are inevitably offset by another party's losses—have often been cited as drivers of partisan conflicts. However, across five studies with diverse samples of U.S. residents ( n = 1383), four of which were preregistered, we find that overall zero‐sum endorsement shows weak and inconsistent associations with affective polarization. Instead, beliefs are frequently one‐sided: Many individuals endorse the idea that their political opponents' gains are harmful while rejecting the notion that their own gains come at others' expense. We refer to the strength of this outgroup‐ingroup imbalance as zero‐sum asymmetry . Across correlational and experimental studies, higher zero‐sum asymmetry reliably predicts heightened partisan animosity. At the same time, it is associated with higher civic efficacy and, in several samples, greater political participation and democratic commitment. Notably, symmetric zero‐sum endorsement is relatively rare compared to asymmetric patterns. Together, these findings highlight the directional structure of perceived political trade‐offs as a key contributor to affective polarization, with broader implications for how citizens interpret partisan competition and evaluate the health of democratic systems.

Beyond remembrance: Genocide commemoration as a lens for understanding collective victimhood and contemporary conflicts

Mengyao Li, Ani Grigoryan, Slieman Halabi, Anna Baumert

Full text
Genocide commemorations are a key societal mechanism through which collective victimhood is constructed and transmitted across generations, yet their implications for contemporary conflicts remain underexamined. We conducted parallel naturalistic longitudinal studies in Armenia around Armenian Genocide Remembrance Day and Israel around Holocaust Remembrance Day (Time 1), with a lower‐salience comparison 2 months later (Time 2). Across both studies, Armenian and Jewish‐Israeli participants reported heightened existential threat and reduced inclusive victim consciousness around Remembrance Day than at the post‐Remembrance Day assessment. Armenian participants also reported lower willingness to reconcile with Azerbaijan, while their attitudes toward Ukrainian victims of the Russia–Ukraine war did not differ significantly across time. In Israel, attitudes toward the conflict with Palestinians did not differ significantly across time, but participants reported greater willingness to help Ukrainian victims at Time 1. Time 1 indirect effect analyses further revealed competing pathways: greater personal involvement in commemoration was indirectly associated with more conflict‐supportive attitudes (via heightened threat, moral entitlement, and reduced inclusive victimhood) and with more conciliatory and prosocial attitudes (via heightened moral obligation). These findings highlight that genocide commemorations are linked to competing psychological processes, which may explain their divergent implications for intergroup relations, a tension discussed in the paper.

Research & Politics

Political institutions and public opinion on abortion decisions post-Dobbs

Jeong Hyun Kim, Nichole Bauer, Anna Gunderson

Full text
At a time when state institutions are increasingly setting abortion policy through different channels, understanding which decision-making processes the public views as legitimate has crucial consequences for democratic governance and public trust in democratic institutions. We argue that in the post-Dobbs political climate, public support has shifted away from court-issued abortion decisions toward those made through voter referendums, driven by higher perceived procedural fairness in direct democracy. We fielded two unique survey experiments, one before the Dobbs decision removing the federal constitutional right for pregnant people to seek an abortion, and one after the decision. Pre-Dobbs, we find few differences in citizens’ perceptions of abortion decisions made by state courts, legislatures, or referendums. Post-Dobbs, we find that people evaluate both pro-abortion and anti-abortion decisions more favorably when decided through voter referendums. Our results hold critical implications for policies on abortion in the post-Dobbs landscape, but also for policy formation and evaluation on other contentious public policy issues.

European Political Science Review

How Europeans view and evaluate democracy at the European level

Hanspeter Kriesi, Ben Crum, Chendi Wang

Full text
This exploratory study asks how Europeans view EU democracy and how satisfied they are with how EU democracy works. Roughly one-fifth of them do not appear to have any idea about democracy at the EU level. We create a typology of European democrats distinguishing between allegiant, delegative, assertive, and disaffected democrats, show the distribution over the types, and analyze the factors associated with its two dimensions and the types. Citizens’ opinions on EU democracy deviate most notably from their views on national democracy in that much fewer of them want to expand it. As expected, pro-Europeans demand more democracy and are more satisfied with EU democracy than Euroskeptics. Feeling represented at the EU level and trusting EU institutions most strongly enhances demand for and satisfaction with EU democracy. In general, Europeans have clearer ideas about how democracy works in the EU than about how they want it to work.

Hezbollah’s Communication Strategy: Emotional Appeals for Cohesion and Resistance

Stephanie Dornschneider-Elkink, Sofia Quaglia, Luke Horton

Full text
While research has traditionally linked resistance to negative emotions like anger and frustration, a growing body of work now emphasises the importance of positive emotions such as trust and hope. This article analyses Hezbollah’s speeches from 2001 to 2023, focusing on anger and trust while incorporating positive and negative affect. Using quantitative methods to detect emotions and affect in natural language, our findings reveal that positive affect – often overlooked in resistance studies – is expressed most frequently, followed by trust, negativity and anger. Positivity and trust appear in discussions of Hezbollah’s in-group and ideology (Islam), while negativity and anger surface in discourse on violent resistance and regional security. These findings challenge Western media portrayals that associate Hezbollah with hate and negativity, suggesting that Hezbollah uses positivity and trust to reinforce cohesion, while leveraging negativity and anger to rally support for resistance activities.

Ballots over Barrels: Oil, Democracy and Conflict in Guyana

Geoffrey Swenson, Moritz Schmoll

Full text
The political resource curse posits that newly resource-rich states risk experiencing authoritarianism and conflict. This article examines Guyana as a most-likely case of that phenomenon. Drawing on interviews and quantitative indicators, we find that oil has not driven the recent democratic backsliding and rising geopolitical tensions. Instead, Guyana’s democratic fragility stems from winner-take-all electoral institutions in a context of deep ethnic polarisation and major parties’ limited normative commitment to democracy. Regarding conflict, Venezuela’s domestic politics, not oil, is the key driver of regional tensions. We therefore argue that the political resource curse framework may at times obscure more than it reveals, misattributing long-standing political tensions to resource wealth. Domestic and international political dynamics may be better understood as ‘politics with resources’ than politics cursed by oil. Promoting electoral reform, fostering stronger commitment to democratic norms within political parties and ensuring credible deterrence may therefore matter more than technocratic resource governance.

African Youth and the Future of Democracy: How Social Media and Cohort Size Shape Political Participation

Godfred Bonnah Nkansah, Christine Hackenesch

Full text
Despite their demographic size and growing political influence, African youth remain understudied in research on political participation. This article examines how two structural shifts, youth cohort size and social media use, independently and jointly shape electoral and protest behaviour across 40 African countries. Using Afrobarometer data (2016–2023) and complementary contextual datasets, we find that individual socio-economic factors remain the strongest predictors of participation, but demographic structure and digital engagement also matter. Larger youth cohorts are associated with a greater likelihood of voting. Social media use is connected with increases in protest participation while reducing the likelihood of voting, and its interaction with youth cohort size shapes both behaviours in important, non-linear ways. These findings challenge assumptions that youthful populations inherently drive unrest and show that digital mobilisation is strongly conditioned by the demographic context. Our study has significant implications for democratic legitimacy and youth inclusion in Africa’s rapidly changing political environments.

The Epistemic Double Bind of Parity Representation in France’s National Assembly

Ragnhild Louise Muriaas, Marianne Tøraasen

Full text
As legislatures approach gender parity in different parts of the world, questions remain about whether institutional cultures support equal epistemic authority for women. In the French National Assembly, modern republicanism promotes depersonalized representation: deputies are expected to embody the universal citizen, with gender embedded but not defining. Drawing on 24 interviews with deputies elected before and after the 2017 elections, this article develops a feminist institutionalist framework to show how women navigate tensions between republican expectations that demand conformity to masculinized neutrality and pluralist norms that confine authority to “women’s issues.” We introduce the concept of epistemic double bind to theorize this dilemma and present a typology of role expectations and strategic responses that captures how deputies negotiate it. Our findings show that even in more gender-balanced legislatures, women face enduring constraints on epistemic authority. France serves as a critical case for understanding the practical demands of depersonalized representation and how actors reconfigure its contradictions in institutions shaped by parity laws.

Agrarian revolt or populist disruption? The breakthrough of the farmer-citizen-movement (BBB) in the Netherlands

Simon Otjes, Thereza Langeler, Léonie de Jonge

Full text
Founded in 2019, the Dutch Farmer-Citizen-Movement ( BoerBurgerBeweging , BBB) has emerged as a significant force in Dutch politics. In a highly urbanized country, the rise of an explicitly rurally oriented political party is striking. We analyze the origins of the BBB, seeking to explain its electoral breakthrough. Using the frameworks of agrarianism and agrarian populism, we assess whether the BBB represents a revival of either tradition. Using qualitative and quantitative methods, we examine the party’s rhetoric and voter base in its founding years. Our findings indicate that the BBB adapted agrarian populist ideological tenets to fit the 21 st century context in which it operated. It initially mobilized around opposition to environmental policy and regional resentment, while valorizing rural life and expressing distrust of political elites. The BBB illustrates how populism can be reshaped through agrarian concerns, deepening the cleavage between the urban center and the rural periphery.

Campaigning on emotion: Party strategy and emotional appeals in the 2024 UK general election

Iván Sánchez-Marañón, Jordi Rodríguez-Virgili, Miguel Gómez-Irimia

Full text
Purpose: This article examines how political parties used emotional appeals in campaign advertising during the 2024 United Kingdom general election. Methods: Through content analysis of ads published by the Conservative Party, Labour, the Liberal Democrats, and Reform UK across their main social media platforms, we identify party-level patterns in emotional strategy and the conditions shaping their use. Findings: The Conservative Party relied heavily on fear, consistent with its trailing incumbent status. Labour led on anger, directing appeals almost exclusively at the Conservative governing record, while also deploying enthusiasm around the promise of change. Reform UK and the Liberal Democrats made the most frequent use of enthusiasm. Across all parties, enthusiasm targeted the ingroup, while anger and fear targeted the outgroup. Conclusions: These findings align with theoretical expectations derived from incumbency status and poll position across a full field of competing parties in a single campaign, providing evidence that electoral periods fuel affective polarisation.

Keep the peace? The early politics of Covid-19 and the limits of partisan influence on public opinion

Franziska Graf, Alexa Lenz, Christian Adam

Full text
Crises often call for swift and far-reaching government action that depends on broad public acceptance to be effective. While political disagreement is commonly seen as undermining support, its actual impact on public attitudes remains contested. This study examines public opinion during the COVID-19 pandemic in Germany – a context that shifted from early cross-party unity to later politicization. Combining original media data on partisan messaging and panel survey data from 2020, we assess how the emergence of partisan criticism shaped public acceptance of pandemic response policies. Although increasing partisan politicization was associated with declining public acceptance of pandemic measures, we find no evidence that this relationship was driven by partisan cue-taking. Instead, citizens adjusted their party preferences rather than their policy views in response to disagreement with their preferred party. These findings highlight the limits of partisan persuasion, and the growing importance of citizens’ own evaluations as elite consensus erodes.

The Invention of Liberalism

Annelien de Dijn

Full text
Today, it is commonplace to claim that liberalism created the modern world. This paper examines how and why the rise-of-liberalism narrative first emerged by examining textbooks and surveys on the history of political thought published in Britain and the United States between 1890 and the 1990s. In doing so, it shows how, in the interwar period, a new conception of “liberalism” was invented, imagining it as a secular emancipatory movement that shaped the modern world in its image. Second, this paper highlights how this master narrative was created by liberalism’s self-professed enemies—by Marxists and conservative thinkers—as much as by its friends, to serve different polemical purposes. Finally, this paper offers some reflections on the implications of this account of the historiography of liberalism for its contemporary defenders. It concludes that liberals themselves might have good reason to relinquish overblown claims about the importance of liberalism in the making of the modern world.

Power from the People: How Autocrats Use Referendums to Generate Support for Controversial Policies

Jody LaPorte, Ben Noble

Full text
Why do autocrats put their initiatives to a public vote? Scholars have only recently turned their attention to authoritarian referendums as a “nominally democratic institution.” Focusing on “voluntary referendums”—cases when rulers hold a public vote despite not being constitutionally required to do so—we advance two claims: first, that autocrats sometimes stage public votes to proclaim nominal popular approval for controversial policies; and second, that organizing such votes can help generate support for these initiatives. We argue that authoritarian referendums are more than discrete voting moments. Instead, they are complex institutions with important upstream components that rulers can exploit. We illustrate this argument using evidence from the Russian 2020 public vote on constitutional amendments, while also showing its applicability across other autocracies.

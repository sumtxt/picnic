American Sociological Review

Who Benefits from Citizenship? Varying Effects of Naturalization Decisions in the Early-Twentieth-Century United States

Peter Catron, Nathan I. Hoffmann, Mingze Li

Full text
Citizenship acquisition is widely viewed as a mechanism for improving economic outcomes, yet little is known about whether its benefits are distributed evenly. This article examines the varied returns to naturalization in the early-twentieth-century United States, assessing whether naturalization uniformly benefits all immigrants or is especially impactful for those already positioned to profit. Using naturalization records from New York City’s Southern District in the early 1900s, we construct a novel historical dataset for 1,947 immigrants who declared their intent to naturalize. By linking these records to U.S. census data from 1920, 1930, and 1940, we are able to observe immigrants’ characteristics at the point when they make naturalization decisions and to track their outcomes over time. Our findings reveal that immigrants who completed the naturalization process attained significantly higher occupational status than those who initiated but did not complete naturalization. In addition, citizenship is most transformative for those facing the greatest disadvantages. Additional analyses corroborate these findings in linked full-count census data for the entire United States. Citizenship confers occupational advantages not only through formal rights but as a resource that enables claims-making in the labor market, with greater returns to less advantaged immigrants.

Population and Development Review

Capturing Demographic Burden Over Time with the Cumulative Dependency Ratio

Guillaume Marois, Wolfgang Lutz, Klaus Prettner

Full text
The age dependency ratio is widely used to summarize population structure and to inform discussions of economic potential and population aging. However, as a period measure, it reflects the demographic balance at a single point in time and is highly sensitive to the timing of fertility and mortality change. This paper introduces the cumulative dependency ratio, a longitudinal reinterpretation of the traditional indicator that aggregates person‐years lived in dependent and support ages over a specified horizon. By explicitly incorporating the temporal dimension, the cumulative dependency ratio provides a person‐year‐based summary of demographic burden and facilitates comparison across periods, regions, and demographic scenarios. Using examples for the United States alongside comparative analyses across world regions, the paper illustrates how a cumulative perspective smooths short‐term demographic fluctuations, reduces sensitivity to the choice of observation year, and reframes the relationship between population structure and long‐run economic performance. Viewed through this cumulative framework, future fertility changes primarily affect the timing of dependency rather than its cumulative magnitude, and long‐run differences in demographic burden across regions appear smaller than implied by period measures. The cumulative dependency ratio complements existing indicators by providing an interpretable summary of sustained demographic pressures and intergenerational balance.

Is There a Democratization of Joint Physical Custody? Evidence From Europe

Anne Solaz, Eija Lindroos, Carole Bonnet, Mia Hakovirta, Daniel R. Meyer, Milla Salin

Full text
Highlights Joint physical custody remains strongly stratified by education, even in European countries where JPC is widespread. Medium-educated parents catch up with highly educated parents where JPC prevalence is high. Low-educated parents remain less likely to use JPC, regardless of its prevalence across countries. JPC use with young children remains lower, even in countries where shared care is widespread. Flexible forms of JPC (unequal share) are more common in low-prevalence countries.

Nicolás Soler, Tom Emery, Agnieszka Kanas

Full text
Highlights Register data enable researchers to study support availability in country-wide family networks. Survey data can be linked to the register networks to study actual support. Network structure is associated with self-reported availability of family support. Family networks extensively overlap because relatives are present in multiple families.

Parental Separation and the Quality of Ties to Extended Kin

Kateryna Sytkina, Thomas Leopold

Full text
Highlights Parental separation involves reorganization at all levels of the family system. Young adults who experienced parental separation have weaker ties to extended kin. These ties are especially weaker on the paternal side. The gaps are smaller in cases of joint physical custody after parental separation.

Transforming Manhood: Women's Employment and Other Sociodemographic Predictors of Testosterone in American Men

Andrew Francis-Tan

Full text
Highlights This investigation of the predictors of testosterone in American men focuses on the integration of women in the labor market. Men's exposure to women in the workplace is inversely associated with testosterone levels. Other variables significantly predict testosterone, for example, age, race and ethnicity, and marriage. Our results shed light on population-level declines in testosterone worldwide.

Evidence on the Robustness of the Links Between Social Relationships and Mortality

Rosanne Freak-Poli, Stephen P. Jenkins, Michael A. Shields, Trong-Anh Trinh

Full text
Highlights New evidence is provided on the robust links between social relationships and mortality. Analysis uses 19 waves of Australian longitudinal data for individuals aged 50+. Functional and structural social relationships each independently predict mortality. Loneliness is most strongly associated with a higher mortality risk. Investing in social relationships would help protect against mortality risks.

Marriage and Premarital Cohabitation Timing for LGB Adults in the United States: A Research Note

Jessica Noblitt, Miles G. Taylor, Wendy D. Manning, Claire Kamp-Dush

Full text
Highlights Gay and lesbian adults cohabit longer before marriage and marry at older ages than heterosexual adults. Bisexual women in same-sex relationships marry nearly six years later and cohabit about 1.5 years longer before marriage than bisexual women in different-sex relationships, who marry younger than any other group. Age at cohabitation varies little across groups, and differences by partner sex among bisexual men are not statistically significant.

The Short-Term Fertility Impact of Abortion Law Restrictions: A Research Note

Anna Matysiak, Lucas van der Velde

Full text
Highlights Poland's 2020 abortion ruling led to a sharp 6% drop in births 37 weeks later. The decline exceeded the number of legal abortions previously performed. The decline was strongest among younger and childless women. Abortion bans may reduce fertility when they heighten risks of childbearing.

Trends and Patterns in Childlessness by Age, Race and Ethnicity, and Education in the United States: A Research Note

Benjamin-Samuel Schlüter, Leslie Root, Monica Alexander

Full text
Highlights We present a Bayesian parametric model to estimate the percentage of childless U.S. women across age. There have been substantial changes to childbearing trajectories for women born in 1950–1999. The share of women who are childless has increased across most ages in younger cohorts. This increase has not yet had substantial effects on the share of women without children at age 45. Young cohorts would need to experience significant rises in births in the 30s to match earlier cohorts’ low childlessness at age 45.

Social Forces

Music divisions and cultural hierarchy in China: how transnational, political, and historical forces shape symbolic boundaries

Gordon C Li

Full text
This study maps the division of music in China and examines the mechanisms underlying its formation. Methodologically, it introduces multidimensional item response theory (MIRT), a latent-trait approach rarely used in cultural sociology, to identify the social divisions of taste before examining their social stratification through regression analysis. The findings reveal six taste profiles: high-status traditional music; pop music stratified into middlebrow Hong Kong and Taiwan classics, unstratified mainstream Chinese pop, and low-status local music; and foreign music divided into high-status selective forms and unstratified trendy foreign pop. These divisions do not cleanly align with genre categories, a highbrow–lowbrow hierarchy, or omnivore–univore patterns in established accounts. Instead, they reveal dual forms of high-status taste—state-consecrated heritage and cosmopolitan selective foreign pop—reflecting a multidimensional structure organized around region of origin, historical prestige, cohort, and popularity. More broadly, this study reveals how cultural stratification in China extends beyond Bourdieusian models centered on domestic field competition to encompass global flows, state-directed legitimation, and historical local trajectories. These findings extend theories of cultural distinction beyond the assumptions derived from dominant Western liberal-market societies and offer a framework for understanding how cultural hierarchies are constituted across wider national contexts.

Workplace authority in China: gender, parenthood, and work sectors

Manlin Cai

Full text
Workplace authority is a key individual and organizational resource to which women typically have more limited access than men. Yet, less is known about how parenthood shapes this gender disparity and how the relationship varies across employment regimes. Drawing on nationally representative, longitudinal data from the China Family Panel Studies (2010–2022), I examine within-person changes in women’s and men’s access to supervisory authority following parenthood, with particular attention to variation across the state and private sectors. Fixed-effects results show that, overall, women’s likelihood of holding authority declines after becoming mothers, especially after having two or more children, whereas men experience little change. These dynamics differ by work sector. A considerable motherhood penalty is concentrated in the private sector, whereas a fatherhood premium emerges only in the state sector. Parenthood therefore widens the gender gap in authority in both sectors through distinct patterns. These findings suggest that employment regimes condition the gendered meanings of parenthood and how they translate into access to authority. In China, the state sector’s formal protections may buffer motherhood-related disadvantages, whereas its male-dominated hierarchy and pronatalist agendas may sustain fatherhood-related rewards.

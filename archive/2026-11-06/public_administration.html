An Informational Letter From the Federal Government Increases Naturalization Submissions

Oded Gurantz, Amanda Girard, Elisa Cascardi, Sofia Avila, Tomás R. Jiménez

Full text
Citizenship offers enhanced opportunities for immigrants, but the complex naturalization process can be a barrier. We evaluate a preregistered, large‐scale randomized trial that tested the impact of a low‐cost letter that combined informational content with active encouragement from the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) to potentially eligible Lawful Permanent Residents (LPRs) to apply for naturalization. Within a nationwide population of 1,861,178 individuals in LPR status for 6 to 9 years, we find that the letter increased naturalization applications by 1 percentage point (12%). This increase was positive across all groups tested, though the effects were larger for families in higher income zip codes and those who did not submit the fee waiver application, suggesting that financial and time constraints, along with administrative burdens, play a role in naturalization submission rates.

The Effect of Clients’ Financial Resources, Mental Health, and Motivation on Frontline Workers’ Burden Reduction and Prioritizations

Niels Bjørn Grund Petersen, Jonas Krogh Madsen

Full text
The street-level bureaucracy literature shows that frontline workers’ decisions about clients are shaped by perceptions of deservingness based on clients’ demographic characteristics and behavioral conduct. Yet, we know little about whether frontline workers also act on clients’ financial resources and mental health, despite the administrative burden literature identifying these two factors as central to how citizens experience compliance demands. We contribute to both literatures by developing and testing preregistered hypotheses that examine how information about clients’ financial resources and mental health shapes frontline workers’ burden reduction and client prioritization. To do this, we run a large-scale vignette experiment with 775 caseworkers across 39 Danish unemployment agencies, manipulating client age, financial situation, mental health, and motivation but holding gender constant (all clients were male). We find strong evidence that caseworkers reduce burdens and prioritize clients with mental health issues and–consistent with prior work–clients displaying high motivation, alongside some evidence for clients struggling with financial hardship. Importantly, mental health appears more consequential than motivation and financial resources. Contrary to our preregistered expectation, clients with mental health issues received the most support when they also displayed low as opposed to high motivation. Rather than indicating that frontline workers reward low motivation, this pattern most likely reflects that caseworkers interpret low motivation as a symptom of the underlying condition, and thus as a signal of greater need. Our findings show how frontline discretion can operate as a redistributive mechanism that channels support toward clients signaling the greatest need.

Toward an “Identity Turn” in Public Service Motivation Theory: A Problematizing Review and Research Agenda

Lorenza Micacchi, Adrian Ritz

Full text
Since its inception, Public Service Motivation (PSM) has been recognized as a key driver of behavior in public institutions. Despite its prominence, skepticism has emerged. In response, research has primarily focused on empirical analysis and measurement refinement rather than on conceptual development. This conceptual path dependency has become problematic as mixed empirical results increasingly challenge core theoretical propositions of PSM theory. We argue that one potential cause lies in a dominant assumption shaping the field: its positioning within motivation theory. To address this, we conducted a problematizing review across concepts and disciplines, comparing how PSM is understood in Public Administration with related concepts–such as motivation, altruism, and identity–in Management, Psychology, Sociology, and Economics. Based on this analysis, we propose a novel theoretical framework that reconceptualizes PSM as a social identity. In doing so, we outline a research agenda that (i) revises PSM’s core conceptual features and develops a new measurement approach informed by identity theory; (ii) introduces new ways of understanding PSM's functioning through identity work theory; and (iii) situates PSM theory within the broader debate on the “identity turn” in motivation research, highlighting its potential contribution to this multidisciplinary discourse.

Digging Into the Subsoil: Considering and Theorizing Human Services Delivery at the Sub‐Micro‐Level of the Public Service Ecosystem

Kate Broadhurst, Steven Parker

Full text
Public services are often delivered in partnership with families and communities, yet these relationships receive limited attention within public service ecosystem (PSE) theory. Responding to call to develop the sub‐micro‐level of the PSE, we introduce the concept of subsoil sensitivity to foreground the family and community ecosystems that underpin public service value creation. These informal systems are active sites of value creation and destruction with sensitivities arising from their alignment or misalignment with public services. We propose legislative and policy nodes as connectors that translate macrolevel policy into microlevel practice. Drawing on child protection vignettes, we show how nodes create the conditions for interaction, while subsoil dynamics influence whether value is cocreated or codestroyed amid power asymmetries and differing belief systems. The paper extends the sub‐micro‐level of the PSE and offers a framework for examining family and community ecosystems in public service delivery.

The Immortality Paradox: One‐Party Dominance, Political Tenure, and Organizational Continuity in Japanese Agencies

Ayako Nakamura

Full text
In his classic 1976 study on organizational survival, Kaufman argued that public organizations are “immortal”. More recent research, however, has come to the opposite conclusion, finding that public organizations are frequently terminated. These findings largely relate to contexts where changes to the ruling party occur regularly. In contrast, this study examines the survival of government agencies in Japan, where the postwar political system has been characterized by one‐party dominance and short‐lived political leadership. The findings suggest that Japanese agencies remain largely “immortal,” and that when they do undergo transitions this tends to be linked to prime ministers with longer tenures who are able to implement minor, cosmetic changes; hence agencies typically persist even after such events. This study thus sheds new light on how political–administrative relations shape patterns of agency transition and highlights the importance of leadership tenure in sustaining commitments to organizational change.

Emulate the Leader? Explaining Crisis Policy Change in the Canadian Federal System

Liddell Hastings

Full text
In times of crises, do governments coordinate or do they make their own choices independent of what others do? Convergence of COVID‐19 policy at the beginning of the pandemic has been widely studied in federal countries. Yet, there is limited literature looking at the mechanisms that made policy between jurisdictions so similar. Utilizing Canada's decentralized federal system, this paper posits that convergence can be explained by a process of policy diffusion through emulation and a series of leader‐follower relationships. Drawing on the policy diffusion literature, this paper proposes three hypotheses for why some jurisdictions take on leadership roles in crisis policy while others follow. Synthesized as proximity, capacity, and urgency, these hypotheses are tested using a series of pairwise vector autoregressions identifying significant leader‐follower relationships between Canadian provinces. The findings suggest that policy primarily flowed from only a small number of large provinces (Quebec and Ontario), lending partial evidence, but also exceptions to the hypotheses proposed.

Regulatory Rationales in the Face of Emerging Technologies: A Systematic Review

Tara Kerins Aylmer, Tom Hall

Full text
Technology is evolving, and so is the discussion of regulation theory among academics. Taking a unique approach to legal research, this paper utilizes the systematic literature review methodology to uncover quantitative evidence on the current consensus among academics on regulation theory, in particular, regulatory rationales. This paper seeks to determine whether the narrative assumptions that social solidarity‐based regulatory rationales have become more prevalent as technology has evolved are correct. Based on the data gathered, this article asserts that the discussion of regulatory rationales is facing a tonal shift as a result of artificial intelligence. Academics are shifting away from a traditional economic view of regulation toward discussion of regulatory rationales based on protecting the common good. This should be welcome. Social solidarity should serve as a foundational principle of good technology regulation. Through applying the principles of social solidarity, regulators can better balance the risks and benefits of modern technology.

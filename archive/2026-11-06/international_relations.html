Journal of Conflict Resolution

The Price of Incompetence: Security Failures and Selective Accountability in Autocracies

Austin S. Matthews, Jonas Willibald Schmid

Full text
Do dictators hold their cabinet ministers accountable for policy failures, and do such failures push them to appoint more competent replacements? Existing research emphasizes loyalty as the dominant logic of authoritarian elite management, yet many cabinet portfolios carry responsibilities critical to regime survival. We argue that interior ministers, who oversee internal security and counter-terrorism, offer a revealing test of whether performance matters when the stakes are highest. Using an original dataset of more than 1,000 interior ministers across autocracies from 1970 to 2014, we show that deadly terrorism significantly increases the likelihood of ministerial dismissal. Yet, these crises do not lead dictators to appoint successors whose backgrounds signal greater security expertise. The result is a pattern of selective accountability: dictators punish visible failure but do not invest in preventing its recurrence. This asymmetry helps explain why authoritarian regimes often struggle to build effective security apparatuses despite acute survival pressures.

International Studies Review

Is the Territorial Integrity Norm in Its Death Throes?

Mikulas Fabry

Full text
The territorial integrity norm, designed to safeguard states against forcible redrawing of their borders and for decades regarded as foundational to the post-1945 global order, has been repeatedly challenged since 2008. These developments have led commentators to suggest that the norm is undergoing significant weakening or even outright extinction. The assumption informing these assertions is that norm strength internationally is determined by (1) the frequency of violations, (2) the relative power of violators, (3) the effectiveness of enforcement measures by third parties in countering violations, or (4) some combination of the three. In contrast, I argue that the territorial integrity norm has not markedly eroded. Norms are socially held ideas about proper behavior rather than patterns of actual behavior. A norm can be undone only by a collective abandonment of its underlying idea, not by individual infringements or ineffective enforcement. Its strength is the extent to which social commitment to it persists. The necessary empirical indicator of that commitment is concordance: public affirmation of norms, especially in cases of norm breaking. Although supplementing concordance with material measures to counter norm-breaking constitutes additional evidence of that commitment, norm strength is not contingent on the success of these efforts, which may prove elusive even when they entail substantial material coercion. Norm strength is undermined not by international society’s inability to restore the status quo ante when violations occur, but by its failure to call them violations and to continually repudiate them in the first place. In the case of territorial integrity, concordance has been most compellingly expressed by third-party refusals to recognize the forcible acquisition of territory as a valid transfer of sovereignty. In line with the pre-2008 practice, the recent attempts at, or formal backing of, such territorial acquisitions have, overall, continued to elicit sweeping refusals to extend recognition.

Political Studies

When do citizens support government decisions to ignore social protests? The role of instrumental considerations and majoritarian preferences

Lars Brummel, Dimiter Toshkov

Full text
In democracies, protests can challenge public policy reforms, raising questions on how governments should respond. Our study examines how citizens in the Netherlands would prefer their governments to respond to social protests. Using a pre-registered survey experiment (N = 1262), we varied the level of government implementing a reform and the level of public support for a reform. We used observational data to assess respondents’ views on the reforms and their trust in the government. While our findings reveal relatively strong support for accommodating social protests, citizens are more likely to back governments that ignore protests when these reforms have majority support and align with their own views. Trust in the government also plays a role, but the level of government does not matter for citizens’ attitudes. Overall, our findings highlight that citizens expect governments to listen to social protests but their attitudes are further contingent upon instrumental reasoning and majoritarian views of democracy.

International Studies Quarterly

After Zombies Attack: How Low-Quality Monitors Affect Credible International Election Observers

Sarah Sunn Bush, Christina Cottiero, Lauren Prather

Full text
The international election monitoring regime has become considerably more complex in the twenty-first century. Although the number of organizations engaged in high-quality election monitoring has plateaued over time, the number of low-quality monitors that validate autocratic elections has continued to grow. This study investigates how low-quality international election observers affect the behavior of high-quality observers when they are present at the same elections. On the one hand, the presence of low-quality international observers may encourage high-quality groups to step up their game, becoming more thorough and (when merited) more critical in a bid to differentiate themselves from low-quality groups. On the other hand, the presence of low-quality observers may discourage high-quality groups from criticizing flawed elections by introducing counter-narratives, accusing high-quality monitors of bias, and challenging the dominance of high-quality monitors. We test these hypotheses using new data on international election observation from 2000 to 2020, finding evidence that high-quality monitors have become more thorough but less critical in the presence of low-quality monitors.

Subsystems, Bargaining Costs, and Economic Coercion: The Incidental Consequences of US-Led Sanctions Against Russian Oil Exports

Jan Stockbruegger

Full text
How do markets affect the distribution of coercive bargaining costs in economic sanctions? This paper uses subsystem theory to study sanctions as market governance arrangements. It argues that sanctions do not rely on coercion alone, but must also provide benefits to market participants. A sanctions sender is unlikely to enforce sanctions that could disrupt markets on which both sender and friendly third-party states depend. The paper further argues that sanctions can create unforeseen problems in other areas of the world economy, but that states tolerate such negative effects to preserve important market benefits. These arguments are illustrated through an in-depth case study of the 2022 price cap on Russia’s oil exports. The United States and its allies did not enforce the price cap strictly, worrying that doing so could disrupt Russian oil supplies and drive up crude prices. They also tolerated significant maritime safety and environmental risks arising from Russia’s price cap evasion efforts—especially its use of substandard “shadow fleet” tankers—to protect global oil markets. Scholars of economic coercion need to pay closer attention to how market arrangements shape the design and implementation of sanctions—and to their incidental consequences in other areas of governance.

Assessing Populist Belligerence: Evidence of Populism and Democratic Governance’s Interaction on Interstate Violence

Ariel Zellman, Daniel F Wajner, Carmela Lutmar, Özgür Özdamar

Full text
A burgeoning literature seeks to explain populism’s influence on international security. Two regime characteristics appear particularly troubling in this regard: their degree of populism and their degree of democratic governance. While considerable research has explored these paired phenomena regarding states’ domestic agendas, little work has systematically examined these trends vis-à-vis states’ propensity for interstate violence using large-N quantitative analysis. Drawing on populism data from the V-Dem Party dataset (1970–2014), we examine how these factors contribute to states’ likelihood of threatening or utilizing military force. We find that populism’s effects on interstate bellicosity are particularly pronounced among more authoritarian regimes, however populism increases the likelihood of threat-making and use of military force by democracies and non-democracies alike. Overall, bellicose behavior is less apparent from examining degrees of populism or authoritarianism in isolation than from their interaction. These findings contribute to lively debates on populism’s imprint on international peace and stability.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

The Pied Piper of 4chan : Fairy-tale narratives of racist and misogynistic radicalisation in UK elite political discourse

Rohan Kit Bains Stevenson

Full text
UK political elites increasingly represent racist and misogynistic violence as a problem of ‘radicalisation’ in which, like the Pied Piper luring the children of Hamelin, predatory online ‘radicalisers’ lure and brainwash susceptible youth. This article examines how this narrative achieves dominance despite the availability of empirically grounded alternatives, asking what role narrative form plays in its success. Through a narrative analysis of UK counterterrorism policies and parliamentary statements, it reconstructs the narrative’s setting, characterisation and emplotment, demonstrating significant resemblances with several popular fairy tales. The article argues that this intertextual resonance with culturally embedded story forms lends the ‘radicalisation’ narrative a familiarity and coherence that alternative narratives – which foreground the everyday settings, adult perpetrators and structural persistence of these harms – lack. The article concludes by suggesting that contesting the ‘radicalisation’ narrative’s dominance requires telling counter-stories of comparable verisimilitude rather than simply assembling better evidence.

Regimes of violence/nonviolence: regulating the meaning-making of violence and nonviolence

Daniel Santos

Full text
Despite the growth in violence and nonviolence literature, few scholars explore how the meanings of these terms are governed, disregarding their discursive character. Expanding on the revived ‘regime of violence’ theory, this article proposes the concept of regime of violence/nonviolence as an inclusive and comprehensive conceptualisation that entangles violence, nonviolence, and their relations. States reify hegemonic regimes of violence that regulate and reinforce commonsensical state-legitimising understandings of violence. By co-opting nonviolence scholars and institutions, they attempt to do the same with a strategic, statist and reformist notion of nonviolence based on a politics of respectability. Contesting these dominant notions, resistance groups and critical scholars advance alternative understandings of violence and nonviolence that can reify counter-hegemonic regimes of violence/nonviolence. The concept of a regime of violence/nonviolence brings together violence and nonviolence scholarship and can enable more situated, empirical studies of how the meanings and practices of violence and nonviolence are governed.

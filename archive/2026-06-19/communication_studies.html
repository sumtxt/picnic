Performing Identity: Repetition, Emoji, and Candidate Indicators of Amplified Visibility in Jordanian–Palestinian Facebook Discourse

Alaeddin A. Banikalef, Mohammad Issa Mehawesh

Full text
This study examines how Arabic Facebook comments shape political tensions between Jordanians and Palestinians. The corpus contains 800 public comments collected from public Facebook pages focused on Jordanian–Palestinian affairs between January and July 2025. Methods combine immersive digital ethnography with systematic netnographic coding and a reproducible coordination–detection protocol applied at three scales: micro (linguistic strategies), meso (semiotic affordances), and macro (temporal and network patterns). The results show that dialectal markers, religious formulae, emojis, short slogans, and threaded replies interact to produce strong identity signaling and affective circulation. A subset of items displays heuristic markers associated with amplified visibility, such as exact-text repetition, clustered timestamps, repeated links, and low-engagement account profiles. These markers are presented as candidate indicators of amplification, not as proof of centralized orchestration. This study provides open detection scripts and appendix materials for human adjudication and follow-up network analyses. Findings point to low-cost interventions, such as prompts before verbatim reposting and triage workflows for flagged clusters, and to the need for cross-platform tracing and bot analyses to confirm provenance.

“My Non-Existing Organ”: Transitional Objects, Compensatory Romance, and the Affective Enclaves of Chinese Female Idol Fandom

Erika Ningxin Wang

Full text
This article examines how young Chinese female fans of the idol group ONER construct and defend gendered subjectivities through digitally mediated intimate practices across Weibo, Douban, and private WeChat groups. Drawing on 24 months of multi-sited digital ethnography across seven Chinese cities, the study documents two identificatory positions, the girlfriend-fan and the mum-fan, and two material-semiotic practices through which these identifications are enacted: coded language and cyber-penis memes . Building on Radway’s argument that women’s affective fandom constitutes psychic compensation for the unmet needs produced by patriarchal social structures, the study demonstrates that Chinese female fans engage in a comparable compensatory romance in response to the constraints imposed by China’s gender governance regime and state regulation of female fan culture. Ethnographic analysis reveals that girlfriend-fan and mum-fan positions are fluid, strategically negotiated stances through which fans manage the tensions between romantic fantasy, maternal retreat, and moral anxiety. Coded language and cyber-penis memes function as transitional objects in Winnicott’s sense, while Butler’s framework of phallic resignification reveals these memes as collective acts of symbolic resistance. Together, these practices constitute the affective enclave, a collectively defended potential space within China’s surveilled platform ecology in which non-normative female desire is articulated and protected.

The celebration of exploitation: A case study in platform promotion of users’ data

Isabelle Higgins, Graham Meikle

Full text
This article explores how a large digital platform exploits its user data to legitimise itself through the tone, form and content of its public-facing outputs. Our case study is the annual Insights (Year in Review) reports released by the pornographic platform Pornhub between 2013 and 2024, which we engage with as cultural artefacts and upon which we conduct detailed content and discourse analysis. These reports, Pornhub claims, are based on the extensive data captured about every unique visitor who accesses the site each year. We show that, in the reports, Pornhub works actively to legitimise and celebrate a multifaceted range of practices which exploit the data gathered, often unknowingly, from users. These practices rely upon the company’s platform infrastructure and use of social media logics. The Insights reports serve as a stark reminder of the power platforms have to exploit their users’ data to create celebratory narratives which elide complex ethical questions.

‘They have my kundli ’: Understanding urban upper-middle-class Indians’ beliefs about social media algorithms

Pranav Malhotra

Full text
This study examines urban upper-middle-class Indian social media users’ knowledge of, attitudes toward, and practices with social media algorithms. Interviews ( N = 30) reveal they primarily use Instagram and have a basic understanding of algorithms, with algorithmic awareness and knowledge functioning as forms of cultural capital. They also articulate folk theories about how algorithms operate, some of which reflect a relational rather than individually-centered view of algorithmic personalization. Furthermore, they express mixed attitudes toward algorithms, including a positive view of algorithms as smart technologies they can control to view global trends, and a critical view of algorithms as manifestations of platform power associated with surveillance and advertising. These views are informed by long-standing cultural beliefs equating technology with globalization and aspiration, contemporary concerns about social media’s effects, and participants’ social status. This study therefore expands our understanding of how context shapes algorithmic sensemaking, presenting insights from a critical Global South setting.

When the Algorithm Understands You (or Doesn’t): LGBTQ+ Youth’s Identity-Related Interactions with Algorithmic Media

Y. Anthony Chen, Samuel Hardman Taylor

Full text
During their daily media use, youth interact with algorithms. Drawing on the algorithm responsiveness process framework, this paper examines the co-construction of identity between youth and algorithmic media, focusing on perceived algorithm responsiveness (PAR), perceived algorithm insensitivity (PAI), and algorithm training as sociotechnical processes that structure how LGBTQ+ youth perceive themselves. Study 1 ( N = 335), using six weekly diaries among LGBTQ+ youth, showed that affirming LGBTQ+ representations curated by algorithms predicted higher PAR and lower PAI, especially when less algorithm training was needed. In turn, higher PAR and, unexpectedly, higher PAI predicted greater identity pride. Study 2 ( N = 664), a cross-sectional survey, found that LGBTQ+ youth reported lower PAR than non-LGBTQ+ youth. An indirect effect of algorithm training on self-concept clarity via PAR emerged for non-LGBTQ+ participants but not for LGBTQ+ participants. These findings highlight social identity in algorithmic media as a relational, dynamic process, and LGBTQ+ youth can shape content recommendations for identity-related needs.

Co-Opetition in Crowdsourcing Challenges: Comment Network Dynamics and Relational Homophily Among Contestants in Crowdsourcing Challenge Communities

Yiqi Li, Peter Monge, Janet Fulk

Full text
Situated in a creative crowdsourcing challenge organizational community, this research examines how different types of homophily affect network formation among ideators, the challenge innovators. Longitudinal analysis of comment network events among ideators was conducted with relational event models. Status homophily was examined based on ideators’ self-reported public profiles. Findings show that ideators’ location by country significantly influences relational events, but occupational homophily did not. Value homophily was examined based on the content similarity of ideators’ idea submissions. Results indicate that ideator’s choices of platform content categories, those that are designed by the challenge management, significantly influenced ideators’ communication patterns. Specifically, long-term needs were a salient homophily factor among platform categories, whereas short-term needs were heterophilous factors. Finally, a multidimensional homophily measurement using latent class analysis on idea-category-submission patterns predicted the occurrences of communication relational events and achieved an improved model fit compared to modeling all platform categories together.

Policy and Internet

Does the Community Understand the Community Guidelines?

Friederike Quint, Yannis Theocharis, Mia Nahrgang, Nils B. Weidmann, Margaret E. Roberts

Full text
Amid continued public controversy and political debate about online speech governments and civil society have pushed social media platforms to increase transparency around how they regulate speech, through community guidelines and content moderation systems, to support users to hold companies accountable for content removals. But how well do users understand apply platform content rules? We report results of two complementary studies. In Study 1, lab participants were randomly assigned to judge whether posts violated the guidelines of 10 platforms. Users' interpretations of the guidelines were inconsistent with each other, especially for platforms with longer documents. In Study 2, we preregistered an experiment testing whether shortened versions of Meta's guidelines improved understanding. Reducing length without simplifying moderation rules did not lower disagreement about what content the platform moderates. Taken together, our results indicate substantial room to improve the interpretability of community guidelines, enabling users to evaluate moderation more reliably. These findings highlight design tradeoffs and motivate clearer, example‐driven guidance.

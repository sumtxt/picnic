Review of International Organizations

Trade agreements and the design of democracy-related provisions (TRADEM): The creation of a new data set

Manfred Elsig, Kirthana Ganeson, Andrew Lugg, Marine Roux

Full text
Scholars have long sought to understand the relationship between trade agreements and democracy. This research note contributes to studying this relationship by presenting a new, comprehensive database on democracy-related provisions in preferential trade agreements (PTAs). The data is unique in its coverage and data creation approach. First, it is the most systematic and in-depth collection of democracy-related provisions in PTAs. It includes obligations to promote democracy, the protection of individual rights, alongside other important democracy-related provisions to increase transparency and stakeholder participation, and to preserve policy space. Second, it combines manual text coding and machine learning approaches to generate the dataset and two types of new indices. The research note first provides an overview of the existing literature on the PTA-democracy nexus. Then, it outlines our conceptualization of how particular PTA provisions are relevant from the viewpoint of democracy. This leads to a new typology covering six dimensions encompassing seventy-two democracy-related provisions. The research note then describes how we combine manual coding and machine learning to create the TRADEM database. We further present descriptive statistics focusing on categories and trends over time and region for a total of 792 PTAs signed between 1949 and 2022. We conclude by outlining potential applications and research avenues.

International Studies Quarterly

Strategic Censorship? Public Opinion, Authoritarian Politics, and the International Trade Regime

Seowoo Chung

Full text
International organizations promote cooperation by diffusing information among states and the public. But how does this information reach the public under authoritarian regimes? I examine how an authoritarian government with a censorship apparatus can use government-controlled media to offset international organizations’ information effects. The authoritarian government selectively censors IO news based on the likelihood of success in invoking a defensive reaction, suppressing complaints made by neutral counterparts that may lead to concerns for noncompliance while allowing limited attention toward provocative complaints by hostile states. I find support for my arguments through analysis of an original dataset of Chinese newspaper articles by 116 newspapers on all WTO disputes involving China. Survey experiment on Chinese citizens tests the microfoundations of the information strategy, finding that exposure to information about China being sued in a WTO dispute significantly reduces support for international law, especially for those disputes against the United States.

Divide and Conquer: Industry Market Structure, Inter-Firm Rivalry, and Bargaining over Technology

John Minnich

Full text
Abstracts When do firms trade technology for market access? Theories of foreign investment typically frame host state bargaining power in terms of domestic characteristics like market size. This article examines how the structure of global markets in which firms are embedded and the inter-firm competition this generates shape firm-state bargaining over technology in ways not reducible to host state attributes. I argue that as the number of firms in an industry rises and global market shares become less evenly divided among them, weak market players face strong incentives to transfer technology in return for access to new markets. In this context, host states can “divide and conquer” investors to secure better terms of trade and investment. Case studies of technology transfer in commercial aircraft manufacturing and semiconductor design and fabrication in China show how industry market structure shapes firm-state bargaining over entry terms both across and within industries.

Feminist Realism: Jean Bethke Elshtain and Armed Civic Virtue

Paul Kirby

Full text
Abstracts Recent scholarship has led both to a reconsideration of political realism and to a recovery of women’s international thought. The two projects meet at a surprising juncture: the rearticulation of realpolitik in gender egalitarian terms. This article introduces and interrogates the seemingly paradoxical hybrid of “feminist realism” through its most accomplished and controversial academic proponent: Jean Bethke Elshtain. I argue that Elshtain merged feminist and realist commitments over many decades despite a hostility to grand theory. Drawing on public works and personal archives, I show how she combined a pessimistic anthropology drawn from Saint Augustine and Reinhold Niebuhr with an account of anarchy inspired by Hannah Arendt with Kenneth Waltz. These realist aspects were reconciled with Jane Addams’ notion of civic householding and unsettled by the spectre of armed civic virtue. Elshtain illuminates a strand in international political thought of greater past and future significance than has yet been appreciated.

Credibility Without Formal Commitments: How Market Reputation Reshapes BITs

Shiyang Wu

Full text
Under what circumstances do states reconsider their formal commitments to international cooperation? This paper examines how shifts in market reputation shape treaty renegotiation and termination in the context of bilateral investment treaties (BITs). Analyzing 2,620 BITs across 178 countries over 8 years, I demonstrate that states are more likely to renegotiate and withdraw from treaties when their reputational standing in global business climate indicators improves. Enhanced market reputation reduces the perceived costs of exit, empowering states to demand revisions or abandon agreements when bargaining fails. The study further reveals that the strength of treaty lock-in provisions moderates this relationship. Specifically, states with stronger reputational gains are more likely to challenge BITs with shorter lock-in periods, as these treaties impose fewer long-term constraints. In contrast, treaties with extended lock-in dampen the effect of reputation improvements, delaying host states’ ability to capitalize on their enhanced reputation. These findings advance our understanding of international cooperation by highlighting the interplay between informal constraints and formal commitment and the importance of institutional design for regime stability. They also demonstrate how global markets can shift power to new sets of states.

Autocrats and Ideological Imperative: Russia’s Anti-Nazi Campaign in International Organizations

Alexander Baturo

Full text
While scholarship exists on the contestation of liberal norms, the conditions under which autocrats gain global support for their own normative campaigns remain less understood. This paper proposes that dictatorships can gain normative standing in international organizations (IOs) by repurposing existing consensus, thereby making opposition more difficult. Alongside seeking majority support, autocrats also aim to isolate and force liberal actors into socially disadvantageous positions, even when doing so reduces overall support. To test this argument, the paper presents a mixed-methods study of the Soviet Union’s and Russia’s promotion of anti-Nazism as a fundamental principle underpinning the international system—with Russia cast as its guardian—from 1946 to 2024. Given the linkages between Nazism and racial discrimination, Russia has been able to attract broad support in contexts sensitive to colonial legacies and apartheid, rendering opposition to its initiatives morally unacceptable. These tactics, employed during the Cold War, have returned in force in a more multipolar setting. Drawing on United Nations meeting records and voting data, text analyses of other members’ discourse, and comparative analyses across IOs, the paper explains how and why the norm of anti-Nazism was repurposed through rhetoric and agenda manipulation. The findings underscore the enduring importance of ideology for autocrats in international politics.

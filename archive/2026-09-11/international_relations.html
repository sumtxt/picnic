Signaling for aid? Effects of signing democracy-related provisions in preferential trade agreements

Marine Roux

Full text
Aid for Trade has been a key instrument established by donor agencies to support the implementation of trade-related commitments to the World Trade Organization and preferential trade agreements (PTAs). Yet relatively little is known about the links between the signature of non-trade provisions in PTAs and foreign aid. Democracy-related provisions are of particular relevance, as they potentially offer a unique mechanism for democratic donors to link good governance criteria to economic interests. I theorize that democracy-related provisions can lead to increases in aid through establishing institutional frameworks that enhance long-term cooperation and monitoring. By contrast, I do not expect recipients to receive additional foreign aid support to reward credible commitments to democratization, to implement such provisions, nor as a by-product of deep trade commitments. Overall, I find support for these hypotheses, estimating a staggered difference-in-differences model over 100 mixed-regime country pairs from 1966 to 2019. This paper demonstrates that signing democracy-related provisions can enable signatories to secure aid deals, although not aimed at democratization or the implementation of such provisions.

Journal of Peace Research

The transformation of Mexican cartels and violence against government officials

Marco Alcocer, Megan Erickson

Full text
Since the early 2000s, Mexican cartels have increasingly threatened, attacked, and assassinated government officials. Existing explanations emphasize electoral incentives, political vulnerability, and government crackdowns. We propose an additional and largely overlooked explanation: transformations within Mexico’s criminal underworld itself. As large cartels fragmented, more localized criminal organizations emerged, multiplying both symmetric and asymmetric inter-cartel conflicts. We argue that cartel capacity and inter-cartel conflict interact to produce distinct incentives for the targeting government officials. To test these claims, we combine original data on assassinated politicians in Mexico between 2000 and 2018 with existing datasets on attacks against political actors and municipal-level cartel presence. We find that high-capacity cartels and inter-cartel conflicts are the strongest predictors of violence against politicians. While early violence is driven primarily by high-capacity organizations, lower-capacity splinters become significant perpetrators after fragmentation intensifies, but only in municipalities experiencing inter-cartel conflict. Lower-capacity cartels operating alone do not predict assassinations. We further find that cartel dynamics primarily shape violence against local and, to a lesser extent, state-level politicians, but not federal officials. These findings demonstrate that changes within the criminal underworld play central roles in shaping patterns of political violence in Mexico’s ongoing security crisis.

The role of militarized interstate disputes in presidential term-limit evasions: from crisis to consolidation

Hilal Sert, Anthony J DeMattee

Full text
Militarized interstate disputes (MIDs) have well-documented effects on public opinion and executive authority, yet their implications for domestic institutional change remain underexplored. We argue that interstate conflict reshapes leaders’ incentives in ways that facilitate presidential term-limit evasion (TLE). Early-stage disputes can generate rally effects that expand executive discretion and reduce resistance to constitutional change. As conflicts persist, public fatigue and uncertainty about leadership turnover may increase the appeal of continuity, altering both the feasibility and the mode of institutional reform. We argue that prolonged hostile MIDs make referendums, where executives can mobilize conflict-conditioned public support, more attractive than legislative or judicial channels, which become increasingly risk-averse under prolonged conflict. Using an extended dataset on executive term limits in Sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America from 1989 to 2023, we show that interstate conflict is most closely associated with referendum-based TLE attempts, and that prolonged hostile disputes substantially raise the likelihood of successful referendum-led evasions. These findings contribute to research on executive aggrandizement, institutional change, and the domestic consequences of international conflict by identifying a pathway through which interstate insecurity can shape constitutional politics. The article shows that conflict does not merely affect public opinion or leader survival; it can also influence how incumbents seek to revise the rules that govern political succession.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Narratives as Separating Equilibria: On the Origins of the Ukraine War

Giacomo Corneo

Full text
NATO enlargement and Russian occupation of Crimea marked crucial turning points in the process that eventually resulted in the Ukraine war. According to one narrative, that Russian move was part of a plan to re-establish dominion over Eastern Europe. According to a rival view, it was an attempt to counter a U.S. plan to subjugate Russia. This paper scrutinizes the logical requirements of those narratives in a multi-stage game of incomplete information that produces equilibrium play such that first NATO is enlarged and then Russia attacks Ukraine. The two competing narratives correspond to two different separating equilibria. Conditions for their existence inform about the consistency and plausibility of the associated narratives.

International Theory

The problem of intelligence in International Relations theory

Robert Schuett

Full text
States spy. Yet International Relations (IR) theory has no systematic account of intelligence. I make three arguments. First, realist IR theories are too narrow to theorize intelligence as the complex social practice it is. Realism renders intelligence invisible or reduces it to a single instrumental raison d’état logic. Second, the English School’s societal approach opens the way for theorizing intelligence as instrument of normative practice. Extending Adam Watson’s raison de système to intelligence, grounded in the intelligence–diplomacy relationship and corroborated by an emerging lex specialis , establishes that intelligence helps constitute international society rather than merely operating within it. Third, the societal approach does not exhaust the subject. Raison d’humanité names a third logic – intelligence in the service of justice – and critical approaches deepen the account by asking whose survival, whose order, and whose humanity the framework serves. The three logics are in permanent tension and tend toward a rough hierarchy, reflecting political choice rather than structural necessity. This analysis raises a question for future research: whether intelligence qualifies as a primary institution of international society – a practice whose place is not at the margins of world politics but at its center. Where there are spies, there is society.

Accidental architects: self-effacing objectives in international relations

Krzysztof Pelc

Full text
International relations scholarship typically assumes that states achieve their objectives through deliberate, strategic action. This article develops an alternative account: many state objectives are self-effacing – they cannot be attained directly, but only as byproducts of other pursuits. I argue that this dynamic is especially likely under interdependence, where networks emerge through the voluntary participation of new entrants. Under such conditions, overt bids for future leverage provoke resistance, hedging, and duplication. Where durable structural advantage does materialize, it tends to arise indirectly, as the unintended result of principled state commitments, or of nonstate actors pursuing goals unrelated to geopolitics. I illustrate the argument through cases in global finance and global supply chains, showing how leverage repeatedly emerges from processes no state intended. Finally, I turn to foreign influence campaigns to demonstrate how states can nonetheless design conditions conducive to ‘welcome byproducts.’ Yet doing so requires relinquishing some control. The result is a reframing of strategy in international relations: dominance proves most likely when it is not openly pursued.

Anarchy versus organization: how the social science of International Relations emerged from controversies over war, evolution, and Darwinism

Jens Steffek, Anna Nakhratian

Full text
In this article, we show how IR’s central debate about international anarchy emerged from controversies over war, evolution and Darwinism that took place around 1900. It was a chiefly European debate between apologists of war – polemically called ‘Social Darwinists’ – and proponents of a ‘scientific pacifism,’ in particular the sociologist Jacques Novicow and the peace activist Alfred H. Fried. Apologists of war cited theories of evolution to portray international politics as an agonistic struggle among states, civilizations or races. Arguing from that premise, they naturalized political violence and imperialism. Scientific pacifists cited theories of evolution to show that anarchy was malleable and could be overcome through international organization. In their view, evolution rewarded cooperation of humankind in its struggle against the forces of nature. Ever since, evolutionary theorizing has returned to IR debates. Most defining, as we argue, has been the controversy between Kenneth N. Waltz and Alexander Wendt over the consequences of international anarchy. It was equally a debate about the right interpretation of what evolution implies for international politics. We conclude that evolutionary theory has been a lifetime companion of disciplinary IR, intimately related to the anarchy theme.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

Do electoral polls explain the winner–loser gap in political trust? A panel study on polls, election results, and political trust in Belgium

Dieter Stiers, Marc Hooghe

Full text
There is an ongoing debate on whether elections boost levels of political trust across the board or exclusively among the “winners” of the elections. The subsequent question is who exactly can be defined as the “winner” of a specific election. In the current study, we report on a panel survey that was conducted both before and after the 2024 general elections in Belgium. Results show that the experience of elections tends to boost levels of political trust, but this effect is stronger among the perceived winners of the elections. Most importantly, we find that this effect depends more strongly on a comparison with published public opinion poll results than on a comparison to the result of previous elections. We argue, therefore, that the winner–loser debate should take into account the effects of polls and mass media communication in shaping voters’ expectations when operationalising winning and losing.

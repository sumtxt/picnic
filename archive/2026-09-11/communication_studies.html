Journal of Communication

Looking good, fitting in: co-trajectories of appearance- and friendship-related identity distress and positive online self-presentation in adolescence

Gaëlle Vanhoffelen, Lara Schreurs, Laura Vandenbosch

Full text
Adolescence is a critical period for identity development, often accompanied by identity distress. Simultaneously, adolescents are also preoccupied with presenting themselves as positive as possible on social media, which may reflect and reinforce identity distress. Yet, the dynamic associations between adolescents’ identity distress and positive online self-presentations remain unexplored. Accordingly, this three-wave panel study among adolescents from Belgium, France, and Slovenia (N = 1,697; Mage = 15.14, SDage = 1.78, 57.2% girls) used Latent Class Growth Analysis to identify distinct co-trajectories of identity distress and positive online self-presentation in the domains of friendship and appearance over one year. The findings showed that identity distress and positive self-presentations can follow maladaptive, (high risk) adaptive, or stable co-trajectories over time, depending on the identity domain, adolescents’ demographic, and social media use characteristics. In doing so, the study provides novel evidence on the heterogeneous co-development of adolescents’ identity distress and online self-presentation.

Loneliness and the local news environment

Danny Hayes, Anusha Trivedi

Full text
Recent research suggests that changes to the media environment have contributed to loneliness in the United States. This work focuses on smartphones and social media, with the decline of local news receiving little attention. We argue that local news promotes social well-being by informing residents about their communities, fostering attachment and a sense of belonging that can reduce loneliness. We provide evidence in two studies. First, using state-level data and multiple measures of news availability, we show that loneliness is higher in states with fewer options for local news. Second, we find in a nationally representative survey that people who rarely consume local journalism report greater loneliness than regular local news consumers. There is some evidence that the relationship is strongest in rural areas, which have experienced severe declines in local news access. We conclude by proposing a research agenda to further explore how community news may support social well-being.

Exposure to “connective language” in political Facebook posts leads to more enjoyment and less psychological reactance

Gina M Masullo, Shuting Yao, Zhi Lin, Josephine Lukito, Ashwin Rajadesingan, Natalie J Stroud

Full text
In this preregistered study, we experimentally tested (n = 2,032) whether “connective language”—language that expresses openness to others’ perspectives and humility in presenting one’s viewpoints—reduces psychological reactance to disagreeable political messages. Compared with non-connective Facebook posts, a single connective post reduced expressed reactance, counter-arguing, and perceived threat to one’s freedom. Connective posts also elicited fewer negative and positive emotions. Participants found connective posts more enjoyable and they spent more time reading them, although these posts were slightly longer. However, post type did not affect behavioral engagement, such as liking or replying. Our findings suggest that social media platforms should optimize their algorithms to surface connective language, and the public should consider adopting this type of language.

Constructing Online Harms: How “Trust and Safety” Translates Safety Into Risk

Tomás Guarna

Full text
How are online harms recognized and prioritized within platform companies? Academics, journalists, and regulators have focused on identifying how digital platforms produce dangerous effects, but how harms are constructed within these organizations remains largely opaque. Based on 42 interviews with Trust and Safety specialists—the workers who define, operationalize, and create mechanisms to prevent or mitigate harmful effects—and ethnographic observation of industry forums, I describe how these workers transform concerns about platform dangers into actionable rules and processes. First, they position themselves as advocates for users, claiming moral authority to represent them within their organizations. Second, they translate abstract concerns into concrete organizational risks. Third, they publicize their companies’ harm-mitigation efforts in industry forums, performing responsiveness to critics and distributing responsibility across the sector. User harms become organizationally visible when they can be translated into corporate risks—legal liability, reputational damage, or revenue threats—and technical problems.

Cross-platformization: How U.S. right-leaning media curate their posts on Twitter and Truth Social

Josephine Lukito, Yini Zhang, Bin Chen, Stephen Prochaska, Meredith L. Pruden, Yunkang Yang, Wei Zhong, Megan A. Brown, Ross Dahlke, Jason Greenfield, ... (+2 authors)

Full text
This study examines how seven right-leaning media organizations utilize cross-platformization strategies to reach audiences across multiple platforms; in this case, Twitter and Truth Social. Focusing on both content volume and story packaging, we collect right-leaning media organizations’ social media posts and content from the 2022 U.S. midterm election. Combining computational and qualitative methods, we find that outlets posted only a small share of total stories across the two platforms and were selective in their content, often posting only on Twitter or Truth Social. For content posted on both platforms, the outlets posted content that ideologically aligned with the users of that platform. More specifically, right-leaning outlets on Truth Social tailored their posts to ideologically aligned users.

Are they virtue-signalling or sincere? The impact of perceived motives on the effectiveness of online collective action

Lisette Yip, Emma F. Thomas, Hawa Muhammad Farid, M. Noor Tahir

Full text
Social media is an important tool used by supporters of modern social movements to persuade others to participate. However, people who engage in online collective action often face speculation about their motives and may be perceived as acting for self-serving reasons. How do these perceptions impact the effectiveness of collective action? In three experiments ( N = 916), participants viewed posts (Study 1) or an immersive social media feed (Studies 2–3) where a user encouraged them to take action for a political cause. They stated that they acted out of genuine concern (autonomous motivation), to avoid guilt and social disapproval (controlled motivation), or did not specify (baseline). Participants who viewed controlled-motivated posts perceived the user as less autonomously motivated and were therefore less likely to socially identify as supporters or take collective action. Thus, movements will be more effective at mobilising (online) support when they are seen to arise from autonomous motives.

Privacy Asymmetries in Digital Public Mobility: Trust and the Perceived Value of Personal Data

Mónica Humeres, Alexandra Davidoff, Teresa Correa, Claudia López

Full text
As algorithmic platforms increasingly mediate public service delivery, data‐driven systems shape how citizens experience trust and privacy while generating digital traces that inform policy decisions. In the context of public mobility, these dynamics manifest through routine interactions with systems that track movement, log behavior, and process personal information, transforming everyday travel into a site of data extraction with policy implications. This study investigates how individuals in Chile interpret and respond to digital transport systems, focusing on their concerns about data privacy, institutional trust, and the value of the data they generate. Drawing on interviews ( N = 27) and six focus groups ( N = 48), the paper uses the notion of privacy asymmetries to characterize the uneven dispositions users hold across three dimensions: (1) between functionality trust in digital platforms and data privacy concerns; (2) between acceptance of data collection practices by private companies and by the state, and (3) across demographic lines, particularly gender. These asymmetries reflect how historical legacies, political context, and sociodemographic categories shape trust and skepticism toward data use in public systems.

Trust in the Machine? Practitioner Perspectives on the Role of AI in Online Content Moderation

Elena Martellozzo, Kylie McCarthy, Megan Trafford, Emmanuel Adinkrah, Paul Bleakley

Full text
The scale and volume of user‐generated content produced in the Web 2.0 era have led to content moderation becoming an essential aspect of most online platforms' business models. This form of digital labour is often performed by human content moderators (CMs) in the Global South, however, artificial intelligence (AI) technology has increasingly changed the nature of this work leading to serious questions about the future of this industry. A mixed‐methods survey was conducted with Africa‐based CMS ( n = 107) who responded to several open‐ended questions related to their perceptions of AI and its potential use in content moderation. Thematic analysis revealed strong support for the use of AI in content moderation. However, CMs noted several barriers to AI implementation, such as an inability to apply context, which limits its efficacy. The findings suggest that African CMs appreciate the potential benefits that AI can offer to the content moderation industry, yet still see a clear need for AI to be balanced with human oversight to properly address the contextual nuance that is inherent to the role.

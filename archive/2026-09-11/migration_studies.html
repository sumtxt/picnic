Living with Uncertainty: Ontological Insecurity and Mutual Victimhood in Rohingya–Host Relations

Tuhin Roy, M M Abdullah Al Mamun Sony, Md Kamrul Hasan, Shima Chowdhury

Full text
This article examines the transformation of relations between Rohingya refugees and host communities in southeastern Bangladesh, documenting a shift from early hospitality to entrenched hostility under conditions of protracted displacement. Drawing on qualitative fieldwork, including in-depth interviews and sustained observation, the study moves beyond refugee-center or host-burden frameworks to analyze displacement as a relational process. Anchored in the concept of ontological insecurity, the findings show that prolonged uncertainty destabilizes the moral and social certainties of both communities, generating reciprocal perceptions of abandonment, insecurity, and loss of dignity. Informal economic interdependence and gendered labor displacement intensify psychosocial distress, translating structural inequality into moral resentment and social withdrawal. Rather than fostering solidarity, shared precarity produces a condition of mutual victimhood in which suffering becomes comparative and competing, reinforcing stigma and dehumanization. The article argues that without institutional mechanisms capable of restoring recognition and ontological security for both refugees and hosts, displacement governance risks entrenching durable social fracture rather than enabling coexistence.

Reverse or Reflexive Transnationalism? Second-Generation Turkish-Germans’ Engagements With “Here” and “There” After Return Migration

Russell King, Nilay Kılınç

Full text
This article examines how second-generation Turkish-German “return” migrants navigate life in their parents’ homeland and construct various transnational reorientations due to the mismatches between their return expectations and the post-return realities of living in Turkey. Based on the life-story narratives of 71 participants, the findings show that the second generation’s return often results in multiple challenges and, in some cases, profound disillusionment. In response to the realization that the return does not provide a complete sense of homecoming, they start engaging in practices of “reverse transnationalism,” reorienting them toward Germany, where they were born and raised. These practices include visits to Germany, pursuing careers where they can use the German language, building social networks with other German-speakers, and integrating aspects of the German mentality and lifestyle into their professional, social, and family lives. Some contemplate a “re-return” to Germany, but this depends on citizenship. Others re-evaluate the attractions of both countries in a more complex form of what we term reflexive transnationalism . The article concludes that both physical and symbolic acts of reverse and reflexive transnationalism help second-generation returnees to adapt to the challenges posed by the social, economic, and political realities of their ancestral homeland.

Beyond CVs: A Video-Based Experimental Approach for Studying Ethnic Evaluation Biases in Hiring

Juliane Kühn, Tobias Wolbring

Full text
Ethnic biases in hiring are most commonly studied through correspondence tests that examine discriminatory behavior and text-based factorial survey experiments that focus on evaluations of applicants. To move beyond written application materials, we explore the potential and limitations of a complementary approach: a factorial survey experiment in which hypothetical applicants are presented in two formats—written CVs and 90 second video applications. The videos approximate more interactive stages of the hiring process, allowing for the integration of visual and auditory cues, while maintaining comparability between applicants of different ethnic backgrounds (German, Asian, and African). Deepfake technology ensures that all videos feature an identical audio track, characterized by accent-free, fluent, and high-quality German. In our study, managers ( n = 1,289) with personnel responsibility in German companies evaluate applicants for a marketing position to examine how the prejudice level and the availability of applicant information shape hireability assessments. Our findings show that providing additional information through video applications can enhance evaluations of ethnic minority candidates. However, the influence of respondents’ level of prejudice does not differ significantly between the two hiring stages. Overall, the proposed approach shows great potential for future research but also faces limitations and concerns, particularly regarding social desirability.

The Game of Making Money: How Rural Migrant Delivery Riders Are Motivated to Work Very Long Hours in China

Yu Feng, Paul Waley, Jie Shen

Full text
What motivates rural migrant delivery riders in China to work very long hours, and how are they managed by their working platforms? This paper investigates the motivations of rural migrant riders in Shanghai to engage in continuous work and examines the management strategies employed by their platforms. Drawing on participant observation and interviews with rural migrant riders working for the two largest on‐demand food delivery platforms in China, this paper finds that gamified algorithmic controls incentivize riders' labour. Driven by strict working performance assessment, Shanghai riders are attracted by three types of games: the everyday game of assessing work performance, the game played with their working platforms and the game played among riders. Building on these findings, this article identifies three defining features that condition consent from workers within the platform labour regime: its temporal fragmentation, informational asymmetry and hyper‐individualization. The gamification process ultimately aligns with broader trends of datafication within the online food delivery sector. These findings contribute to understandings of labour games in platform economies and offer insights into the emergence of ‘sticky labour’ in exploitative working environments. In doing so, it underscores the contingent, situated nature of labour motivation in the platform economy and extends Burawoy's analysis to digital labour regimes.

Why Stay? Local Opportunity Anchoring and Gendered Immobility in Peri‐Urban China

Hongwei Yu, Yufeng Hou

Full text
Why women remain in split households cannot be inferred from residence alone: staying may be desired, accepted, or constrained. Drawing on two interview rounds a year apart and later online follow‐up with 10 women in a peri‐urban Yunnan village whose partners worked elsewhere, this article examines how staying was sustained as local tourism contracted. Six sustained employment‐based staying through other local jobs; three remained through care, schooling, and remittances; one migrated as high household expenses coincided with reduced childcare demands. We define local opportunity anchoring as the employment‐based pathway through which work within commuting range sustains the feasibility of residence already organised around care, schooling, and hukou: nearby jobs become usable when social networks convey job information, care arrangements make employment feasible, and earnings meet household‐specific needs. The findings separate the feasibility of staying from women's orientations and show how life‐course positions, linked lives, and peri‐urban accessibility shape divergent trajectories.

Career Pathways in the Global Labour Market: Non‐European Master's Graduates in Germany, Italy, and the United Kingdom

Erik Zeltner, Christof Van Mol

Full text
This paper examines the career pathways of international STEM and business master's alumni who graduated in Germany, Italy, and the United Kingdom, based on 28 in‐depth interviews with international master's graduates from India, China, and Iran. The findings indicate that the career pathways and labour market destinations of non‐European master's graduates are significantly influenced by the contextual conditions present in both home and host countries. In contrast to the clear trend in Germany and Italy towards the host labour market or other Western‐oriented markets, the data from the United Kingdom indicates that contextual conditions resulted in either a successful transition to the host labour market or a return to the home country. The pursuit of career pathways is also significantly influenced by conditions prevailing in the home country. However, the findings of this study further indicate that these effects are subject to variation depending on the contextual conditions of the respective host country.

Local Enrollment or Cross‐Regional Portability? Public Healthcare Service Utilization and Older Migrants' Settlement Intentions in China

Cuiying Huang, Zheng Luo, Ye Liu, Zhixin Feng

Full text
China has substantially reformed its medical insurance system to enable direct cross‐regional settlement of medical expenses since the late 2000s, yet little is known about how different forms of medical insurance participation—local enrollment vs. cross‐regional portability—differentially shape older migrants' settlement intentions. To address this gap, using nationally representative survey data on 5002 older migrants who have reached the statutory retirement age, this study examines how public healthcare service utilization influences older migrants' settlement intentions in China. Public healthcare service utilization is measured using four indicators: participation in destination‐based medical insurance, participation in origin‐based medical insurance with direct settlement at the destination, establishment of a local resident health record, and participation in health education activities. Multinomial probit models are used to account for the categorical nature of settlement intentions: no intention, short‐term intentions, and long‐term intentions. Model results show that older migrants enrolled in destination‐based medical insurance are more likely to express long‐term settlement intentions, whereas those relying on origin‐based insurance with cross‐regional settlement tend to exhibit short‐term intentions. In addition, older migrants who have established a local resident health record are more likely to express long‐term settlement intentions. Furthermore, origin–destination disparities in the quantity and quality of medical service provision significantly moderate the association between medical insurance participation and older migrants' settlement intentions. Our findings advance understanding of older migrants' settlement intentions by revealing the divergent effects of local enrollment and cross‐regional portability on long‐term vs. short‐term settlement, and by highlighting the moderating role of origin–destination disparities in medical service provision, with implications for deepening cross‐regional settlement reforms to better facilitate the long‐term urban integration of older migrants.

The Residential Mobility of Mid‐Term Temporary Populations: Personal Trajectories and Sociospatial Patterns in Rome

Barbara Brollo

Full text
This article examines how medium‐term temporary dwellers—who have been living in a city for months or a few years without planning to settle in the long term—impact upon intra‐urban residential mobility and sociospatial inequalities in Southern European contexts. Building on debates on residential mobility, (im)mobilities and temporary urban populations, the paper argues that these groups should be treated as a distinct and influential mobility actor. Empirically, the study draws on original mixed‐methods evidence from Rome, combining a survey and follow‐up interviews to link motivations for arrival, housing practices, and intra‐urban relocations. Rome is discussed as a relevant Southern European case where post‐crisis housing‐market restructuring, uneven accessibility and the expansion of platform‐mediated rental circuits intensify competition for centrally located housing. The findings show that temporary dwellers' residential movements and strategies contribute to neighbourhood change and can reinforce displacement pressures and unequal access to central and semi‐central areas, thereby shaping broader patterns of residential mobility for the overall resident population.

“We Create a Nest and Then They Leave”: Rural Refugee Resettlement and Dynamic Trust in Iceland

Lara Wilhelmine Hoffmann, Magnus T. Bernhardsson

Full text
Policy makers increasingly promote refugee resettlement in rural areas. Despite trust being widely recognized as central to successful resettlement, little attention has been given to how trust is manifested in rural regions and whether urban‐based understanding of trust‐building governance practices requires adaptation for rural settings. Drawing on 111 interviews (2023–2024) with refugees, teachers, municipal social services employees and volunteers involved in resettling Syrian and Iraqi refugees in Iceland (2015–2021), we conceptualize rural trust as a relational process shaped by mobility, memory, temporality, and hypervisibility. Situating our findings within trust and migration governance debates, we show how small localities manage responsibilities typically handled by larger bureaucracies and thereby exhibit an overlap between interpersonal and institutional trust. We argue for reflective approaches that account for mobility and temporality, consider common socio‐demographic features of sparsely populated areas, while recognizing the heterogeneity of rural areas.

From Advantage to Precarity: Evidence of Reversed Skill Returns Among Migrant Women With Caregiving Responsibilities

Felicia Istad, Yeonwoo Sim

Full text
Despite growing policy interest in attracting diverse talent, little is known about how family dynamics and caregiving responsibilities influence the labour market integration of skilled migrant workers. This article examines the employment of migrants in South Korea, focusing on the role of skill levels, gender, and caregiving responsibilities in shaping labour market outcomes. Applying a Difference‐in‐Differences approach to national survey data ( N = 21,018), this study evaluates the stability of migrant employment amid labour market disruptions. The results show that migrants experienced an overall four percentage‐point decline in employment, with a much sharper reduction among highly educated mothers, whose employment probability fell by 18.6 percentage points. This finding illuminates the compounded effects of human capital, gender, and family care duties on migrant labour market outcomes. The study contributes to research on migrant employment by demonstrating the gendered precarization of skills, emphasizing the role of caregiving responsibilities. The results underscore the need for family‐friendly policies that address the specific vulnerabilities of female migrants in order to enhance their economic incorporation and long‐term retention in the labour force.

Contingent Infrastructures Between Italy and the Philippines: Intermediating Labour Mobility at the Fringes of Formal Recruitment Systems

Fabio De Blasis, Paola Bonizzoni

Full text
The article examines the mobility of Filipinos to Italy through the lens of contingent infrastructures: loosely coordinated and improvised assemblages of actors and practices that enable migration in the absence of effective institutionalised transnational recruitment systems. Drawing on policy content analysis and 28 semi‐structured interviews conducted between 2023 and 2025, the paper analyses how these infrastructures operate within the opportunities opened by Italy's quota‐based labour migration system, both within and beyond the domestic work sector. The article argues that, despite the existence of highly formalised labour migration channels, mobility in this corridor largely depends on the bottom‐up activation of a heterogeneous and loosely coordinated set of actors, including long‐established migrants, Italian employers, and legal‐administrative intermediaries operating across Italy and the Philippines. These actors collectively bridge institutional gaps created by weak coordination between the Italian and Philippine migration regimes and by the absence of effective institutionalised transnational recruitment mechanisms capable of matching labour supply and demand in a sector that operates largely through word of mouth. The findings show that contingent infrastructures are deeply ambivalent. On the one hand, they enable migrants to access mobility opportunities and navigate restrictive migration policies. On the other hand, dependence on brokerage, interpersonal ties, and the discretionary practices of for‐profit intermediaries exposes migrants to uncertainty, high costs and potential exploitation.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Political Fracturing within the Asian American Electorate

Ning Soong, Scott Duxbury

Full text
Racial minority political influence may be jeopardized when constituent subgroups disagree on policy priorities and on the political parties that best represent their interests. In this study, we examine the prevalence of political fracturing— the formation of pluralistic subgroups that coalesce around political ideology, identity, trust, engagement, and issue opinions within broader racial groups—within the Asian American electorate, an increasingly influential minority group. We capitalize on granular data from the 2016 Collaborative Multiracial Post-Election Survey to assess the prevalence and characterizing features of political fracturing, and we test several possible explanations of political fracturing among Asian Americans, especially those that relate to ethnic variation within the Asian American electorate. Latent class analysis uncovers three distinct classes of roughly equal sizes that broadly align with liberal, conservative, and politically ambivalent groups. Logistic regressions reveal that class membership is predicted by respondent ethnicity, and that age, education, income, and religious identification partly explain effects from ethnicity. These findings uncover evidence of political fracturing among Asian Americans that is not captured by isolated analyses of issue opinion and political participation and suggest that a focus on broad racial categories in minority group politics research may mask considerable political subgroup heterogeneity within minority racial groups.

Home Country Influence or Local Issues?—A Study of New York State Senate District 17 Elections in 2022 and 2024

Loren Collingwood, Miao Wang

Full text
Do American voters of East Asian immigrant backgrounds decide their vote choice based on international issues related to their home countries, or are immigrant-dense communities focused on domestic issues like most American voters? We evaluate these questions using two state senate elections (2022 and 2024) in a highly dense immigrant district (17th State Senate District, Brooklyn, New York), comparing the electoral impact of a high-salience international issue (Taiwan) against a divisive domestic issue (a proposed homeless shelter). We develop measures for both factors and test them with precinct-level data. We find that despite the high share of immigrant-background voters and growing attention to the Taiwan issue in American politics in recent years, the homeless shelter issue dominated the subnational electoral politics of this district. We buttress these results with two analyses of East Asian CMPS respondents, finding that domestic economic issues are more important to these voters than international concerns.

Biracial at the Ballot Box: Exploring the Association Between Interracial Relationships and Political Beliefs

Byron Miller, Madelyn Chandler, Nico Allen

Full text
Despite the tremendous influence politicians have over the lives of the growing number of people in interracial relationships in the United States, little is known about the political beliefs of interracially partnered individuals. Using nationally representative data from the Add Health Study, we find people in interracial relationships generally have more liberal political views than those in same-race relationships, but differences are moderated by the individual’s race/ethnicity, sex, and marital status. Specifically, interracially partnered women have liberal-leaning beliefs, but individuals who are Asian, Hispanic, and married have more conservative-leaning views. Three-way interactions further reveal interracially married Asian and Black persons both have more liberal-leaning political beliefs. The impact the growing prevalence of interracial relationships may have on the future evolution of American political representation and policies is discussed.

In Pursuit of the Status Quo: ASWB’s Research, Grantmaking, and Regulatory Practices

Matthew DeCarlo, Mary Nienow

Full text
The Association of Social Work Boards (ASWB) Examination Program is used in all 50 states to regulate the practice of clinical social work and in the majority of states to regulate the practice of masters and bachelors-level social work. After releasing descriptive data demonstrating biases in exam pass rates by race, age, and dominant language, ASWB engaged in research and regulatory actions that violate social work ethics and psychometric best practices. This article will critique the research, grantmaking, and regulatory practices that support the ASWB Examination Program using extensive citations to psychometric standards, ASWB’s publications and exam documentation, and the researchers’ experiences engaging with ASWB to study the cause of exam score disparities. The analysis will reveal how, after their 2022 release of data demonstrating exam bias with respect to race, age, and language, ASWB funded researchers already affiliated with ASWB to support what it already tells test-takers in its exam guidebook–only structural factors bias exam scores, not psychometric flaws internal to the examination. Moreover, ASWB implemented solutions to exam bias without proper investigation and psychometric support. Because of ASWB’s position as the sole publisher and purchaser of licensing examinations, individual state boards are unable to make incremental changes to prevent biased ASWB examinations from closing the profession of social work to groups for whom the exam is invalid, unreliable, and unfair.

Uniquely Biased: How ASWB Exams Violate Psychometric Best Practices

Matthew DeCarlo, Mary Nienow

Full text
After publication of the 2022 Pass Rate Analysis demonstrating minoritized social workers pass at less than half the rate of White social workers, the Association of Social Work Board (ASWB) Examination Guidebook (ASWB, 2023a), revised its psychometric reporting of exam fairness from “statistically free of race and gender bias” to “differences in exam performance for…different demographic groups…is influenced by many factors external to the exam,” upstream of the examination in the workforce pipeline (p. 9). Focusing only on factors external to the exam ignores the possibility that the internal properties of the exam may be invalid, unreliable, and unfair. Race, class, culture, and other structural factors do not impact ASWB exams the same over time, with ASWB’s 2022 Exam Pass Rate Analysis reporting 10-13% reductions in bachelors and masters examination pass rates after the introduction of the 2018 exam blueprint. Using extensive references to ASWB’s public statements, this article will demonstrate how ASWB ignored evidence of examination flaws and presents external factors as the only possible explanation for disparities in pass rates. Beginning with the policy paradox created by national organizational disagreement on the cause and next steps on exam score inequities, this article will review the extant empirical evidence demonstrating how bias is encoded to the language and theories underlying the examination as well as psychometric shortcomings in ASWB’s exam validation process converge to create a uniquely biased exam.

Early-Career Research Funding Predicts Success in Scientific Careers: A Mathematical Model and Empirical Analysis of Matthew Effects

Yamaguchi Yuhei

Full text
Success breeds success. This simple idea—the Matthew effect, or cumulative advantage—is a classical theme in the science of science. Does obtaining a research grant very early in a researcher’s career, at the very beginning of their academic trajectory, create a performance gap that continues to widen for decades? We approach this question from two sides. First, we build a mathematical model in which talent, publications, research funding, and promotion mutually reinforce one another, and show that even when talent is held constant, the gap in research output between early grant recipients and non-recipients widens over time. We distil this prediction into two hypotheses. Second, we test these hypotheses using a large panel dataset of approximately 40,000 researchers spanning eight broad fields of Japanese academia, drawn from the researchmap database. Using the graduate school of origin as a proxy for talent and prestige, we find that the advantage associated with receiving an early-career fellowship remains positive, statistically significant, and steadily increases over time even after accounting for differences in the institution of origin. In short, even a head start lasting only a few years leaves a lasting mark on a researcher’s career, with its effects continuing to grow more than a decade later. By combining an explicit model of cumulative advantage with large-scale empirical evidence across fields, we quantify how the earliest allocation of research resources shapes the long-term stratification of science.

Contextualising Urban Change: A Novel Classification System for Measuring Built Environment Change Across Great Britain

Jess Hepburn, Fiona Caryl, Rich Mitchell, Richard Patterson, Laura Macdonald, Alexandros Sfyridis, Daniela Fecht, Jenna Panter

Full text
Public health policy increasingly recognises the influence of built environments on health outcomes. However, most spatial datasets cannot track context-dependent environmental changes over time, a critical limitation when assessing the health impacts of urban development. This study develops the Built Environment Context and Change Atlas (BECCA), a novel methodology quantifying context-dependent built environment changes across Great Britain (2015-2020). Using the best available national spatial data, we integrated information on buildings, roads, and land cover, applying multivariate clustering to classify both environmental context and changes at 500m resolution. Stakeholders guided the definition of meaningful change categories. BECCA identified that 11% of GB cells underwent change, with substantial variation in development patterns across Functional Urban Areas, ranging from peripheral expansion to infill growth. While developed for Great Britain, this methodological framework is adaptable to other national contexts with similar spatial data availability, offering a template for international environmental change monitoring. By addressing critical gaps in existing environmental change data, BECCA provides a robust foundation for future research linking built environment dynamics to health outcomes, supporting evidence-based urban planning decisions.

Watching the walls go up: r/wallstreetbets segregated after the GameStop squeeze

Kevin Andrej Quiring

Full text
Online communities keep ideas apart. Across Hacker News and Reddit, concept pairs that should co-occur but never have stay apart at rates far below chance. We call this a wall. The exception was r/wallstreetbets: at chance before 2020, walled by 2024 like everywhere else. Here we report a registered study of when that happened, using rolling quarterly windows over a fresh single-source corpus of 98.1 million Reddit posts and comments, 2019 to 2024. Three predictions committed in advance all pass. The community moves from chance-level mixing to persistent walls, onset 2021-04-01, with zero reversions. In the two windows straddling the January 2021 GameStop episode, suppressed ticker pairs co-mention 28.6 and 30.9 standard deviations above chance. It is the only above-chance excursion this research program has observed, and shuffled data never produce it. Analysis-oriented control communities stay walled through the squeeze, so the excursion is specific to r/wallstreetbets. Two causes were in play, thirty-fold growth during the squeeze and a moderation overhaul after it, and we decided in advance that a mid-2021 onset could point to either. Segregation transitions in online communities can be measured and dated, but their timing alone cannot identify the mechanisms behind them.

Ideas that never meet online mark divided communities, not future discoveries

Kevin Andrej Quiring

Full text
In science, two well-studied ideas never discussed together make a likely candidate for a future discovery. Predicting which pair connects next is the aim of literature-based discovery. Whether this holds in online discussion is untested. Here we test it on 200 thousand Hacker News discussions from 2015 to 2017, with a pre-registered replication on 41.5 million Reddit finance posts, 2017 to 2024, using stock tickers. Four pre-registered evaluations use threads, then authors, as documents. Pairs never discussed together, called suppressed pairs, connect no more than popularity and overlapping topics predict. On the Science4Cast benchmark of AI papers, where the effect is known, the same method finds it. The standard test against chance flags connections too easily when document sizes vary. It reported 19 to 24 percent of pairs connecting within authors' quarterly output, yet shuffled data connected more pairs than real data. A per-pair permutation test leaves new connections no higher than false positives produce. Suppressed pairs appear together far below chance: nearly 9 standard deviations at author level, over 100 at thread level. Both results replicate across the 2020-2021 market regime change. Ideas that never meet online mark communities that stay apart, not future discoveries waiting to happen.

Well-intended policy gone wrong? Voluntary Surrenders as a de facto amnesty for illegal wildlife use and trade in Colombia

Natalia Munoz Cassolis, Johana M. Herrera Montoya, Douglas MacMillan, Francisco Perera Rieder, Melissa Arias

Full text
The illegal wildlife trade (IWT) has been the focus of numerous international and national legal frameworks aiming to counter the harms it poses to biodiversity. The enforcement of these laws typically involves the seizure or confiscation of wildlife specimens, which have become a common metric for IWT reporting and a proxy for estimating its scale. Another commonly used, but less understood mechanism is the Voluntary Surrender of wildlife specimens. Our research examines the legal implications and practical implementation of Voluntary Surrenders in Colombia, where this administrative mechanism was originally designed as a transitional educational tool to raise awareness of the environmental and welfare harms of illegal wildlife pet keeping, and to encourage individuals to relinquish illegally held specimens. Drawing on a thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews with environmental legal experts, law enforcement officers, and conservation researchers and practitioners, we identified the typologies of actors and circumstances giving rise to Voluntary Surrenders, alongside the legal interpretation, implementation, and governance challenges linked to this mechanism. We demonstrate how this well-intentioned policy operates as a de facto amnesty for IWT in Colombia, enabling offenders to evade sanctions and law enforcement personnel to bypass burdensome sanctioning procedures. Consequently, Voluntary Surrenders have perpetuated impunity, normalized illicit wildlife possession, reinforced a notion of IWT as a non-serious crime, and obscured its true scale, as these incidents are systematically excluded from national and international reporting frameworks, such as the CITES Illegal Trade database. We conclude that an urgent revision of this regulatory framework is needed.

AI in Science and its Implications: A Narrative Review

Philip Shapira, John Nelson, Julie Jebsen, Seoyoon Kang

Full text
This narrative review synthesizes diverse empirical, technical, conceptual, and policy literature on the development and use of AI in science. Drawing on a sociology of science and technology framing, we examine how the adoption of AI may redistribute scientific work, expertise, epistemic authority, resources, and accountability across organizational and infrastructural contexts. The literature suggests three cross-cutting patterns. First, AI tends to redistribute rather than eliminate work, creating new demands for configuration, monitoring, interpretation, and validation. Second, gains in productivity and novelty are uneven and depend on problem characteristics, data, infrastructure, and complementary human expertise. Third, AI may shift authority and institutional power toward actors that develop, own, and govern models and computational resources. AI also raises contested questions about explanation, evidence, creativity, responsibility, inequality, and environmental costs. We identify priorities for research on the epistemic status of AI-generated knowledge, inequalities in access to infrastructure and training, disciplinary variations, changing forms of accountability and institutional authority, and the governance of AI in and for science.

Correcting molecular myopia in chemical risk governance: hormonal infrastructure as a conceptual lens

Taneli Rajala, Nina Janasik

Full text
Widespread declines in human fecundity and biodiversity signal a creeping crisis that links to proliferating chemical pollution. Problematically, the risk governance of chemicals is mired with laboratory-level molecular knowledge that poorly matches with population-level threats arising from cumulative, chronic exposures to multiple borderline toxicants. We identify this molecular myopia as a fundamental misalignment between the scales at which chemical governance operates and the scales at which ecotoxicological research raises alarm—a misalignment reproduced in ongoing EU regulatory reforms despite their protective ambitions. In this conceptual work, we apply institutional theory and science and technology studies to explicate the key role of scale alignment in enabling effective governance intervention. Exchangeable Versus Age- structured Priors in Small-Area Estimation of Adult Mortality From Sibling Survival Histories

Shorful Alam, Nazmul Haque, Abdul Mazed, Md Foysal Ahmed

Full text
Sibling survival histories provide adult mortality estimates for countries lacking civil registration, but age-sex disaggregation often leaves cells sparse. Analysts routinely smooth these estimates using either an exchangeable prior, which borrows strength across all cells, or an age-structured prior, which borrows from neighboring ages, a choice rarely justified explicitly. We compared empirical Bayes, Bayesian IID, and age-structured (RW2) smoothing of visibility-corrected estimates from the 2024 Nigeria and 2016 Nepal Demographic and Health Surveys, chosen for contrasting sparsity (37–93 versus 8–30 effective deaths per cell), plus a simulation under known ground truth. In Nigeria's denser cells, the two priors agreed closely; in Nepal's sparser cells, six of 14 diverged materially, four female age groups showed nominally significant divergence, and three remained significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Raw sparsity did not predict which cells diverged: simulations show divergence instead reflects genuine local departures from the broader age pattern, which age-structured priors recover more faithfully than exchangeable priors, at a modest cost that includes spreading artificial input distortions to neighboring cells. Empirical Bayes intervals also badly undercovered when the true pattern was smooth. We recommend routine reporting of exchangeable-versus-structured divergence as a standard part of small-area demographic estimation.

Compensability in composite lifestyle and well-being indices: a simulation study of power-mean aggregation

Aamir Saeed Malik, Ahmed H. Hassan, Waqas Rasheed, Shahid Bashir

Full text
Composite indices of well-being and health almost always combine domain scores using an unweighted arithmetic mean. This choice permits unlimited compensation between domains: a high score in one domain can wholly offset a collapsed score in another. Whether that is appropriate depends on what the index is for, yet the choice is rarely stated as a choice. We examine its consequences for a five-domain lifestyle index, comparing four members of the power-mean family across four data-generating mechanisms in a Monte Carlo study of 100,000 profiles, reported following the ADEMP structure. Three findings follow. First, the arithmetic mean has a uniform gradient: every domain returns the same marginal gain, so the operator supplies no ordering over which domain to improve. Non-compensatory operators identify the weakest domain with probability one. Second, the arithmetic mean permits substantial masking: where one domain has collapsed below 50, between 4.7% and 35.9% of such profiles still score at or above the conventional adequacy threshold of 70, against 0.2% to 9.5% under the harmonic mean. Third, disagreement is concentrated, not general: rankings agree closely for correlated, mostly high scores but diverge sharply for imbalanced profiles. Aggregation therefore encodes a normative judgement about substitutability. Which operator to use follows from purpose: the arithmetic mean to summarise attainment or rank units; an exponent between −0.25 and −0.75 to direct effort at the weakest domain while limiting false alarms; and not the minimum, which flags three quarters of adequate profiles as inadequate once measurement error is admitted.

Simulation-based inference for cultural evolution: a tutorial with baby name data

Mason Youngblood

Full text
Cultural evolutionary models are usually easy to simulate but difficult to fit, because most have no tractable likelihood. Simulation-based inference (SBI) addresses this by treating the simulator itself as the likelihood, and now ranges from rejection-based approximate Bayesian computation (ABC) to amortized neural posterior estimation (NPE). Knowing which to use is hard, partly because they are rarely compared on the same model and data. In this tutorial I fit a Wright-Fisher-style model of baby name popularity to uncensored female naming records from South Australia (1944-2017, with inference on 1981-2017), comparing rejection ABC, ABC with random forests, and NPE with BayesFlow on one shared set of 10,000 simulations. The model includes frequency dependence (β), an age-dependent bias (α), and random variation (γ). Recovery varies widely across parameters and methods, from r = 0.96-1.00 for β to r = 0.39-0.97 for γ, and these differences track what each estimator does with the summary statistics it is given and, for γ, how much of the signal those statistics retain. I use the comparison to work through what makes a parameter identifiable, the diagnostics that indicate whether a posterior can be trusted, and common failure modes. All code and data are provided.

Productive Policy Competition and Asymmetric Extremism

Alexander V. Hirsch

Full text
Viable policies must be developed by individuals and groups with the expertise and willingness to do so. We develop a model in which competing policy-motivated developers differ in their intrinsic ideological extremism and ability at crafting high quality policies. The unique equilibrium exhibits unequal participation, inefficiently unpredictable and extreme policies and outcomes, wasted effort, and an apparent advantage for extreme policies. While asymmetries between the developers always reduce observable competition, they can nevertheless benefit the decisionmaker. This contrasts starkly with the classic all-pay contest -- where the decisionmaker is always harmed by asymmetries due to the discouragement effect -- and results from the developers' policy motivations. I also introduce and solve a broader class of generalized policy contests that nests both the policy-development model and the all-pay contest, and develop new methods for solving asymmetric contests with spillovers. Broadly, my analysis provides a novel rationale for why extreme actors may dominate policymaking that is rooted in the nature of policy development, and highlights the difficulty in assessing the normative implications of such dominance.

Wiebke Dorfs, Maximilian Hampel, Joanna J. Bryson

Full text
Technological progress plays a crucial role in international relations. In particular, innovation has long been theorized as instrumental to power transitions. It is therefore unsurprising that the return of China as a global superpower and its technological advances has triggered considerable discussion. One narrative that has emerged is that of a binary "AI Cold War" competition between China and the US, similar to the 1947-1991 competition between nuclear superpowers. Another though is that the digital and information revolutions should facilitate technology and therefore power diffusion. Full text
The August 2026 Bhote Koshi–Trishuli disaster was not simply another flood. Religious Leaders and Distributive Politics in Postwar Italy

Full text
How do unelected local leaders shape distributive politics? Juan David Gelvez

Full text
Do voters hold politicians accountable for climate-related disasters? Existing research offers competing explanations: some argue that voters punish incumbents for poor disaster management, others emphasize the electoral rewards of effective relief, and still others highlight contextual conditions—such as disaster timing and intensity—that moderate these effects. This paper develops and tests a conditional accountability framework that integrates these perspectives, arguing that the political consequences of disasters depend jointly on disaster exposure and the credibility of government response. Using a novel dataset of over 47,000 climate-related disasters in Colombia (1998–2021), linked to municipality-level presidential election results, I estimate fixed-effects models capturing within-municipality and within-cycle variation in disaster exposure and relief. The results show that disasters generally reduce incumbent vote share when they are severe or occur close to elections, but that timely and visible relief can partially offset these effects. These findings unify competing theories of disaster politics and show how accountability under climate stress depends on the interaction between citizen evaluation and government strategy.

Recovering Collective Structure in Congressional Voting: A Fused Latent Factor and Network Approach

Hane Lee, Andrew Davison

Full text
Congressional voting reflects collective behavior structured by chamber-wide patterns such as partisanship and relational patterns among particular subsets of legislators, including factions and coalitions. Yet latent factor frameworks that dominate roll call analysis assume conditional independence across legislators and represent their ideal positions in a linear space. These restrictions can obscure relational patterns that do not align with chamber-wide latent dimensions. To model these behaviors, we adopt a fused latent factor and network model that captures chamber-wide patterns with the latent factors and attributes residual structure to the network. Applying this fused model to the 101st (1989--90) and 114th (2015--16) Senates, we first validate that the latent factors recover partisan voting patterns and identify two systematic failures of the latent factor: senators with moderate latent positions are assigned uncertain probabilities on party-line votes, and intra-party split votes exhibit systematic co-defection patterns. The network component closes the misprediction gap entirely for party-line votes but only partially for intra-party splits, capturing stable co-defection patterns while leaving more transient coalitions less predictable. Next, we apply a degree-corrected stochastic block model to the fitted network and recover interpretable voting blocs in both Senates. The 101st Senate features within-party factions that match historically documented coalitions and are driven by shared issue interests despite overlapping positions in the latent space. In the 114th Senate, the blocks take on an entirely different interpretation, uncovering two institutionally opposed coalitions: a cross-party governing bloc, including members of the Republican party leadership, and a Republican insurgent bloc, both driven by opposition of the other. Our findings demonstrate that roll call data alone contains recoverable signals of collective legislative behavior beyond partisanship that remain after accounting for chamber-wide partisan structure, and the organization of this collective behavior shifts from issue-based coalitions toward institutional conflict across decades of polarization.

Iván Williams, Diego Alburez-Gutierrez

Full text
BACKGROUND Kin deaths are universal, but their frequency, timing, and position within family networks vary across demographic regimes. Existing demographic research describes kin loss, but provides limited tools for summarizing its cumulative and life-course consequences. OBJECTIVE We develop a demographic framework to compare bereavement regimes across populations, focusing on three dimensions: cumulative burden, loss of shared lifetime, and generational overlap in kin deaths. METHODS We derive three indicators from age-specific kinship dynamics: bereavement burden, defined from kin-years lived after kin death; shared time lost, measured as expected kin-years of potential coexistence removed by death; and generational overlap, measuring coincidence between age-specific death schedules of different kin types. The social structure of metacognition: the role of socioeconomic status and gender on students' self-assessment accuracy

Francesca Burato, Jan Skopek

Full text
Educational inequalities are well documented, yet little is known about whether students’ ability to accurately evaluate their own academic performance is itself socially structured. This study examines socioeconomic and gender differences in metacognitive calibration – the alignment between students’ subjective assessments and objective performance – across compulsory schooling in Germany. Drawing on nationally representative data from the German National Educational Panel Study, we analyse mathematics and reading calibration from Grade 1 to Grade 9. Using fractional response models with flexible performance functions, we distinguish overall group differences in miscalibration from differences net of objective competence and examine how these patterns vary across the achievement distribution. Results show that objective competence is by far the strongest predictor of calibration: lower-performing students substantially overestimate their performance, and miscalibration declines with grade level. Reading differences are considerably smaller. These findings suggest that metacognitive calibration is strongly anchored in competence, while gendered patterns of self-evaluation may constitute a distinct dimension of educational inequality.

Four Kinds of Redundancy: Item Recoverability Across Five Survey Instruments

Florian Waldner

Full text
Surveys often ask for more than they need to and some answers can be predicted from the other answers in the same questionnaire. This paper develops a measure for how much redundancy a questionnaire contains. Unlike work on shortening psychometric scales, the object here is the whole instrument including questions covering unrelated topics with no common construct. For each item, the measure asks how well that item can be predicted from the rest of the questionnaire, excluding the question block the items belongs to. Predictions come from cross-validated random forests. The measure is a simple skill score, R2 for continuous items and the Brier skill score for categorical ones. Both compare the model against a marginal baseline. This places items of different types, and questionnaires of different lengths, on one scale. The measure is computed from collected responses and requires data from a fielded sample. We apply the measure to five survey instruments. Looking at the most recoverable items shows that recoverability is not one phenomenon. It arises from different sources. It also reveals that few items could be dropped without losing information. The measure is therefore most useful as a diagnostic for questionnaire design.

Emergence is not a phenomenon: Consilience as aspiration rather than metaphysic

John Nelson

Full text
Scientists and philosophers invoke the term “emergence” to justify separations, or to stipulate relationships, between different human explanatory schemes, often held to constitute a hierarchy of scale or detail: physics, chemistry, biology, psychology, economics, sociology. Emergence is used to name or (allegedly) explain the inescapable experience that, though we believe bigger things are made of smaller ones, “higher-order” descriptions of larger things are often dramatically more effective than are attempts to reconstruct them from their constituent parts. This is often taken as a deep epistemological or ontological phenomenon or mystery. But this mystery appears only if we assume that one of our theories has already managed to fully capture the contours of some piece of reality. If we drop this assumption and view theories merely as limited and provisional toolkits for coping with different sorts of human problems, gulfs between paradigms cease to be ontological mysteries and become mere ignorance. The problem of emergence as a general phenomenon (rather than as a grab bag of specific puzzles about whether and how any theory can hook up to any others), can therefore be dissolved. This dissolution would help level out the hierarchy of the sciences and decrease epistemic hubris, with salutary implications for collective action and research strategy. We can still aspire to hook all our theories up with one another, but we need not assume that the world must go along with the attempt.

The Long‐Term Trends in Cohort Fertility: Global, Regional and National Estimates and Projections for Cohorts Born in 1935–2055

Giulia Gonnella, Vladimíra Kantorová, Mark C. Wheldon, Patrick Gerland

Full text
This study presents the first global dataset of fertility estimates and projections for cohorts of women born between 1935 and 2055. Leveraging the World Population Prospects 2024 revision, which provides single‐year single‐age fertility indicators for 1950–2100, we reconstructed cohort fertility profiles for all countries. These data offer a unique opportunity to analyze cohorts that have completed childbearing and those entering reproductive ages, extending analyses beyond countries traditionally covered by the Human Fertility Database (HFD). Cohort measures were derived by transforming period age‐specific fertility rates into Lexis triangles and aggregating them into cohort parallelograms, following HFD methodology. Validation against observed cohort data confirmed high accuracy of our estimates. Results revealed that cohort fertility declines have been more gradual than period declines, with substantial regional heterogeneity. Europe and Northern America reached replacement‐level cohort fertility with the 1946 cohort, while sub‐Saharan Africa is projected to remain above 2.3 births per woman for the 2055 cohort. In low‐fertility countries, completed cohort fertility is projected to fall below 1.5 births for the 1983 cohort in many cases. These findings underscore the stability of cohort measures compared to period indicators and highlight their value for understanding long‐term fertility dynamics and informing demographic research and policy.

Fertility Dynamics across High‐Income Countries in a New Era of Polycrisis

Samuel Plach, Arnstein Aassve, Nicolò Cavalli, Matteo Valetto

Full text
The COVID‐19 pandemic opened a polycrisis of interrelated disruptions and compounding economic and subjective uncertainty that likely shaped fertility in high‐income countries, especially in its second phase, in which the Ukraine war was a major source and amplifier of uncertainty. Yet fertility evidence remains largely limited to the pandemic. We estimate monthly “excess” total fertility rates (TFRs) and find that declines compared to projected pre‐polycrisis trends were short‐lived during the first phase and excess TFRs overall positive in all 26 sample countries except Eastern Europe and Portugal. Instead, during the second phase, excess TFRs are strongly and increasingly negative in Eastern and Central Europe, Greece, Portugal, France, Denmark, and Sweden. Investigating potential explanatory variables, we find that the total, energy, and housing consumer price indices and reversed consumer confidence are negatively associated with excess TFRs, especially in Eastern and Central Europe, Greece, France, Denmark, and Sweden. Refugee inflows, the economic policy uncertainty index, and the geopolitical risk index show weaker negative associations. But country heterogeneity is large. Our analysis suggests that fertility dynamics are closely linked to the polycrisis, citizens’ economic conditions and confidence are the strongest correlates, and extensive welfare regimes alone may not consistently sustain fertility under accumulating uncertainty.

Working from home among older adults in the UK: examining its link to labor market exit intentions and expected retirement age

Yanan Zhang, Sarah Harper, Senhu Wang, Bing Gong

Full text
Prolonging work life has been widely discussed in countries facing population aging. We examine the association between working from home (WFH) and both the labor market exit intentions and expected retirement age among the older workforce in the UK, utilizing data from the harmonized British Household Panel Survey (BHPS) and the UK Household Longitudinal Survey (UKHLS), covering the period from September 1998 to May 2020. Findings indicate that WFH is linked to reduced labor market exit intentions and a later expected retirement age, through improved subjective well-being. The association between WFH and expected retirement age is significantly stronger among women than among men. The estimated associations for the labor market exit intentions are also larger among women, although the corresponding gender interaction terms are not statistically significant. Additionally, the specific domains of subjective well-being that mediate the link between WFH and the labor market exit intentions differ between men and women. These insights support enhancing WFH policies to keep older adults economically active, which improves their well-being and advances gender equality by prolonging older women’s careers.

Human rights and populist wrongs: how populist incumbents threaten democracy, 1990–2023

Wade M Cole

Full text
This paper leverages variation in respect for human rights to intervene in ongoing debates over the consequences of populism for democracy. Compiling data for 103 democratic countries between 1990 and 2023, and using a novel cross-national classification of populist leaders and parties, I examine the relationships between left-wing, centrist, and right-wing populist incumbency and 18 measures of civil, political, and social rights. Two-way fixed-effects and heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences analyses reveal that populists do not attack human rights indiscriminately. Instead, left- and right-wing populists preside over sharp declines in core political rights that are essential to free and fair elections, including the freedoms of speech, association, and the press. Their effects on civil and social rights are more limited and vary by populist subtype, while centrist populists generally exhibit weaker effects overall. These patterns suggest that left- and right-wing populists violate human rights strategically, concentrating on rights that enable political opposition, public criticism, and democratic accountability. The findings imply that populism is destructive not merely of liberal democracy, as commonly argued, but of democracy itself. More broadly, they demonstrate the value of disaggregating populism and human rights for understanding threats to contemporary democracy.

Do caring fathers earn less? Earnings consequences of fathers’ parental leave use

Corinna Frodermann, Ann-Christin Bächmann, Andreas Filser

Full text
In many Western societies, policy efforts seek to increase fathers’ involvement in parental leave, yet many fathers remain reluctant due to concerns about earnings consequences. Despite these concerns, the consequences of parental leave and their underlying mechanisms remain understudied. Using novel German administrative data on 109,735 married fathers who became first-time parents between 2007 and 2013, we analyze their earnings trajectories following parental leave. The large sample allows for investigating potential differences in earnings trajectories between fathers who took no parental leave and fathers who took leave of different durations. To improve comparability between the different groups of fathers, we apply two-way fixed-effects models weighted by coarsened exact matching. Moreover, the data enables us to consider differences in post-birth work volume as potential confounder for differences in earnings trajectories. We find that fathers who take parental leave experience lower earnings trajectories compared with those without leave. This difference increases with leave duration: 7 years after the birth of their first child, fathers who took up to 2 months of leave see their annual earnings increase 2.2 percentage points less relative to their pre-birth baseline than fathers who took no leave—for those who took longer leave, the gap is 12.8 percentage points. While these gaps initially appear disproportionately larger for longer leave durations, the disparity diminishes once we account for differences in active employment and part-time transitions. Moreover, the differences in work volume explain a large share of the observed earnings gaps, reducing them to 1 and 4.6 percentage points.

School preferences across countries and social groups: a factorial survey experiment in the United States, Germany and Italy

Guido Salza, Moris Triventi

Full text
This paper examines whether evaluations of primary-school characteristics vary across national and social contexts. Using a factorial survey experiment with more than 2500 respondents in the United States, Germany, and Italy, participants evaluated hypothetical schools differing in six attributes. By holding information, cost, and proximity constant, the design isolates stated preferences from several constraints that shape observed school choices. Multilevel models estimate the effects of school attributes on enrolment intention, perceived academic effectiveness, and expected social integration. Two findings stand out. First, respondents with and without university education evaluate school characteristics in remarkably similar ways, providing limited evidence that school preferences are strongly stratified by educational background. Second, although the general pattern of evaluations is broadly similar across countries, the relative effects of particular attributes vary: U.S. respondents are more responsive to school performance levels, Italian respondents to academic growth, and extracurricular activities and parental involvement also differ in salience across contexts. Respondents react significantly to all six school attributes across the three evaluative dimensions, indicating that school preferences are shaped by multiple academic, social, and organizational considerations. Overall, our findings suggest that cross-national and social inequalities in school attendance are unlikely to be explained by preference differences alone.

Varieties of Firm Republicanism: Institutional Models of Workplace Governance

Sean Irving

Full text
Recent scholarship treats the firm as a regime of power in which workers are subject to arrangements analogous to those between state and citizen. Republican political theory is influential within these debates, identifying domination, the exposure to arbitrary power, as an obstacle to equal dignity and rights within firms. This article develops a new framework for understanding republican approaches to firm governance. It identifies three models: the regulatory-consultative model, which constrains managerial supervision; the workplace-democracy model, which addresses exclusion from governance; and the worker-ownership model, which treats ownership rights as the central source of domination. The article establishes the first systematic typology of republican institutional responses to domination in the firm and develops a configurational approach to their application. Rather than treating these models as rival blueprints or requiring their simultaneous application, it argues that the appropriate institutional response depends on the setting in which domination takes shape. This offers a new way of thinking about how relations of equal dignity and rights can be secured within firms.

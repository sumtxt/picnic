Correcting for Nonignorable Nonresponse Bias in Ordinal Observational Survey Data

Lukáš Lafférs, Jozef Michal Mintal, Ivan Sutóris

Full text
Many political surveys rely on post-stratification, raking or related weighting adjustments to align respondents with the target population. But when respondents differ from nonrespondents on the outcome itself (nonignorable nonresponse), these adjustments can fail, introducing bias even into basic descriptives. We provide a practical method that corrects for nonignorable nonresponse by leveraging response-propensity proxies (e.g., a respondent’s rating of the interview or interviewer-coded cooperativeness) observed among respondents to extrapolate toward nonrespondents, while directly integrating observable covariates and retaining the benefits of post-stratification with known population shares. The method generalizes the variable-response-propensity framework of Peress (2010) from binary to ordinal outcomes, which are widely used to measure trust, satisfaction and policy attitudes. The resulting estimator is computed by maximum likelihood and implemented in a compact R routine that handles both ordinal and binary outcomes. Using the 2024 American National Election Study, we show that accounting for nonignorable nonresponse produces substantively meaningful shifts for life satisfaction (estimated latent correlation ρ ≈ 0.47 $\rho \approx 0.47$ rho almost equals 0.47 ), while yielding only modest changes for retrospective economic evaluations ( ρ ≈ 0.14 $\rho \approx 0.14$ rho almost equals 0.14 ), highlighting when nonignorable nonresponse substantively affects survey estimates.

Get Out the Volunteer: Encouraging Diverse Political Party Activism

Jonne Kamphorst, Horacio Larreguy

Full text
Political parties in advanced democracies face a dual challenge: fewer citizens are willing to volunteer, and those who remain active are disproportionately older and male. This is troubling given the central role that volunteers play in campaigns and party processes. Whether party leaders can recruit a larger and more diverse activist base remains an open question. We partnered with a major party in the Netherlands and conducted two field experiments before the 2021 general and 2022 municipal elections, targeting over 35,000 party members. The party randomized whether members received a personal call, an email or both. Results show that calls—where members could voice their opinions—increased volunteer sign-up rates (83% in Study 1 and 361% in Study 2), especially among younger members. By contrast, emails had no effect. These findings clarify mixed evidence in the literature, suggesting that voice opportunities are key to expanding and diversifying the activist base.

American Journal of Political Science

Designing multi‐site studies for external validity: Site selection via synthetic purposive sampling

Naoki Egami, Diana Da In Lee

Full text
Multi‐site/context studies have become popular strategies to address the most common and challenging external validity concerns about contexts. Under such studies, scholars conduct causal studies in each site and evaluate whether findings generalize across sites. Despite the potential, there has been little guidance on the fundamental research design question—how should we select sites for external validity? Existing approaches have challenges: random sampling of sites is often infeasible, while the current practice of purposive sampling is suboptimal without statistical guarantees. We propose synthetic purposive sampling (SPS), which optimally selects diverse sites for external validity. SPS combines ideas of purposive sampling and the synthetic control method—it selects diverse sites such that nonselected sites are well approximated by the weighted average of the selected sites. We illustrate its general applicability using both experimental and observational studies. Overall, this paper offers a new statistical foundation to design multisite studies for external validity.

British Journal of Political Science

Calculation and Conscience: Motivations for the Substantive Representation of Ethnic Minorities

Stephanie Zonszein, Apurav Bhatiya, William Dinneen, Guy Grossman

Full text
Existing work shows that minority legislators are more likely to represent their group’s interests compared to dominant group legislators. However, it is unclear whether this is due to intrinsic motivations or electoral incentives. We use a regression discontinuity design (RDD) to analyze ethnic minority representation in the UK Parliament. By comparing white MPs who narrowly beat minority candidates to minority MPs who narrowly beat white candidates, the RDD controls for electoral incentives since it holds constant constituency factors correlated with a minority parliamentary win. Analyzing over 1 million parliamentary questions and speeches, we find that minority MPs are more likely than white MPs to discuss issues important to ethnic minorities. Additional evidence suggests that narrowly elected MPs face similar electoral incentives and that minority MPs representing minorities face re-election penalties. Our findings are consistent with the idea that minority substantive representation is driven at least in part by intrinsic motivations.

A Few Good Women? Gendered Socialization and Norms of Civilian Protection in United States Military Training

Andrew Bell, Thomas Gift, Jonathan Monten, Chiara Ruffa

Full text
Many professionalized militaries seek to train combatants in “norms of restraint” (NoR) based on international legal principles of noncombatant immunity. Does training in NoR affect the attitudes of women and men toward the protection of civilians differently? Drawing on unique surveys of United States Army officer cadets at the US Military Academy (USMA), we find that while women and men held similar attitudes at the start of their academy training, women’s attitudes shifted more toward civilian restraint, despite receiving identical formal training in international humanitarian law (IHL), combat ethics, and officership. To explore the causes of this gender divergence, we conduct open-ended, in-depth interviews with former USMA cadets. We find that socialization at USMA is deeply gendered, and that differences in norm socialization are linked to a prevailing “gender order” constructed by formal and informal rules, practices, and norms that attribute distinct roles to women and men in military settings. Despite the seemingly “ungendered” nature of USMA’s intensive training in IHL and combat ethics, this gender order creates different socialization experiences for women and men, resulting in divergent attitudes toward civilian restraint. These findings shed light on how socialization and gender interact to shape combatant attitudes toward NoR, and are especially notable given the USMA’s status as the US Army’s “flagship of ethics education.”

Elections, Government Turnover, and Citizen Acceptance of Political Violence: Evidence from Election Panel Surveys in Zambia and Kenya

Inken von Borzyskowski, Michael Wahman

Full text
Elections in unconsolidated democracies are contentious moments with heightened risks of violence. Although elections offer hope to the politically marginalized, ruling parties often enjoy insurmountable advantages, sometimes leading to frustration and concerns for groups and citizens’ exclusion from power. How do elections shape citizens’ acceptance of political violence in unconsolidated democracies? Furthermore, can government turnovers reduce the support for political violence among citizens previously excluded from political power? We argue that citizens respond both to electoral outcomes and their perception of the electoral process when updating their attitudes on political violence. We leverage an original panel survey of the Zambia 2021 election to measure changes in attitudes to political violence before and after an election. Because the 2021 election resulted in a turnover of power, it provides a critical test to evaluate updated perceptions amid political change. We show that election winners reduce their support for political violence and election losers increase their acceptance of violence, even in the case of a turnover. We also show that increased perceptions of election quality reduce the acceptance of violence. Extending our study to an analysis of original panel data from the Kenyan 2017 election (a case without a turnover and low election quality), we find similar patterns.

Colonial Legacies and the Dynamics of Opposition: How Factional Power Shapes Cycles of Moderation and Radicalization

Nasir Almasri

Full text
This article explains why opposition movements in the Middle East cycle between radicalization and moderation over long periods. I argue that twentieth-century colonization shaped both exclusionary state institutions and radical opposition groups, structuring how later episodes of inclusion and exclusion influenced movement strategies. The paper explains subsequent oscillations through a single core mechanism: shifting factional balances of power. State inclusion and exclusion redistribute influence between moderates and radicals, generating shifts between confrontation and accommodation. I develop and evaluate the theory through longitudinal process tracing of the Muslim Brothers in Egypt and the Iraqi Communist Party, supplemented by shadow cases illustrating colonial presence (Palestine) and absence (Turkey), using evidence from 96 semistructured interviews, archival materials, and participant observation conducted between 2021 and 2024 in Palestine, Iraq, and Turkey. By foregrounding temporality and factional dynamics, the article reframes debates on Islamist moderation and authoritarian durability as path-dependent and iterative processes rather than linear transformations.

European Journal of Political Research

Overpromising populists? Why challenger parties make fewer, not more, promises than established parties

Mathias Bukh Vestergaard

Full text
Many Western democracies have experienced an increase in the prevalence of parties that have never been in government. Since these so-called challenger parties are not held accountable to the same extent as established parties, there is a concern that they will simply churn out promises and fuel a process of rising political distrust. This paper explores the extent to which this image of overpromising populists is accurate. Instead of expecting more promises from challenger parties, the paper argues that we should find the opposite. Using a novel dataset of over one million content-coded quasi-sentences from election manifestos, the paper empirically demonstrates that challenger parties make substantially fewer election promises than established parties and that this is likely due to their lack of governmental experience. These results have important implications for research on the growing phenomenon of challenger parties and their distinct communication in electoral campaigns.

Do emotions affect political trust judgments?

Camille Marie Landesvatter, Paul Cornelius Bauer

Full text
Our study explores the nature of political trust ratings in surveys. We provide novel empirical evidence that, in addition to informed and rational reasoning, emotional states play a role in trust evaluations. Leveraging a U.S. sample, we employ a novel smartphone survey methodology that captures real-time voice responses during the trust evaluation process. We then classify these responses using sentiment analysis and speech emotion recognition techniques. Our findings demonstrate a substantial presence of negative sentiment when respondents evaluate political trust. Moreover, the sentiment conveyed in these responses significantly impacts trust scores, with positive sentiment bolstering trust and negative sentiment leading to its decline. A more granular examination of emotions reveals limited variation with only one notable effect where respondents employing ‘happy’ language and paralinguistics exhibited higher trust scores.

Many shades of climate skepticism: Exploring climate change attitudes among local political elites in Germany

Anna-Sophie Heinze, Anne Küppers

Full text
Climate change – and skepticism toward it – is increasingly present not only among far-right parties but also across the political spectrum in more nuanced forms. This article examines the diverse climate attitudes of local political elites, who play a key role in the implementation of climate policy. We develop an analytical framework to assess varying degrees of climate skepticism, with particular attention to a broader ‘gray zone’ of attitudes that combine acceptance of anthropogenic climate change with doubts about its implications, responsibilities, or policy responses. We empirically examine the views of municipal council members in Saxony and Thuringia – two eastern German states characterized by salient climate and energy policy conflicts and ideologically competitive party systems. Using original web survey data, our findings reveal a wide spectrum of partial climate skepticism extending beyond the Alternative for Germany (AfD) and into parts of the political mainstream. Moreover, we identify substantial variation across different dimensions of climate skepticism as well as significant intra-party heterogeneity. Overall, parties are not monolithic actors on climate-related attitudes but internally diverse organizations in which multiple positions coexist. Understanding these local-level attitudes is crucial for assessing the political feasibility of climate policy implementation. By mapping the ‘gray zone’ of climate skepticism among local political elites, this study contributes to a more fine-grained understanding of climate attitudes within and across party lines.

Quarterly Journal of Political Science

Discretionary disclosure and electoral accountability

Kun Heo

Full text
Politicians often have discretion over what policy information to reveal to the public. This paper develops a formal model comparing electoral accountability under mandatory and discretionary disclosure. I identify a fundamental trade-off between detection − where voters directly observe wrongdoing − and inference − where voters penalize the suspicious absence of information. Mandates facilitate detection but eliminate the strategic signal embedded in discretionary disclosure. Mandates weaken the connection between silence and possible wrongdoing, effectively exonerating the incumbent in voters’ inferences. The model demonstrates that when the baseline probability of information transmission is high, the inference-based mechanism constrains rent-seeking more effectively than the detection-based mechanism. Consequently, disclosure mandates can paradoxically weaken electoral discipline: by making silence less informative, they undermine voters’ ability to draw inferences from silence, even while increasing the overall probability that voters observe policy outcomes.

Masking or lexicographic preferences? Using conjoint experiments to distinguish between two fundamental theories of choice

Simon Calmar Andersen, Morten Hjortskov

Full text
Many studies on candidate choice and other political preferences suggest that the effect of one attribute may mask the effect of another attribute, which the respondent has no information about. If, for instance, respondents lack information about party membership, they may infer that female candidates are Democrats, which would lead to biased estimates of the pure effect of candidate gender. We propose an alternative explanation—namely that respondents have lexicographic preferences—and we present an empirical test using conjoint experiments to distinguish between what we call pure masking and lexicographic preferences. We illustrate our method using data on the effect of gender, race, and party in candidate choice and replicate the results in a preregistered conjoint experiment. We offer recommendations for researchers studying candidate and political choices.

Revisiting the SciPop Scale: Measurement alignment, scoring rules, and cross‐context comparability

Xue‐Fei Yan, Zhihao Ma

Full text
Science‐related populism—populist attitudes toward scientific authority—has attracted growing scholarly attention. Although the SciPop Scale measures this construct, its cross‐national psychometric properties remain underexamined. Using data from the Trust in Science and Science‐Related Populism project ( N = 69,341; 68 countries/regions) and the Swiss Science Barometer ( N = 1124), we evaluate factor structure and reliability, test multigroup invariance, apply alignment for approximate invariance, and compare scoring philosophies (Bollen vs. Goertz approach) on criterion validity. We further employ multidimensional item response theory models to provide complementary item‐level diagnostics, including discrimination, threshold, item information, and differential item functioning. Results show that reliability is high and a four‐factor model fits most broadly. Full measurement invariance holds across sex, age, education, residence, political orientation, and religiosity; partial invariance appears across regions, economic development, cultural values, and regime types. Cross‐national comparability is limited but improves via measurement alignment. Different scoring methods identify different weakest dimensions. For criterion validity, Bollen‐based aligned scores consistently outperform the Goertz approach, yielding higher explanatory power in cross‐national and cross‐group analyses. Finally, we provide guidelines for researchers using the SciPop Scale and future directions for measuring science‐related populism.

Working hard to belong: Meritocratic ideology and deportation sentiments among Latino and Asian Americans

Nathan K. Chan, Jin X. Goh

Full text
Why do some Latino and Asian Americans support conservative policies that may discriminate against them? Elaborating on system justification theory, we argue that ideologies such as meritocracy perpetuate the status quo by placating marginalized groups and assuaging their emotional concerns regarding their unfair treatment. This research examined the extent to which endorsing meritocratic ideology is associated with deportation policy attitudes and the degree of emotional concern about deportation among Latino and Asian Americans. Using data from the 2024 and 2020 Collaborative Multiracial Post‐Election Surveys, we find that higher meritocratic ideology predicted greater support for deportation policies and lower emotional concern about deportation for Latino and Asian Americans. This research demonstrates that Latino and Asian Americans who have ingrained the ethos of meritocracy are more likely to support conservative policies and develop sentiments that work against the interests of their communities.

National security versus human security: Intrastate conflict and states’ rhetoric on conventional arms control in the United Nations general assembly

Tobias Risse

Full text
While arms control during the Cold War was primarily concerned with weapons of mass destruction and their role in national security, the end of the bipolar order shifted attention toward intrastate conflicts, the conventional weapons with which they are fought, and their humanitarian consequences for civilian populations. Yet we still lack systematic evidence on how governments directly involved in such conflicts perceive conventional arms control. I argue that acute intrastate conflicts, but not intractable conflicts, lead states to view conventional arms control primarily as a matter of national security rather than a humanitarian issue. I test this argument quantitatively, using data on states’ rhetorical positions on conventional arms control in the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) and on enduring internal rivalries (EIRs). Consistent with my expectations, I find that intrastate conflicts unrelated to EIRs are associated with a stronger emphasis on national security in UNGA speeches, whereas conflicts embedded in EIRs are not significantly related to states’ rhetoric on conventional arms control. These findings shed new light on the relationship between intrastate conflict and arms control.

Reassurance and betrayal: Cross-ethnic appeals by party leaders in divided societies lead to asymmetric voter reactions

John Garry

Full text
Why do conciliatory political appeals sometimes polarise rather than moderate electoral competition in divided societies? Existing accounts of consociational power-sharing explain how hardline parties can become credible negotiators, but they say less about how voters respond when the leaders of displaced moderate parties attempt to broaden support beyond their traditional community. I argue that leaders’ cross-ethnic appeals function as reassurance signalling: they reduce perceived threat to the outgroup while simultaneously weakening perceptions of defensive credibility among co-ethnics. Voters therefore update leader evaluations in opposite directions relative to a party’s inherited reputation. Using survey data from the 2022 Northern Ireland Assembly election, I compare leader evaluations to party baselines across identity groups. A leader who emphasised cross-community accommodation was evaluated more favourably by the opposing community and less favourably by co-ethnics, while leaders emphasising intra-group defence did not exhibit this pattern. The findings suggest moderation in divided societies redistributes support across groups rather than uniformly increasing approval, providing a behavioural mechanism within post-tribune party competition.

European Political Science Review

The international and transnational politics of the far right: an emergent research program

Thorsten Wojczewski

Full text
This article offers a state-of-the-art review of a new and growing research program in International Relations (IR) that analyzes the far right’s inter- and transnational politics. Responding to the rise of far-right leaders, parties, and movements worldwide, this nascent literature sheds light on the inter- and transnational drivers, patterns, connections, and effects of far-right politics. To further consolidate and develop this emerging research program, as this article argues, it is imperative to integrate this literature into a wider framework that delineates different analytical approaches and research agendas. For this purpose, the article identifies and examines five research strands: (1) foreign and security policy, (2) international thought, (3) the global causes of the rise of the far right, (4) trans- and international collaboration, and (5) international norms, institutions, and orders. It appraises their different analytical perspectives and insights, connects each research strand with various IR paradigms, subdisciplines, and literatures, and identifies unexplored lines of inquiry. Thereby, the article aims to broaden and diversify the theoretical, empirical, and methodological scope of this research program and to further facilitate synergies between IR and far-right studies. It then discusses a series of limitations and challenges facing this research program and proposes a level-of-analysis approach to advance it.

Living Activism: Biographical Trajectories and the Reconfiguration of Youth Participation

Roger Soler-i-Martí, Juan Carlos Revilla, Jorge Benedicto

Full text
Youth transitions to adulthood have become longer, more complex and diverse, yet these shifts are often overlooked in studies of political attitudes and behaviour, leading to contradictory conclusions. This article explores the evolving nature of youth political engagement through the concept of ‘engagement biographies’, drawing on biographical interviews with young activists in Spain. It examines the extent to which young activists experience political involvement as a dynamic, context-dependent process shaped by their individualised and de-standardised paths to adulthood, interpersonal relationships and broader socio-political contexts. Adopting a biographical perspective enhances understanding of key trends identified in the literature on youth and politics, including the volatility of participation, a preference for direct and less institutionalised activism, the integration of activism into everyday life, and increasing disengagement from formal politics. These insights contribute to a more nuanced understanding of young people’s patterns of political engagement and their broader implications for democracies.

Violence Against Women Political Activists

Juliana Restrepo Sanín, Young-Im Lee

Full text
In the last decade, research on violence against women in politics (VAWIP) has increased significantly. First identified by women activists in the Global South, VAWIP refers to behaviors that specifically target women as women by pressuring them to leave politics (Krook and Sanín 2016). Activists in Bolivia and later in other regions of the Global South first called attention to VAWIP in the late 1990s and early 2000s, when countries around the world began adopting gender quotas to increase the presence of women in government (Krook 2019). Most scholarship in this area has focused on violence targeting women candidates and elected officials (Collignon and Rüdig 2020; Dalton 2021; Håkansson 2021; Herrick et al. 2021; Raney and Collier 2024; Schneider and Carroll 2020). Attention to women in formal politics is warranted given their important representational role. Women’s presence in politics is a necessary first step to achieve political change and advance women’s interests (Beckwith 2011).

Party Politics

The German Local Manifesto Project (GLMP): A new data source for studying local party politics

Martin Gross, Christina-Marie Juen

Full text
Local politics remains comparatively understudied in political science, partly due to limited data on party competition at the municipal level. This research note introduces the German Local Manifesto Project (GLMP), a new dataset of German local election manifestos that expands the empirical infrastructure for studying programmatic competition in municipalities. The dataset contains 2307 manifestos from 306 political actors across 248 municipalities between 1989 and 2024, covering both national party branches and Independent Local Lists (ILL). Compared with existing collections of local manifestos, GLMP provides broader coverage of political actors and municipal contexts. The data allow scholars to analyze ideological positioning and policy priorities in local elections. An illustrative analysis demonstrates how the dataset can be used to compare ideological dispersion between ILL and national party branches. By providing large-scale manifesto data for German municipalities, GLMP opens new opportunities for research on local party competition and political representation.

The personal efficacy of party youth wing members

Henrik Bech Seeberg, Duncan McDonnell, Sofia Ammassari, Niklas Bolin, Reinhard Heinisch, Ann-Cathrine Jungar, Marco Valbruzzi, Carsten Wegscheider, Annika Werner

Full text
Although young people are said to be increasingly withdrawing from participation in political parties, some still join because they believe they can influence them to achieve change. We examine this sense of personal efficacy among contemporary youth wing members and ask: how is youth wing members’ personal efficacy associated with their activity levels and membership length, and with intra-party democracy and youth wing integration into the party? To do so, we combine the Political Party Database with data from the YOUMEM project, the largest cross-national survey of youth wing members conducted to date, covering ∼3600 members of 12 party youth wings in six democracies. We find that higher levels of personal efficacy are associated in particular with individuals being more active; being in a youth wing which is well integrated into the party; and in a context of strong intra-party democracy. Our results thus suggest how parties can foster young members’ personal efficacy and nurture democratically engaged citizens.

Contesting reputational advantages: Issue ownership and negative campaigning

Martin Haselmayer, Marcelo Jenny

Full text
Research on issue-based negative campaigning typically emphasizes issue salience and parties’ own issue strengths. This paper shifts the focus to the targets of attacks. We argue that negative campaigning on policy issues is systematically structured by the competence of the opponent, as parties seek to challenge rivals’ reputational advantages within specific issue domains. Using a dyadic dataset of party press releases from five Austrian national election campaigns (2006–2019), combined with survey-based measures of perceived issue competence, we show that parties are significantly more likely to direct negative messages at highly competent opponents. In contrast, sender-party competence and voter issue salience do not consistently shape targeting patterns. These findings suggest that issue-based negative campaigning is driven less by parties’ own issue strengths than by their opponents’ relative standing, highlighting how attacks serve as a strategy of reputational contestation in issue competition.

Electoral Systems and Electoral Integrity: A Cross-National and Cross-Temporal Analysis With Policy Implications

Grigorii V. Golosov

This study examines the relationship between electoral systems and electoral integrity across a panel of 1,641 elections held in 141 countries over the period 1832–2024. Employing a correlated random-effects framework, which simultaneously recovers within-country estimates equivalent to fixed effects and between-country estimates capturing stable structural differences, the analysis reveals that proportional representation is associated with short-run reductions in electoral integrity within countries over time, reflecting administrative complexity, diffuse accountability costs, and the institutional turbulence that typically accompanies electoral system transitions. Yet countries with persistently proportional electoral systems exhibit substantially higher electoral integrity in cross-national comparison, reflecting the long-run co-evolution between proportional representation institutions and integrity-sustaining democratic infrastructure. These divergent effects cannot be recovered by cross-sectional or pooled analyses, which conflate two opposing mechanisms. The study shows that while proportional representation ultimately benefits electoral integrity when its full range of benefits is materialized in the process of co-evolution with democracy, the short-term transitional effects should not be overlooked.

Backlashes to Gender Equality and Authoritarian Populism in the U.S.

Stan Oklobdzija, Christopher Witko

Full text
According to recent scholarship, backlash toward gender equality is fueling support for the authoritarian and populist right in many countries and, perhaps, the rise of Trumpism in the U.S. Yet, there are potentially different types of backlash to gender equality. We argue that the rising status of women over the past several decades may result in anti-feminist attitudes, not just among older men who yearn for the traditional gender arrangements of their youth as some scholars argue, but also among younger, single men lacking college degrees, who are less attractive partners for women. We call this “mate-value backlash.” Using ANES data, we find that anti-feminist attitudes have long been associated with support for Republican presidential candidates, but only in recent years have these attitudes become more prominent among unmarried men, without college degrees.

Where You Go, What You Do, Who You Are: Predictors of Political Science Job-Market Outcomes, 2023–2024

Scott W. Desposato

Full text
Several studies have found that only graduates of top-ranked PhD programs have a reasonable chance of landing a tenure-track professorship. However, these findings are based on an examination of the backgrounds of current faculty at PhD-granting institutions, which leaves findings vulnerable to several types of bias. I address these limitations by analyzing the placements of political science PhD candidates during the 2023–2024 academic job-market cycle. In my analysis, departmental rank had virtually no impact on whether candidates found tenure-track assistant professorships, but it did predict the types of positions that candidates found. Other factors had stronger associations with placement outcomes. In particular, publishing solo-authored and coauthored articles, having underrepresented minority or female identity, and studying American politics were all associated with significantly higher placement rates.

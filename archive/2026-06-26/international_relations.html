International Organization

Dirty Work and the Domestic Politics of Aid

Santi Foncillas, Erasmus Kersting, Christopher Kilby

Full text
Historically the United States uses both bilateral aid and influence over multilateral development finance to further its geopolitical objectives. Past studies explain the choice between these two instruments based on either a divided government effect or whether the recipient government is a traditional US ally (the dirty work hypothesis). We advance a theory explaining the bilateral/multilateral choice in terms of the confluence of these factors and test its predictions using United Nations Security Council voting, US bilateral aid flows, and World Bank lending. Results confirm theoretical expectations: higher bilateral aid goes to allies who support the United States in the Security Council but only when the US government is not divided and higher World Bank lending goes to non-allies who support the United States in the Security Council but only when the US government is divided. This detailed understanding of the link between domestic politics and governance in international organizations has important implications as the international order moves beyond a US-dominated system.

Democracy, Debt, and the Bench: Political Ideology in Sovereign Immunity Cases

Monica Widmann

Full text
Following pressure from investors, Congress passed the 1976 Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) to facilitate lawsuits by nonstate commercial actors against sovereign states in US courts. This legislation redefined sovereign debt as a commercial activity, seemingly favoring creditors. Yet case outcomes proved variable. While the FSIA established clear procedural guidelines, judicial discretion remained, raising questions about what factors influence outcomes. How deep is the effect of judges’ partisan leanings? To what extent does a left–right divide exist in this issue area? Although sovereign debt of other countries rarely sparks US political debate, I argue that partisan influence persists in these cases, particularly when defendants are democracies. Republican-nominated judges consistently apply the FSIA’s pro-creditor framework regardless of defendant characteristics, reflecting their party’s historical support for transnational commercial actors and contract enforcement. Democratic-nominated judges, however, exhibit conditional deference: when democracies demonstrate fiscal restraint before defaulting—signaling genuine economic distress—Democratic-nominated judges more readily defer to the sovereign defendant within the FSIA’s constraints. When democracies maintain high government consumption while defaulting, suggesting strategic choice rather than necessity, Democratic-nominated judges enforce more strictly. By contrast, both Democratic- and Republican-nominated judges converge on pro-creditor positions when adjudicating autocratic defaults because the absence of credible fiscal signals eliminates conditions for conditional deference. I test this argument with an original data set of sovereign debt litigation cases brought to the United States from 1977 to 2022.

Aid, lending, and TRIPS

Jihye Park

Full text
The rise of superstar firms has accompanied the expansion of an international intellectual property (IP) regime that extends beyond the standards established in the WTO Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS). Despite sustained resistance from developing countries, the United States has successfully promoted TRIPS-plus provisions through preferential trade agreements, with other advanced economies following. This study attributes US success in diffusing TRIPS-plus standards to the strategic deployment of development finance, conditioned on the domestic political feasibility of aid and the regime type of the recipient country. Using National Trade Estimate (NTE) reports from 1995 to 2022 published by the US Trade Representative, I construct a semantic proxy for US assessments of IP protection in partner countries harnessing a state-of-the-art stance detection large language model. Countries facing greater IP-related criticism incorporate more TRIPS-plus commitments into their preferential trade agreements with the United States in exchange for development finance. Compensation varies by regime type. Democracies receive increased foreign aid, while autocracies benefit more from International Finance Corporation lending to the private sector. Placebo tests reveal that among TRIPS-plus commitments, test data exclusivity, US pharmaceutical elites’ top priority that blocks generic drugs past patent expiration, uniquely mobilizes both instruments.

Journal of Peace Research

Does defiance work? How defying “naming and shaming” shapes foreign public opinion

Claudia Junghyun Kim, Dongshu Liu

Full text
When “naming and shaming” fails, it is often because target states respond with defiance rather than compliance. Yet existing research remains limited in two respects: it offers few clear predictions about how foreign publics respond to such defiance, and it pays relatively limited attention to a central purpose of transnational advocacy—empowering aggrieved local actors. This study addresses both gaps by examining how international norm compliance constituencies respond after target states reject external criticism of their human rights records. Drawing on survey experiments on American public responses to defiance by Japan and China, we show that the effects of defiance are relational. Defiance increased support for Japan, a U.S. ally but did not increase support for China, a U.S. rival. At the same time, defiance also weakened U.S. public support for local rights claimants in the human rights controversies under investigation. This pattern matters because continued foreign pressure is often treated as essential to successful norm enforcement but is rarely examined after the target state openly pushes back. The findings suggest that defiance can weaken the transnational pressure on which local claimants often depend, even when it fails to improve perceptions of the target state itself. The study therefore highlights how defiance reshapes foreign public opinion in relational ways that can blunt continued compliance pressure and undermine support for local rights claimants.

Media reports of coup d’etat and democratic attitude in neighboring countries

Yu Osaki, Masahiro Shoji

Full text
This study examines the impact of a military coup on citizens’ democratic attitudes in neighboring countries. Previous studies have examined the domestic consequences of coups and the possibility of coup contagion across countries, yet little is known about how citizens in neighboring countries respond to coups, despite the potential implications of public opinion for incentives surrounding future coup attempts. Using a nationally representative survey from multiple countries and the unexpected event during survey design, we demonstrate that individuals exposed to news of the coup express greater support for democracy and elections and greater rejection of authoritarian rule, suggesting that citizens view the coup as a cause of political instability rather than a means of liberalization. The effect size is comparable across countries and respondent characteristics, while TV and radio owners react more quickly. These results cannot be explained by media bias, institutional trust, citizens’ learning about the poor performance of military regimes, or concern about their future economic instability. Given the high costs of coup attempts under strong popular opposition, stronger public support for democracy may reduce incentives for coups in neighboring countries. These findings also provide one possible micro-level explanation for the limited empirical support for the coup contagion hypothesis.

She who pays for stereotypes: female leaders and military spending

Nila Zarepour-Arizi

Full text
The elections of Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah in Namibia and Claudia Sheinbaum in Mexico as the first female presidents mark a milestone in the historic progress of women to the executive. This growing number of female leaders has attracted considerable attention from scholars and the public. While some research suggests that women adopt more masculine and assertive policies, others find no significant differences in policy preferences of male and female leaders. Building on “social role theory” and the “lack of fit” model, I suggest that female leaders are more inclined to pursue assertive policies, such as increasing military spending, as a strategy to assert their competence as national leaders and navigate gendered biases. Given the small number of female leaders and their nonrandom election, I incorporate entropy weighting into two-way fixed-effects models and a staggered difference-in-differences to examine the relationship between female leaders and military spending from 1966 to 2021. The results indicate that countries with women in leadership tend to increase their military spending slightly more than those led by men. However, such behavior is mitigated as the share of women in the legislature increases. Additionally, this increase in military spending corresponds with a relative reduction in health and education expenditures. These findings contribute to broader discussions on how stereotypes and sexism influence spending decisions made by female leaders.

Rebel group mergers: security imperatives and the autonomy dilemma

Sedef Aslı Topal

Full text
Existing research suggests that rebel group mergers typically force at least one side to abandon its group identity and come under the control of another organization. This loss of autonomy binds a group's fate to others, undermining its capacity for independent action. Still, some groups merge, either by joining another armed organization or establishing a new one together, even though other forms of alliance, such as joint operations or united fronts, may address their minimum cooperation needs without risking group autonomy. This behavior raises a puzzling question: what leads these groups to merge despite the associated risks? In this study, I examine the conditions under which rebel groups merge rather than rely on alternative alliances. I argue that mergers are more likely when groups face two heightened external threats that require an immediate response: increasing state repression and inter-rebel fighting. Using cross-national dyadic data and a series of multilevel logistic regression models, I find strong support for my main hypotheses. Simultaneous escalations involving state forces, rival rebel organizations, or both make rebel groups more inclined to merge, trading autonomy for greater security.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Shooting From the Hip: The Counterintuitively Intuitive Nature of Deterrence

Brian C. Rathbun, Caleb Pomeroy, Joshua D. Kertzer

Full text
Much if not most of human judgment is intuitive rather than reflective, but what types of behaviors result from non-deliberative as opposed to deliberative choice? We argue that for evolutionary reasons, intuition favors retaliatory aggression and a reliance on the deterrence model, the set of hunches that stress the importance of standing firm and demonstrating resolve to avoid challenges. By contrast, careful and analytical thought tends towards diplomacy and cooperation. By thinking from the gut and shooting from the hip, humans solved deterrence challenges in our evolutionary past. Across four studies—a time pressure experiment, an observational survey of thinking styles, a large-scale text analysis of Cold War US foreign policy elites, and a case study of US President Ronald Reagan—we find support for these expectations. These results are surprising, given that deterrence is often associated with rational deliberation. Instead, the intuitive nature of retaliatory aggression and the embrace of the deterrence model seem to be ecologically rational.

Green-Lighting Accountability: Transitional Justice and Compliance With Rulings From the Inter-American Court of Human Rights

Pearce Edwards, Kelly Morrison, Chesani Askew

Full text
When do democratically-elected leaders comply with rulings from international courts? While international courts have been studied independently, little research examines their interaction with state-level transitional justice (TJ) institutions. We evaluate the incentives of post-authoritarian leaders to comply with rulings of international courts related to violence under military dictatorships. We expect leaders to be more likely to comply with international court rulings addressing authoritarian human rights violations when maximalist TJ (domestic trials and vettings) has been implemented. In contrast, we expect leaders will be no more likely to comply when minimalist TJ (amnesties) has been adopted. Using data on remedies issued by the Inter-American Court of Human Rights from 1989 to 2020 for abuses committed under military dictatorships, we find that compliance is more likely following domestic trials and vettings but amnesties have no significant effect on the likelihood of compliance, as predicted. Overall, our results suggest that international courts serve as complements for domestic TJ.

Political Studies

The rise and fall of class voting in Britain, 1885–2024

Oliver Heath, Humphrey Southall, Paula Aucott, Christopher Prosser

Full text
Understanding when and why social cleavages shape electoral behaviour is a central concern in the study of democratic politics. While many accounts emphasise the long-term social changes, others point to the role of party strategy. We contribute to this debate by analysing the political evolution of one of the central cleavages of the twentieth century—class—in one of the world’s longest-standing democracies—Britain. Analysing a unique new dataset combining constituency-level results across 37 general elections from 1885 to 2024 with data from eight censuses, applying geospatial redistricting methods, we describe the rise, stability and eventual decline of class-based voting, showing that a strong class cleavage emerged in the early twentieth century, remained stable for decades, but then unravelled rapidly after the 1990s. These patterns are difficult to reconcile with theories that emphasise gradual social change and are more consistent with accounts that stress political agency and representation.

International Theory

The disciplinary power of gratitude: gift-giving and moral indebtedness in Cuba (1962–1963) and Ukraine (2022–2025)

Rainer Ricardo

Full text
This article argues that in asymmetric international relationships, gratitude functions as a technology of disciplinary power through which material assistance is converted into moral indebtedness. Unlike material conditionality, which constrains what subordinate actors can do, gratitude constrains what they can say, claim, and be within the relationship, delegitimizing resistance by recoding it as emotional deviance. Integrating Maussian gift theory, emotional-regime analysis, and speech-act approaches to emotion, the article theorizes a specific causal sequence: material assistance is framed as a morally valorized gift, which generates a debt of gratitude, which prescribes an emotional performance of acknowledgment and deference, which in turn constrains the subordinate actor’s capacity to contest donor authority. Empirically, the argument is developed through a comparative emotional-discursive analysis of two structurally asymmetric alliances: the Soviet-Cuban relationship during the 1962 Missile Crisis and the U.S.-Ukraine relationship (2022–2025). Across both cases, great powers mobilized gratitude as a reactive disciplinary instrument, deployed to reassert authority at moments when influence risked erosion. The article contributes to the emotional turn in IR theory by demonstrating that hierarchy is mostly sustained through emotional regimes that prescribe what subordinate actors must feel, display, and acknowledge.

International Studies Quarterly

Footloose Capital in the Land of America First: Populism, Nationalism and Mass Preferences Over Corporate Tax

Cameron Ballard-Rosa, Tyler Ditmore

Full text
Abstracts While outsourcing of production and tax avoidance allow multinational corporations to seek low-cost locations for doing business in a globalized economy, we argue that these strategies may also violate important norms of fair behavior for corporations to contribute to their host countries, leading to heightened preferences for increased corporate taxes, particularly among more populist or more nationalist citizens. Drawing on original survey experiments administered on a nationally representative sample of U.S. respondents, we find that tax avoidance by MNCs corresponds to significantly increased preferences for corporate income taxes, especially among populists. Surprisingly, however, while outsourcing by firms also generally leads to higher preferences for corporate taxes, we do not find that this effect is more pronounced among nationalist respondents. These results speak to the importance of separating out populist from nationalist political movements as drivers of international economic policy in an era of new bases of support for rightwing parties.

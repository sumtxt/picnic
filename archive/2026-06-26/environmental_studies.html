Annual Review of Resource Economics

Modeling Uncertainty in Integrated Assessment Models

Yongyang Cai

Full text
Integrated assessment models (IAMs) are pivotal tools that synthesize knowledge from climate science, economics, and policy to evaluate the interactions between human activities and the climate system. They serve as essential instruments for policymakers, providing insights into the potential outcomes of various climate policies and strategies. Given the complexity and inherent uncertainties in both the climate system and socioeconomic processes, understanding and effectively managing uncertainty within IAMs are crucial for robust climate policy development. This review provides a comprehensive overview of how IAMs handle uncertainty, highlighting recent methodological advancements and their implications for climate policy. We examine the types of uncertainties present in IAMs; discuss various modeling approaches to address these uncertainties; and explore recent developments in the field, including the incorporation of advanced computational methods.

Global Environmental Politics

Fossil Fuels and Finance: How Power and Agency Influenced the Norwegian Divestment Decisions

Devon Reynolds

Full text
The Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG), one of the world’s largest investment funds, made two fossil fuel divestments—first removing coal companies from its portfolio and later removing some oil and gas companies. Although the two divestments occurred in similar contexts, they had significant differences in both process and outcomes. The evidence presented shows how overlapping fossil fuel and neoliberal hegemonic structures affected the choices and impacts of strategic actors working both for and against fossil fuel divestment. This study contributes to international political economy by presenting a framework for examining interactions between coexisting hegemonies that overlap without being equivalent. Using this framework, the study identifies how strategic actors may negotiate conflicts between power structures to achieve their goals and points to trade-offs resulting from leveraging one power structure against another.

Banning Bitcoin: The Material and Normative Dimensions of Energy Security in the Regulation of Cryptocurrency Mining in the United States and Canada

Heather Millar, Antulio Rosales, Ty Tarnowski, Holly Burke

Full text
New digital industries like artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and cryptocurrency mining are voracious energy consumers, with complex implications for states’ climate action, economic development, and energy security. We explore the politics of these emergent industries by examining subnational regulatory responses to the recent “crypto rush” in North America. We explore the interplay of material and interpretive dimensions of energy security and climate action in the US states of Texas and New York and the Canadian provinces of Alberta and Québec, explaining why some regulators ban cryptocurrency mining while others do not. Although some fossil fuel–abundant energy jurisdictions position mining as just another industrial consumer, jurisdictions with credible climate commitments have restrained the industry, situating mining as a threat to green industrial expansion. Cryptocurrency miners have responded by promoting digital production as a path to future energy security, a strategy we anticipate will be replicated by other digital industries.

Flexible Networks Asserting Agency: Youth Participation in UNFCCC Processes

Yi Hyun Kang, Amandine Orsini

Full text
Youth are increasingly regarded as key actors in climate politics. At the global level, youth participation is visible at the conferences of the parties (COPs) to the UNFCCC. While the number of youth participants has gradually increased, the structural dimensions of youth participation, especially their networks in international policymaking, remain underexplored. This article addresses this knowledge gap by analyzing youth involvement at the UNFCCC COPs from COP1 to COP27 using social network analysis. By examining coaffiliation and side-event co-organization networks, we argue that youth actors persistently contribute to climate political spaces as active network cocreators. Despite structural inequalities, youth-to-youth collaboration, from both Global South and Global North, bridges informal and formal modes of engagement in climate politics, asserting youth agency. Moreover, the varied communities of youth networks show that youth are heterogeneous actors pursuing diverse mobilization strategies, not necessarily based on ideational affinity.

The Carbon Pricing Politics of Methane: The Minor Miracle of the US Methane Fee

Nikolai Drahos

Full text
Carbon pricing attempts have been repeatedly thwarted at the federal level in the United States. Yet, in 2022, the United States adopted a federal price on oil and gas methane emissions as part of the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA), imposing a cost on an industry with significant political clout using a politically unpopular policy instrument. What made the first US federal carbon pricing scheme politically feasible? Using interviews with key insiders in the IRA negotiations, I provide the first study of the political history behind the methane fee. Synthesizing and expanding on the extant literature, I suggest that four enabling conditions rendered carbon pricing politically feasible: industry division, effective ideational arguments, a conducive legislative pathway, and quiet politics. While carbon pricing is sometimes written off as a political nonstarter, theorizing political feasibility as the product of multiple, conditional factors recognizes that opportunities for pricing reforms—though circumscribed—are also contingent.

The Global Politics of Sea Level Rise Adaptation

Florian Steig

Full text
Sea level rise (SLR) is increasingly reframed as a global political problem, no longer confined to local adaptation needs or the struggles of atoll nations. Recent UN debates and the creation of the Ocean Rise & Coastal Resilience Coalition (ORCRC) mark the institutionalization of SLR debates within global climate governance. This article asks how SLR is rendered governable as an object of global politics, and how that problematization reshapes the solution space for local adaptation. Adopting a poststructuralist governmentality framework and focusing on the ORCRC, I trace the visibilities, rationalities, technologies of governing, and subject positions inherent to the emerging global governing of SLR. Through a globalized vision of (coastal) resilience, this governmentality privileges the rollout of transnational knowledge infrastructures, embeds adaptation in cost–benefit logics, and opens coastal cities to investment. I argue that the dominant problematization of SLR as a biophysical challenge for humankind risks obscuring the socioeconomic and colonial drivers of climate vulnerability, which could undermine the transformative potential of the SLR agenda.

Burden Sharing in Carbon Trading: Partner Country Selection and Public Support for Storing CO2 Abroad

Susanne Rhein, Thomas Bernauer

Full text
To limit climate change, emission reductions must be complemented by technologies that remove carbon dioxide (CO2) from the atmosphere and store it permanently underground. As some countries lack sufficient geological storage capacity, cross-border CO2 transport and storage are gaining traction. Yet such cooperation raises political concerns about burden shifting and public acceptance. This study examines under what conditions citizens support exporting removed CO2 for storage abroad. Drawing on two survey-embedded experiments in Switzerland and the United Kingdom (N = 1,500 each), we show that support is higher when partner countries are democratic, pursue ambitious climate policies, locate storage sites away from residential areas, and enjoy domestic support for CO2 storage. By contrast, storage costs play a comparatively smaller role in shaping public support. These results highlight the importance of engaging in international climate cooperation in ways that are cost-effective but also socially and politically responsible.

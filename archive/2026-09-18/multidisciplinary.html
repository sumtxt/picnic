Predictors and prevalence of support for white nationalism in the United States

Patrick J. Egan, James Bisbee, Joshua A. Tucker

Full text
White nationalism has become increasingly visible in American public life 1–10 . Yet we lack knowledge of the predictors and prevalence of explicit support for this ideology, as survey research has treated it as a latent construct captured through indirect indicators 11–15 . We introduce and validate a measure that informs respondents of the core tenets of the white nationalist movement and then asks whether they support the movement. Expressed support is distinct from organizational affiliation with the movement, which we do not measure. Here we show with three national surveys of non-Hispanic white adults (conducted from 2021 to 2025) that support for white nationalism is (1) higher among white people encountering personal hardship and local social distress, consistent with strain theories 3,16 ; (2) unrelated to local racial composition or recent demographic change, complicating status threat accounts 4,17,18 ; and (3) elevated among white people with close friendships primarily online, consistent with theories of extremism driven by online mobilization 7,10,19 . Support for white nationalism is more common among white people with lower incomes and education, Republicans and conservatives, and those who are politically disaffected. In our national probability sample, prevalence of support was estimated at 4.9% of white adults, including 13.5% of white men aged 18–29. Support for white nationalism seems to constitute a discrete dimension of white racial attitudes, and many white people who endorse its core ideas are unaware of the label.

Defining collectivism as duties in relationships rather than generalized warmth improves predictive validity in 100 cultures

Thomas Talhelm, Liuqing Wei, Rui Sun, Danila Medvedev, Álvaro San Martin, May Helmy, Adil Samekin, Alejandra Zaragoza Scherman, Alexander S. English, Andreea Ursu, ... (+97 authors)

Full text
Experts widely agree that East Asian cultures are more collectivistic than Western cultures. Yet 76 survey studies found no reliable difference. Our explanation is that the most-used surveys are contaminated with ‘warm fuzzy’ items, which describe warmth and prosociality towards everyone near and far. We argue that collectivism is better understood as ‘responsibilism’, which emphasizes responsibilities in close relationships. Chronic loneliness and isolation phenotypes and physical and mental health in older adults

Yuteng Ma, Jessica K. Bone, Rosie Mayston, Qian Gao

Full text
Chronic social deficits (persistent feelings of being lonely or isolated) have been linked to premature mortality, yet the underlying physical and mental pathways remain unclear. This study used data from 4,090 older adults in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, a longitudinal panel study. Using outcome-wide analyses, we assessed how the chronicity of social deficits was associated with 17 health outcomes across physical, mental, cognitive and behavioural health domains. Regression models showed that chronic loneliness was longitudinally associated with increased risks of worse general health, functional capacity, and mental wellbeing, and more unhealthy behaviours. Chronic isolation and onset of isolation were associated with lower quality of life, whereas isolation remission was positively related to enhanced mental wellbeing and cognition compared to no isolation. Findings suggest an association between chronicity of loneliness and adverse health outcomes, highlighting chronic social deficits as an important correlate of healthy ageing.

Cognitive appeal promotes the persistence of inefficient solutions and hinders cumulative cultural evolution

Ali Seyhun Saral, Manvir Singh, Antoine Jacquet, Haneul Jang, Maxime Derex

Full text
Through cumulative cultural evolution, human populations discover novel solutions that allow them to solve complex problems. However, populations also reliably develop and sustain practices that are costly yet appear to have no effect on their stated outcome, such as bloodletting, divination, and malicious sorcery. Here, we show that ineffective, costly solutions can develop and be maintained because of their cognitive appeal. In a pre-registered transmission-chain experiment, participants were tasked with developing a solution to a problem over several trials, after which they passed instructions onto the subsequent generation. The visual appearance of the experimental task was manipulated to vary the extent to which cognitive biases could interfere with participants’ expectations of efficacy, enabling a direct test of the effect of cognitive appeal on cumulative cultural evolution. Participants in the low-appeal treatment ( n = 240) progressively develop more efficient solutions across generations. Participants in the high-appeal treatment ( n = 240), in contrast, produce costlier solutions, and we did not observe a statistically significant increase in efficiency over the course of the experiment. These results suggest that, even under conditions favorable to cumulative cultural evolution, a form of cognitive appeal can alone prevent the process and promote the emergence and cultural persistence of ineffective solutions.

Wildfire exposure and political participation in Africa

Siyuan Chen, Michael Akwasi Appiah-Kubi, Lina Meng, Pengfei Liu, Lu Lin

Full text
Civic engagement can shape collective responses to climate change. However, evidence on how climate-related disasters affect political participation in Africa remains limited. Combining Global Fire Atlas records with Afrobarometer surveys of more than 100,000 people in 34 African countries, we show that people living close to wildfires are more likely to vote, contact local councillors, attend community meetings, and raise collective issues, whereas protest participation shows weaker responses. These effects are larger in arid regions and among people with higher climate literacy or access to information, members of politically dominant ethnic groups and those living near ethnic boundaries. Our findings suggest that participation is motivated mainly by immediate material loss and demand for public services, rather than environmental concern. These findings highlight the importance of addressing post-disaster needs to sustain political engagement and public support for long-term climate adaptation in the Global South.

Beyond redlining: Gentrification, displacement, disadvantages, and exclusivity predict urban environmental and health inequities

Idowu Ajibade, Kevan B. Moffett, Jason Maxfield, Kate Gregory, Axcelle Bell, Jackson Voelkel, Todd Rosenstiel, Aaron R. Ramirez

Full text
This study investigated environmental justice by developing and comparing four contemporary urban segregation typologies (persistent exclusivity, persistent disadvantage, recent gentrification, and recent displacement-hosting) with historical Homeowners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC) zones and 104 variables spanning the natural and built environments, pollution, environmental hazards, health, and disability. We applied principal component analysis to the 2010 and 2020 American Community Survey and the US Census data to derive neighborhood typology indices for 342 tracts in Portland, Oregon. Using Spearman’s correlation and end-member comparisons, we assessed relationships among the typologies, HOLC zones, and environmental and health outcomes. HOLC zones predicted persistently exclusive areas in the urban core but not current injustices elsewhere. Persistently exclusive neighborhoods, both within and outside HOLC zones, had more tree canopy, less dense development, lower disaster risks, better preventive care, and fewer diseases than other areas, but more air pollution exposure. Recently gentrified areas had more transit and community resources but worse heat exposure, pollution, and impervious surface coverage and aligned strongly with HOLC C and D zones. Recent displacement-hosting neighborhoods were cleaner and greener but lack transit and community resources, while exhibiting weak health outcomes. Persistently disadvantaged areas, often outside HOLC zones, faced the most severe conditions, including minimal access to nature, aging infrastructure, the highest pollution and hazard exposure, and the worst health outcomes and disability rates. Our study offers insights into the spatial mechanisms of urban inequities and well-being, underscoring the need to look beyond redlining to fully understand and address today’s social and environmental injustices.

Full text
A key challenge to ensuring no one is left behind in the green transition lies in the persistent segregation of urban populations and the unequal distribution of environmental costs and benefits. Residential segregation has long reinforced socioeconomic and racialized disparities, and climate change introduces new dimensions to these injustices as adaptation policies increasingly shift financial and environmental burdens onto marginalized communities. While housing plays a central role in shaping exposure and resilience, the role of housing tenure—especially ownership arrangements beyond market logic—has received limited attention in just green transitions research. This study addresses this gap through a political ecology approach, combining spatial and policy analysis to examine the origins, distribution, and impacts of climate adaptation costs within Copenhagen’s not-for-profit housing sector. The paper offers a theoretical contribution by conceptualizing technomanagerialism as a critical yet overlooked mechanism of inequality within not-for-profit housing systems. It extends debates on climate burdens beyond market-based housing to demonstrate how inequalities become embedded even within ostensibly equitable systems, ultimately reinforcing patterns of urban segregation.

Multiple drivers and long-term patterns of the ecology of segregation in Latin America

Elizabeth M. Cook, Jason Sauer, Karin Weil González, Olga Barbosa

Full text
Segregation is a multidimensional phenomenon deeply entwined with historical, social, and ecological contexts. The ecology of segregation framework explores how social norms institutionalize racism, classism, and power dynamics to drive inequitable patterns of environmental benefits and hazards. The ecology of segregation has thus far been applied in the Global North, yet is relevant but not as well studied in the Global South and specifically Latin America. More work is needed to consider how the framework can be applied in other historic, sociocultural, and governance contexts. This paper applies the framework to Valdivia, Chile, illustrating how settler-colonialism, the 1960 9.5 magnitude earthquake, and neoliberal urban policies have shaped Valdivia’s patterns of segregation and ecological outcomes. Historical colonization entrenched systemic inequalities, while the earthquake altered wetland cover and formalized segregation. More recent land-use policies and urbanization trends further exacerbate disparities, with wealthier neighborhoods gaining greater access to green spaces and environmental amenities. We make the case that the long-term historic patterns and processes have important implications for understanding the current dynamics of—and future trajectories for—environmental justice. The findings call for inclusive urban planning, with community driven bottom–up and top–down approaches and acknowledgment of Indigenous perspectives, to mitigate entrenched inequalities and foster sustainable development.

The Corpus of English–Tagalog Code-switching: an integrative corpus of linguistic contact effects

Aaron Santa Maria, Renata Enghels

Full text
The Corpus of English–Tagalog Code-switching (CEnTaCS) is a new corpus designed to document naturalistic English–Tagalog bilingual speech. Locally referred to as Taglish, it is a widely-used yet understudied contact variety in the Philippines. Recorded in 2025 in Metro Manila, the corpus comprises three interrelated datasets: individual story retelling recordings, cognitive control data based on the Arrow Flanker task, and sociolinguistic questionnaire data. CEnTaCS offers fine-grained data on code-switching across clausal, lexical, and morphemic levels, with particular attention to intra-word code-switching, a salient but still insufficiently documented feature of Taglish. Beyond code-switching, the corpus captures a broad range of language contact phenomena, including borrowing, calquing, convergence, and interference. By making these data systematically available, the corpus supports an integrated account of mixed-language practices that brings together structural, cognitive, and sociolinguistic perspectives. CEnTaCS thus enables researchers to investigate how contact-induced structures are constrained linguistically, how they relate to bilingual processing, and how they index social meaning and identity in a community where bilingualism is deeply embedded in everyday life.

The spectre of de-assetization: legal infrastructures and fragile fixes in fossil decommissioning

Lukas Bogner

Full text
Efforts to decommission coal power plants in the Global South are constrained by the legal infrastructures that create and protect them as assets. This article examines the Asian Development Bank’s Energy Transition Mechanism (ETM) as a case of managing these constraints via a series of increasingly complex and fragile spatio-temporal fixes: First, power purchase agreements and investment treaties require compensation of investors at business-as-usual asset valuations; second, for lack of public funding, this compensation is to be generated via a blended finance mechanism; third, its inability to stem these costs necessitates further legal-financial engineering, including a novel guarantee mechanism and ‘coal-to-clean’ credits. The ETM and its successive fixes are haunted by the power relations inscribed in asset creation and protection in the Global South. This renders ‘de-risked’ transition finance dubious as a blueprint for fossil decommissioning and highlights the troubled nature of climate-related asset stranding in transnational, hierarchical legal space.

Modeling Civic Engagement in the Age of AI: A Communication Mediation Approach to AI News, Multifaceted Trust, and Participation in a Two-Wave Panel Study

Seungahn Nah, Fanjue Liu, Jun Luo, Lee LaPlaca, Chris DeFelice

Full text
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes both a topic of news coverage and a force shaping the information environment, citizens must make sense of AI-related issues while evaluating machine-generated content, journalistic coverage, and institutional actors. This study applies the Orientation-Stimulus-Reasoning-Orientation-Response framework to examine how AI-related news consumption is associated with interpersonal discussion, multifaceted trust, and AI-related offline civic participation. Using two-wave panel data from U.S. adults (Wave 1 = 2,010; Wave 2 = 942), the study models AI-related news consumption across television, newspapers, online news sites, and social media as the stimulus; AI-related online and offline discussion as reasoning; trust in AI-generated news, legacy media coverage of AI, and AI-related institutional actors as reorientation; and AI-related offline civic participation as the response. Results reveal a selective communication pathway. Newspaper and social media AI news use were the most consistent predictors of AI-related discussion. Online discussion was associated with later trust in AI-generated news and legacy media coverage of AI, and trust in AI-generated news and AI-related institutional actors was associated with later offline participation. The observed indirect pathways operated through online discussion and trust in AI-generated news. These findings refine communication mediation research by showing that public engagement with AI-related issues depends on where people encounter AI news, how they discuss it, and which sources and institutions they come to trust.

It Doesn’t Take Extremes to Trigger Hate: A Mixed-Methods Study on Meme Extremity and User Comments on 9GAG

Antonia Drexel, Ursula Kristin Schmid

Full text
Users engage with socially and politically relevant topics by sharing memes and adopting the creative styles of meme-sharing platforms like 9GAG. Although memes can contain political critique, research also linked them to hate speech and increasingly extreme content. Little attention has been paid to entertainment-first platforms, where (perceived) homogeneity, anonymity, and humor-driven social norms may shape how political and hateful content is produced and discussed in the comment section. This study addresses this gap using a sequential mixed-methods design. First, we analyzed 343 memes on 9GAG with quantitative content analysis, categorizing them into classes based on varying degrees of extreme content using latent class analysis. Second, via qualitative content analysis, we investigated the comment sections of six memes representing each identified class to determine how extreme content levels influence hateful comments. Results show that anonymity and ingroup identity foster a climate in which hate is normalized and often obscured through humor. However, even purely humorous memes provoked hateful comments, suggesting that factors beyond meme extremity, like topic sensitivity and referenced outgroups, also shape hateful follow-up communication.

How Definitions Shape Our Understanding of Political Influencers on Social Media

Jennifer Oser, John Wagner, Nir Grinberg

Full text
Political discourse now circulates through sprawling social media networks where multiple actors wield influence in addition to traditional elites. Yet scholarly conclusions about who actually shapes that discourse diverge because leading studies operationalize “political influencer” in fundamentally different ways. To clarify the stakes of those choices—and advance more coherent theorizing—we examine six definitions that are prevalent in the literature and are rooted in audience size, elite ties, network importance, and message engagement. We then apply these distinctive definitions to a single dataset, namely a panel of 597,815 registered U.S. voters on Twitter during the 2020 presidential campaign to identify convergence and divergence across definitions. Three patterns emerge. First, conceptual boundaries matter: definitions that differ in scale rarely converge on the same influencers, confirming that different lenses reveal largely distinct influencer groups. Second, definitional emphasis reshapes the portrait of influence itself: elite-based criteria surface highly political, centrally positioned senders with compact but engaged followings, whereas follower- and engagement-based criteria net vastly larger, more heterogeneous groups that address politics only episodically. Third, upstream choices about conceptual boundaries and definitional emphases cascade downstream to the characteristics of influencers’ audiences, producing systematic shifts in followers’ age, gender, racial, and partisan characteristics. By showing how conceptual decisions impact both who counts as a political influencer and which citizens follow their messages, this study lays the groundwork for clearer conceptualization of the relevant dimensions for advancing the accumulation of knowledge on this increasingly important political phenomenon.

Visibility, Sovereignty, and the Affective Politics of Media: Decolonial Struggles Over Representation and Reception During the 2022 Indigenous Protests in Ecuador

Inti Josue Cachipuendo-Contero

Full text
This article examines how visibility, affect, and epistemic power intersected during the June 2022 Indigenous-led protests in Ecuador, when media became a central terrain of struggle over meaning and legitimacy. Combining multimodal media analysis with ethnographically informed audience research, the study compares three communicative regimes: government, commercial, and Indigenous/community media, to show how each enacted distinct infrastructures of visibility and political intelligibility. Government media constructed visibility as surveillance, framing dissent as a crisis of order through securitized language, ritualized imagery, and affective scripts that moved audiences from fear to reassurance. Commercial broadcasters pursued legitimacy through moderation, translating protest into economic disruption and containing conflict through the aesthetics of balance and procedural neutrality. In contrast, Indigenous/community media operating through platformed livestreams enacted visibility as relational sovereignty, embedding communication within collective action, historical continuity, and participatory verification. Drawing on decolonial media theory and scholarship on affective publics and Indigenous digital activism, the article conceptualizes media as ontopolitical infrastructures that organize not only information but the conditions under which political subjects and truths become recognizable. Reception data from Kichwa-speaking communities demonstrate that Indigenous audiences engaged in collective practices of interpretation, verification, and refusal. Affective responses such as anger and pride functioned as epistemic resources, transforming exposure into critique and reception into communal knowledge-making. The article advances a decolonial reframing of visibility as infrastructure and theorizes reception as epistemic labor sustaining narrative sovereignty.

“ In a way, I want TikTok to tell me what I want. . . ” Users’ experience of TikTok as a televisual algorithmic flow

Carlos Entrena-Serrano, Trisha Meyer, Emilia Gómez, Jo Pierson

Full text
This article revisits Williams’s concept of flow, Firth’s notion of algorithmic flow, and Picone et al.’s concept of Small Acts of Engagement (SAOE) to examine how users experience TikTok’s algorithmic flow. SAOE are understood as low-effort, everyday acts through which users negotiate their identities and shape their digital consumption. While SAOE have been a valuable lens for understanding user agency on earlier social media platforms, we argue that TikTok’s interface design and cultural framing introduce new challenges. The platform’s design, together with users’ perception of TikTok’s algorithmic flow as a televisual experience, reduces their SAOE to a minimal expression: a rhythm of watch, scroll, repeat , discouraging other forms of intentional, identity-driven engagement. Through a mixed-methods study combining interviews and analysis of participants’ donated TikTok data, we show how our participants experience TikTok as a place for effortless entertainment and continuous consumption, closely resembling the experience of traditional television flow.

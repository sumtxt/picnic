Does cross-border engagement shape emotional identification? Exploring the link between dimensions of transnational engagement and the feeling of belonging among intra-European migrants

Ilona Pap, Jörg Rössel

Full text
Public debates increasingly link immigrants’ national belonging to their cross-border engagement. Using survey data on six intra-European origin groups in Switzerland (first and second generation), we examine how four dimensions of transnational practices and relations —return intentions, social, political, and economic ties—relate to identification with the country of residence (CoR) and the country of origin (CoO). Unlike previous research, that has often led to inconclusive findings, we study different dimensions of transnational engagement and their heterogenous logics separately in their relation to both the identification with the CoO and the CoR based on representative data. Our findings demonstrate that the diverse dimensions of transnational activities and relations differ in their association with identification. This underscores the need to analytically separate these dimensions. In addition, several of these dimensions covary with identification with the CoO, but only one clearly covaries with identification with the CoR. This indicates that transnational engagement with the CoO and integration into the CoR are to a considerable extent, different worlds for intra-European migrants.

Population, Space and Place

Social Patterning and Spatial Variation in Intergenerational Geographic Proximity in the United Kingdom

Sen Li, Hill Kulu, Urška Demšar

Full text
Existing understanding of intergenerational geographic proximity remains limited owing to the complexity inherent in defining geographic proximity and insufficient attention to fine‐scale spatial variation. This study focuses on children's proximity to parents, parents' proximity to adult children, and three‐generational co‐residence among individuals with both living parents and children, thereby capturing multiple kin relations and relational directionality. It further extends previous literature by examining finer‐grained spatial variation in these measures alongside individual‐ and household‐level differences. Using data from the UK Household Longitudinal Study, the results show that younger adults are more likely to co‐reside with their parents, children, or both, as expected. Higher educational attainment is associated with greater residential distance from parents and children. South Asians tend to maintain closer intergenerational ties through co‐residence or nearby residence. Spatial variation is pronounced: individuals in Northern Ireland are more likely to reside with or live near parents or adult children, whereas those in the South, such as London and the South East, are less likely to have their parents or adult children living nearby. Our findings reinforce the life course principle of linked lives, underscoring how living arrangements are interconnected across generations, and demonstrate the importance of adopting dual perspectives when examining geographic proximity within kinship dyads.

Partnership and Fertility Trajectories of Immigrant Women Across the Migration Process to Spain: A Comparison by Country of Origin

Mengyao Wu, Alberto del Rey Poveda, Rafael Grande

Full text
Although many studies examine family patterns among immigrants and how these differ from those in the native population, a migration‐focused perspective investigating how partnerships and childbearing trajectories intersect throughout the migration process remains scarce. Applying multichannel sequence analysis to retrospective data from the 2018 Spanish Fertility Survey, this study simultaneously analysed changes in women's partnerships and fertility behaviour around the time of migration and explored how these patterns differed by migrant origin. The pattern of family‐related migration revealed a substantial increase in cohabitation and first births shortly after migration. Cluster analysis identified five typical family trajectories among immigrant women. Multinomial logistic regression models were then used to examine how these trajectories were associated with immigrant origins. Contrary to expectations, the results did not show significant differences in these family trajectories by country of origin, except for the Early Marriage and Fertility Transitions and Single Motherhood pathways . These findings provide comprehensive insights into the interdependencies among family life domains during the migration process.

Faith, Fear, and Belonging: Return Perceptions Among Displaced Syrian Alawites and Christians

Metin Gani Tapan, Necati Buğra Kuddaş, Aykut Can Demirel

Full text
This study examines how displaced Syrian Alawite and Christian refugees understand return following the fall of the Assad government. Using Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis, it draws on in‐depth interviews with 21 refugees in Türkiye, 13 Alawites, and eight Christians. Four interconnected themes were identified: deep fears surrounding political transition and identity‐based insecurity; uncertain futures shaped by political volatility, economic survival, and fragile peace; eroded institutional belonging through exclusion from governance and public employment; and infrastructure deficits that undermine the possibility of rebuilding one's life. Alawite participants more often emphasized collective blame, retaliation, and persistent profiling because of their presumed association with the former government's actions. Christian participants placed greater emphasis on inadequate protection, distrust, and uncertainty regarding whether their political neutrality would be recognized. Concerns regarding political volatility, economic survival, renewed conflict, and public services largely converged between the two groups. The study shows that return conditions are interpreted through collective memory, religious positioning, institutional recognition, and spatial belonging rather than operating as uniform push or pull factors. Therefore, return ideation depends on whether Syria can be imagined as a safe, recognized, and sustainable place to rebuild everyday life.

International Migration

Coerced Voluntariness: Parliamentary Narratives on Return Migration in Germany and Turkey

Seda Rass‐Turgut

Full text
International frameworks treat return migration as either voluntary or forced. This article challenges that binary through a computational corpus analysis of two entangled parliaments: 4597 German Bundestag protocols (1949 to 2025, migration analysis from 1970) and 966 Turkish Grand National Assembly protocols (since 2011). In the Bundestag, voluntariness language dominates while coercion oscillates in two waves, peaking in 1994 and 2024. In governing‐party speech the two operate as complementary governance technologies rather than opposites, a pattern termed coerced voluntariness for the German case. A six‐category narrative analysis finds burden ranked first in both parliaments, but the Turkish corpus shows a distinct fraternity‐to‐burden reversal after 2016. Despite documented institutional transfer, the two parliaments converge on burden yet diverge on securitisation: the Bundestag moves towards ethno‐nationalist framing while the Turkish security share stays low and stable. Institutional transfer, the evidence shows, is not matched by discursive convergence.

Relational Dynamics and Financial Preparedness for Old Age Among Immigrant Spouses in South Korea: Focusing on Gender Differences

Nayoung Heo, YoonKyung Kwak

Full text
This study examines how spousal satisfaction and social relations are associated with financial preparedness for old age among marriage‐based immigrants in South Korea, with attention to gender differences. Drawing on nationally representative data from the 2021 National Survey of Multicultural Families and gender‐stratified survey‐weighted regression models, we assess associations between relational resources and perceived preparedness. The findings show that spousal satisfaction and social relations are positively associated with preparedness for both women and men, though the patterns differ. For women, preparedness is linked with both marital and broader social ties, and the association with social relations is stronger at lower levels of spousal satisfaction. For men, preparedness is more strongly associated with spousal satisfaction, with social relations playing a weaker role. These results are interpreted through a social capital perspective, highlighting preparedness as relationally embedded. By analysing both male and female immigrant spouses—an often overlooked population—this study contributes to the growing literature on aging, relational dynamics and migrant integration in East Asia, and underscores the importance of gender‐sensitive perspectives in both research and policy.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

A DuBoisian Approach to Civic Education

Matthew D. Nelsen

Full text
This article presents a DuBosian approach to civic education: one that advocates for content that is racially inclusive and addresses political activism; centers conversations about politics; and ensures that K-12 educators reflect the diversity of their students. I argue that this approach provides a useful framework for classroom practices that can equip young people with the capacity needed to navigate and transform a democratic system defined by persistent racial inequality. I also assess whether there is public support for such an approach using a national sample of Americans. I find that a majority of respondents across racial, ethnic, and partisan lines, including many parents, support central tenets of a DuBoisian approach to civic education. By emphasizing the tangible pedagogical techniques associated with a DuBosian approach to civic education, it may be possible to sidestep the ambiguity and polarization of curriculum debates and reengage in the nuances of civic education policymaking.

American Sociological Review

Strategic Inversion: How Refugees Reconfigure Everyday Resistance under Benevolent Oppression

Rekha Krishnan, Aloysius Marcus Kahindi, Rajiv Krishnan Kozhikode

Full text
What does everyday resistance look like among dominated populations experiencing paternalistic care rather than extraction? Research on everyday resistance emerged from contexts where power extracts surplus from the oppressed. In this scholarship, the backstage is a space where the dominated cultivate hidden transcripts to cope with oppression, while the frontstage, where performances are enacted to those in power, is primary. Inversions of this order, when they occur, are momentary. Using ethnographic fieldwork in two Tanzanian refugee camps, we show that under benevolent oppression—control justified by paternalistic care rather than extraction—dominated populations respond through “strategic inversion”: a reordering of dramaturgical space where the backstage is primary and the frontstage remains tactical. Refugees moved the center of social life backstage, into a thriving underground economy, and kept up frontstage displays of helplessness to shield it. Within this alternative social order, competition and cooperation operated as two routes to heshima (dignity/deference). Competition resisted the uniformity of the refugee label, and cooperation rejected the helplessness it presumed. Refugees sustained this inversion through compliance flooding , rule-following performances so dense that officials could not tell forbidden from permitted activity; embedded enforcement , as guards became accomplices in maintaining the clandestine economy they policed; and collective coordination , where communities maintained alert systems and consistent performances to shield backstage activities. Our analysis extends research on everyday resistance by demonstrating that under benevolent oppression, subordinates actively build and experience empowerment, rather than merely imagining alternative moral orders or experiencing them briefly in bounded rituals.

Organizational Consolidation: How Organizations Reflect or Refract Societal Hierarchies

Brent Hoagland, Hana Shepherd

Full text
To understand how organizations distribute resources, we need to understand how they come to evaluate their members as worthy. This article introduces “organizational consolidation”: the degree to which ascriptive statuses—race, gender, class—align with the context-specific criteria that actors use when evaluating one another. Building on relational inequality theory and inhabited institutionalism, we argue that organizational consolidation shapes which attributes stand out, how actors categorize one another, and how combinations of attributes yield recognition or marginalization. When consolidation is high, local meaning-making is more likely to reproduce patterns of recognition that track ascriptive statuses; when it is low, alternative patterns become possible. We develop a concept-methods package that turns this framework into a roadmap for observing local patterns of recognition: association rule mining, latent class analysis, and partial order set modeling. We illustrate the value of these tools in a small, racially diverse U.S. high school where consolidation is low: teachers evaluate students through combinations of race, gender, and organizationally relevant characteristics, rather than any single attribute. By formalizing organizational consolidation as a measurable structural condition, we provide a diagnostic for comparing how organizations reflect or refract broader social hierarchies—shifting focus to how and under what organizational conditions inequality takes the specific forms it does.

Population and Development Review

Internal and International Migration of Scholars in Times of Conflict: The Case of Ukraine, 2009‐2022

Michael Zaslavsky, Aliakbar Akbaritabar

Full text
Current migration theories occasionally ignore the potentially negative aspects of scholarly migration as a form of high‐skilled migration, largely viewing it in a positive light. Yet scholarly migration is not always a tale of freedom and innovation, as the labels “researchers at risk” and “scientists in exile” demonstrate. We document the problematic aspects of conflict‐related scholarly migration among researchers with Ukraine‐affiliated publication histories from 2009 to 2022. We primarily draw on OpenAlex bibliometric records to reconstruct affiliation‐based migration histories and aggregate them to Ukraine's first‐level administrative regions. Results suggest that after 2014 there was substantial internal and international out‐migration from the occupied Eastern Ukrainian regions as well as the annexed Crimean peninsula, with over 50 per 1,000 scholars leaving the Eastern regions to both internal and international destinations and over 100 per 1,000 scholars out‐migrating from Crimea to international destinations in the first three years after the start of the conflict in 2014. These results show how armed conflict can be associated with a reorganization of national science systems by changing where researchers work and publish, thereby calling into question accounts that treat scholarly mobility primarily as voluntary high‐skilled migration.

How Political Events Become Demographic: Toward a Cumulative Demography of Conflict and Violence

Mathis Ebbinghaus, Liliana Andriano, Patrick Heuveline

Full text
Political conflict and violence shape the core demographic processes of mortality, migration, and fertility, while also influencing union formation, health, and kinship networks. Existing studies have often asked whether conflict is positively or negatively associated with demographic outcomes, yet findings remain strikingly mixed. This reflects the heterogeneous nature of conflict and violence themselves, the institutional and social contexts in which they occur, and the multiple, interacting processes through which they influence demographic outcomes. To advance a cumulative research program for conflict demography, we identify three priorities. First, researchers should distinguish exposure to conflict and violence from the extent to which individuals are affected by them and take seriously the processes of meaning‐making through which conflict events acquire demographic significance. Second, researchers should identify a limited set of generalizable social mechanisms that connect conflict and violence to demographic outcomes, recognizing that the same mechanism can produce different empirical results in different contexts. Third, researchers should develop macro‐level analyses of conflict‐affected populations because changes in population size, composition, and distribution are population‐level processes that cannot be derived solely from micro‐level mechanisms. The articles in this issue exemplify these three priorities and together outline a methodologically plural program for a cumulative conflict demography.

Curricular-credential decoupling: how schools respond to college and career readiness policy

Matt S Giani, Madison E Andrews, Tasneem Sultana, Fortunato N Medrano

Full text
This study examines schools’ college and career readiness (CCR) policy responses through the lens of decoupling. We investigate how high schools have jointly implemented career and technical education (CTE) and industry-based certifications (IBCs), and whether there is evidence of curricular-credential decoupling via misalignment between the subject areas of students’ CTE coursework and IBC completion. Descriptive analyses of Texas’s statewide longitudinal dataset (n = 2,119,750) demonstrate the rapid rise in certification rates combined with minimal change in CTE course-taking patterns and a concomitant decline in the rate of CTE-IBC alignment, suggesting schools may be using IBCs as a superficial way to meet CCR requirements. Regression analyses show modest relationships between student characteristics and IBC receipt and CTE-IBC misalignment, while schools explain a substantial proportion of variation. We develop a typology based on these school-level rates, which may be a useful tool for understanding schools’ strategies for CCR implementation across state policy contexts. By comparing school characteristics across typology categories, we highlight factors that may contribute to misalignment and inform policy.

Occupational hazard and political segregation in the US labor market

Ray Tsai Fang

Full text
The tendency for liberals and conservatives to cluster in different occupations significantly impacts society, yet the factors that contribute to this type of segregation remain incompletely understood. Bridging research from cultural and cognitive psychology, I develop a theory describing how occupational environments can shape people’s political ideologies, thereby contributing to political segregation in the labor market. Specifically, I argue that the more hazardous an occupation is, the more likely it is to have a higher concentration of conservative workers because dangerous work environments can socialize people to prefer order and stability. Using data from the Occupation Information Network, General Social Survey, and Bureau of Labor Statistics, I find that hazardous occupations tend to have higher concentrations of conservative workers, even after controlling for several alternative explanations. Further, using longitudinal data from the Youth-Parent Socialization Panel Study, I find that individuals employed in hazardous occupations tended to become more conservative over time relative to their peers in safer occupations. These findings suggest that liberals and conservatives may not only be sorting into different occupations; rather, certain occupational environments may contribute to political segregation in the labor market by shaping workers’ political views.

Review of International Organizations

The conditional effect of autocratization: How regime change shapes international preferences in the Universal Periodic Review

Maria Josepha Debre, Thomas Sommerer

Full text
International organizations (IOs) are increasingly affected by autocratization of their membership. However, we still know relatively little how both episodes of democratic backsliding and autocratic consolidation affect international preferences of IO member states towards liberal norms and institutions. We argue that autocratization creates uncertainties that are consequential for how member states position themselves within IOs, but that the direction of this effect differs across regime types because of varying constraints in democracies and autocracies. Democratic backsliders are primarily concerned with domestic constraints and the risk of empowering opposition through international behavior, whereas autocratic consolidators are more focused on international audiences and reputation costs associated with autocratization. We test our argument in the specific field of human rights by employing information on the acceptance behavior of states in the Universal Period Review between 2008 and 2020. In our quantitative analysis, we find support for the proposed conditional effect of autocratization and regime type when controlling for other predictors of acceptance behavior. Autocratizing democracies are less likely to accept critical comments, whereas autocratizing autocracies are more likely. We also find that this dynamic shifts in recent years likely due to changing international attention put on democratic backsliding states. By integrating human rights and international relations scholarship, this study offers a differentiated account of when states will resist or embrace liberal norms under conditions of autocratization.

Gender, descriptive representation, and the public legitimacy of international organizations

Tyler Girard, Andrea Lawlor

Full text
How does descriptive representation influence citizens’ perceptions of international organizations’ (IOs) legitimacy? While past work has investigated the impact of procedural and substantive dimensions on the legitimacy of IOs, the effect of descriptive representation remains largely unaddressed. We contribute to this literature by investigating whether greater gender representation increases the legitimacy of an IO when the IO governs a stereotypically “feminine” (vs. “masculine”) issue area and whether this relationship is contingent on attitudes toward gender equality. We test our expectations using pre-registered 2 × 2 factorial and conjoint experiments in Canada ( n = 1998). We find consistent evidence that individuals evaluate the legitimacy of both the IO and leader more favorably when the leader is female rather than male. Issue area and gender egalitarian attitudes attenuate the magnitude of this effect, but do not reverse it. Our article advances the study of gender representation in global governance and popular sources of IO legitimacy.

Journal of Peace Research

International scientific collaboration and political protest

Nils B Weidmann, Mina Rulis, Tore Wig

Full text
Universities as the main institutions of higher education are often assumed to be driving forces of democratization and political liberalization. In autocratic countries, political protest is one of the few ways by which political authority can be challenged, and research has shown that universities often constitute focal points for these protests. However, which universities are particularly likely to see protest? In this paper, we focus on international scientific collaboration. We argue that academic collaboration with universities abroad can facilitate the exchange of information and ideas that challenge autocratic rule. We test our expectations in a preregistered disaggregated study, combining data on academic institutions in autocracies, large publication databases and protest event data. Our findings show that the presence of institutions with a high degree of international scientific collaboration is associated with more protest at a location. However, we find no evidence for our expectation that collaboration with democracies, or in particular disciplines (social sciences), matters more. Overall, our results suggest that beyond the impact on research itself, international academic collaboration can have important political implications in nondemocratic environments.

Shifting sands? The impact of conflict displacement on voting

Gal Bitton

Full text
Does exposure to conflict displacement amplify support for far-right parties? This study investigates the political consequences of conflict-induced displacement in northern Israel by exploiting a natural experiment arising from government-ordered evacuations during military conflicts. Using a pre-registered survey and a regression discontinuity design based on an exogenous 5 km threshold, I assess changes in retrospective and prospective voting behavior as well as self-reported political ideology. The analysis reveals no evidence that displacement increases far-right support or induces partisan realignment. Rather, evacuees demonstrate a modest rightward shift in ideological self-placement without corresponding changes in voting behavior. This divergence suggests that displacement influences symbolic political positioning without prompting corresponding changes in voting behavior. These patterns unfold against a broader backdrop of political frustration and electoral disengagement that affects both displaced and non-displaced populations. The findings reveal troubling implications for democratic accountability during external conflict: leaders may be shielded from electoral consequences of security failures even as civic engagement deteriorates, challenging models of wartime accountability.

Right up my alley: Local radical right success, contextual alignment, and immigration attitudes in Sweden

Stefan Dahlberg, Hilma Lindskog, Henrik Oscarsson

Full text
Electoral success of radical right parties is often argued to normalize previously stigmatized anti-immigration attitudes. Yet, it remains unclear whether such normalization reflects genuine within-individual persuasion or shifts in the local climate of political expression. We address this question by linking highly disaggregated district-level vote shares to individual-level panel survey data from Sweden (2018–2022), a period marked by sustained growth and partial normalization of the Sweden Democrats. Cross-sectional analyses reveal pronounced contextual patterns: Restrictive immigration attitudes are more prevalent in districts with higher local radical right support, particularly among voters who do not support the party, while core supporters remain comparatively stable. However, first-difference panel analyses provide little evidence that changes in local radical right support are associated with attitudinal change. The findings suggest that what appears as normalization in ecological analyses is more consistent with contextual alignment than with rapid persuasion, clarifying the micro-foundations of radical right normalization.

The spatial politics of International Climate Finance: A case of India

Soham Banerjee, Dishil Shrimankar, Matthew Paterson

Full text
Do political parties shape where International Climate Funds are allocated? We examine this question in the context of India, where International Climate Funds create opportunities for the central government to leverage externally funded resources to advance domestic political interests. Drawing on a novel geo-coded dataset of project locations from the Kyoto Protocol’s Clean Development Mechanism, we show that projects are more likely to be allocated to constituencies represented by co-partisans of the Prime Minister’s party, and that this effect is strongest in competitive constituencies within states governed by opposition parties, where the central government has the sharpest incentives to build new support. This is surprising because existing literature on the Clean Development Mechanism suggests that decisions on project allocations are depoliticized. By demonstrating otherwise, this article contributes to the literature on International Climate Funds by demonstrating the importance of domestic politics in shaping its distribution.

The changing meaning of left-right self-placement in the United Kingdom

Joelle Tasker

Full text
Understanding how voters use the left-right scale is essential, as it is not only related to vote choice but also underpins how political scientists study ideology. This article finds that the way voters use the left-right scale is variable. Using British Election Study Internet Panel data from 2014 to 2024, I find that while economic and cultural values are both related to left-right self-placement, this varies both between individuals and over time. Those on the left of the self-placement scale can best be differentiated from one another by the strength of their economic values. For those on the right, cultural values are more useful in separating between different self-placement levels. Over time, left-right self-placement has become increasingly related to cultural values alongside economic values, driven by movements by people on both sides of the left-right scale. These findings indicate that even within one country, the meanings of ‘left’ and ‘right’ vary.

Friendship in World Politics: George Padmore, Nancy Cunard, and Dorothy Pizer at the End of Empire

Isabelle Napier

Full text
Abstracts This article develops an historical account of friendship as a political relation central to the twentieth-century project of decolonization through an analysis of the writings and political relationships of Trinidadian Pan-African intellectual-activist George Padmore. Towards the end of his life, Padmore thanked “selected allies” and “friends” for their anti-racist and anti-colonial agitation. He conceptualized a political relation of friendship between colonized and colonizer as a potential aid to ending imperial and racialized subordination and domination. Drawing on archival sources, I recover Padmore’s concept of friendship together with its practice by two White women allies: Anglo-American writer Nancy Cunard and English stenographer Dorothy Pizer. Friendship emerges from the situated theorizing and practice of these three intellectual-activists as an always aspirational, potentially transformative, yet inherently asymmetrical political relation. Grounded in difference, anti-colonial friendship was a political practice that unsettled racial hierarchies in the everyday and aimed ultimately to end empire.

Populism and the Politics of Aid Classification: Evidence from the OECD Development Assistance Committee

Nicolas Bau, Simone Dietrich, Katharina Fleiner, Alice Iannantuoni

Full text
Abstracts Populist leaders increasingly criticize global governance systems, portraying them as elitist and threatening national sovereignty. While research has explored how populism affects international organizations, less is known about how a rise in populism in donor countries affects engagement with standards in international development cooperation. This research note argues that populism affects how donors classify and present foreign aid projects. Focusing on the OECD’s Development Assistance Committee (DAC), we theorize a mechanism of classification avoidance, whereby bureaucrats anticipate political scrutiny from populists and reduce the use of policy markers that signal alignment with global priorities. Using project-level data on aid activities from 1998 to 2022, we show that increases in anti-elitism in donor legislatures are associated with declines in the share of aid classified as targeting DAC priority areas, alongside stable project screening practices and some but more limited sectoral reallocation. While we do not directly observe bureaucratic decision-making, the empirical patterns are broadly more consistent with anticipatory bureaucratic adaptation than with alternative accounts of policy change. Our paper highlights how domestic political dynamics shape engagement with international standards through bureaucratic practices.

From Domestic Contestation to Alpha Male Foreign Policy: Phantasies of Masculinity and the US Self

Veronica Barfucci, Deborah Faudoa Rodríguez, C Nicolai L Gellwitzki

Full text
Abstracts How does masculinity inform the 2024 US presidential election and subsequent US foreign policy? To address this question, we develop the concept of phantasy of masculinity, an affectively charged imaginary that links normalized gender discourses with the narrative reconstruction of Self and Other(s), and that becomes increasingly regressive along a spectrum of intensification towards hypermasculinity. Analyzing official speeches and the media discourse surrounding the 2024 US presidential election campaign and the first year of foreign policy in the second Trump administration, we show that phantasies of masculinity Othering domestic political rivals were central to narrative contestation for both Republicans and Democrats. We further argue that US foreign policy is regressing towards hypermasculinity, in an affectively driven yet ultimately futile attempt to restore ontological security under conditions of perceived crisis. The article makes two contributions to International Relations (IR) scholarship. First, it develops the concept of phantasy of masculinity as a framework for analyzing the psychological and affective dimensions of masculinity. Second, it offers an explanation for seemingly “irrational” US foreign policy under Trump. We conclude by calling for closer integration of feminist IR and Ontological Security Studies to better understand geopolitical implications of gendered insecurities in an uncertain era.

Donor Influence at the African Development Bank

Tetsekela Anyiam-Osigwe, James Raymond Vreeland

Full text
Major powers have prized influence in Africa throughout history. This paper investigates sway over Africa’s foremost international financial institution: the African Development Bank (AfDB). We focus on the two largest nonregional shareholders, the United States and Japan. Analyzing panel data of AfDB loans from 1991 to 2021, we find that increases in Japanese bilateral aid as well as voting with Japan at the United Nations are associated with larger AfDB commitments. We do not uncover similarly convincing evidence of US influence at the AfDB. Qualitative evidence suggests that unlike the United States, Japan’s imperial past and lack of historical ties with Africa encourage a quiet yet persuasive engagement with the institution, which furthers the country’s economic and political aims on the continent. Our results advance the view that governments can pursue their self-interests through international institutions and that they may be particularly successful when they do so subtly.

Friending or Blocking? U.S. Public Attitudes toward Restricting Foreign Social Media Apps

Ka Zeng, Damian Raess, Paul Musgrave

Full text
What influences U.S. public support for bans of foreign social media applications? In particular, how do concerns about national security, economic opportunities and threats (such as industrial espionage), reciprocal market access, and the policies and traits of an app influence individual support for banning? We address these questions through a conjoint experiment conducted in November 2024 (N = 1,494). Respondents weighed security threats (such as risks to military interests) and economic interests (including job creation and reciprocal market access) heavily. Concerns about social media platforms themselves, such as strong data privacy protection and content moderation, also mattered, but to a lesser extent. A recontact survey conducted in spring 2025, after President Trump’s decision to reverse the January 2025 TikTok ban, shows stability over time. Overall, our findings reveal a surprisingly strong and stable consensus about what factors promote support for bans—with TikTok-like features arousing opposition among the American public. Nuanced differences do emerge: Republicans and Democrats differ over content moderation policies, while TikTok users and nonusers diverge regarding security threats and owners’ ties to governments. Our findings underscore the importance of the geopolitical and economic implications of foreign apps as well as how consumer experiences influence threat perceptions.

Charisma goes viral: Péter Magyar and the digital construction of political authority

Rudolf Metz, Eszter Farkas, Veronika Kövesdi

Full text
How is charismatic authority constructed and rewarded in digital political communication? This article develops a multidimensional framework for analysing digital charismatic signalling and applies it to Péter Magyar’s rapid political rise in Hungary. Drawing on leadership studies, social psychology and political communication, we conceptualise charisma as a relational, signalling-based process enacted through exceptional personal qualities, community construction, visionary appeals and collective challenges. Using quantitative content analysis of 486 Facebook posts published between February and June 2024, we examine how specific signals predict likes, love reactions, shares and comments. The findings show that digital charisma does not operate as a uniform engagement engine. Relational, emotional and identity-based cues generate the strongest affective engagement, especially similarity to followers, cultural-historical references, emotional mobilisation and normative-symbolic signals. Competence-oriented cues and visionary appeals have limited independent effects, while blame attribution stimulates comments. The article advances a fine-grained account of digitally mediated charismatic followership.

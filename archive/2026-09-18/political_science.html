Turning Hazard Models Inside Out

David Melamed, Jack G. R. Wippell, Eric W. Schoon, Ronald L. Breiger

Full text
Regression Inside Out (RIO) describes a set of related methods based on a decomposition of regression coefficients by individual cases. In this article, we offer three important extensions of RIO: 1) we generalize RIO to any model for which linear predictors are estimated; 2) we introduce an adapted bootstrap approach for determining whether case-level contributions differ from the baseline average contribution for cases; and 3) we describe how RIO is related to model diagnostics, particularly DFBETA. To illustrate the value added of these extensions, we apply RIO to a published example of the Cox proportional hazard model exploring factors associated with democratic durability.

Comparative Political Studies

Inhibitory Institutions: The Tradeoffs of Public Goods Quantity and Quality

Alisha C. Holland

Full text
Governments frequently invest in public goods that citizens don’t want, yet voters hesitate to punish politicians who build useless or corrupt projects. I introduce the concept of inhibitory institutions—rules that allow state or societal actors to review, obstruct, or veto public investments before they are approved—and argue that they improve public goods quality at the cost of reduced quantity. This quantity-quality tradeoff binds most tightly when bureaucrats enjoy career autonomy from politicians and political competition encourages institutional enforcement. I illustrate the tradeoff through case studies of metro projects in Bogotá and Lima, where divergent institutional conditions led to contrasting outcomes for the quantity and quality of construction. Quantitative evidence from local infrastructure projects in Colombia suggests that political alliances to bypass inhibitory rules increase spending but reduce quality. At a time when observers want to cut procedural checks to get things built, this article underscores the risks for what gets built.

British Journal of Political Science

Policy as a Bargaining Outcome: Coalition Leverage and Pledge Fulfillment

Mark A. Kayser, Jochen Rehmert, Petra Schleiter

Full text
Which parties can deliver the policies that they promised? In multiparty contexts, parties must bargain to realize electoral promises. Prior work shows that parties influence policy change by using resources such as legislative seats, ministerial posts, and agenda, veto, and policing powers, while only theoretical work considers parties’ bargaining leverage from potentially changing the government. But for parties, fulfilling pledges (not incremental policy change) is paramount and they use their full leverage to achieve it. We offer and test a more complete and politically relevant account of multiparty policymaking that includes parties’ bargaining leverage through coalition options and focuses on the realization of distinct policy promises. We estimate regression analyses of pledge fulfillment in nine parliamentary democracies from 1974 to 2011 as well as local average treatment effects across a discontinuity and find that parties’ bargaining leverage from potential coalition change trumps conventional explanations for policy outcomes.

The Audience of Coalition Bargaining: Voter Reactions to Portfolio Allocation

Ida B. Hjermitslev, Mathias Wessel Tromborg

Full text
Obtaining portfolios is one of the most important pay-offs that parties receive from getting into office. Accordingly, we know a lot about how portfolios are distributed between coalition partners, but surprisingly little about how voters react to these distributional outcomes. We address this research gap both theoretically and empirically. Theoretically, we argue that voters use cues from the quantitative and qualitative portfolio distributions to evaluate coalition partners’ competence in negotiations, which in turn shapes party support. Empirically, we use original experimental and panel survey data from the 2022 Danish general election. We find that voters infer negotiation competence from the number, power, and party-specific salience of the portfolios a party obtains. We also demonstrate that many voters paid attention to the real-world negotiation outcome and withdrew their support for the parties they perceived handled the negotiations less competently.

Reshaping Belonging: National Identity Change in Post-Referendum Britain

Jac M. Larner, James David Griffiths, Daniel Wincott

Full text
National identity is usually treated as a stable driver of political behaviour, yet we know little about whether it responds to major political events. We examine how two pivotal electoral events – the 2014 Scottish Independence Referendum and the 2016 EU Referendum – were associated with changes in citizens’ sense of national belonging. Using the 2014–2025 British Election Study Internet Panel, we show that both referendums precipitated significant shifts in national identification, primarily among those on the losing side. Post-referendum changes in Scotland proved temporary, but among Remain voters British identification fell substantially and remained depressed through 2025, with the gap between winners and losers widening over time. These findings suggest national identity is more event-sensitive than commonly assumed, and that divisive referendums may leave durable marks on how citizens in multinational states relate to the state by eroding shared national sentiment.

From Antagonistic Talk to Cooperative Acts: The Effect of Polarization Cues on Newspaper Audiences

Mike Farjam, Hendrik Meyer

Full text
Do media depictions of a ‘polarized society’ merely describe division or actually deepen it? We report two laboratory experiments in Germany (Study1 $$N=302$$ ; Study2 $$N=295$$ ). After reading authentic newspaper articles in which brief polarization cues were experimentally included versus omitted, participants (i) completed standard affective- and ideological-polarization measures and (ii) played incentivized Dictator, Ultimatum, and Trust Games with ideological out-group partners. Polarization cues increased both affective polarization and ideological extremity. Behavioral responses, however, were mixed. In the Dictator Game, where the recipient cannot respond, treated participants shared less with the out-group. In the Trust Game, characterized by mutual dependence and potential efficiency gains, treated participants sent more to out-group partners. Ultimatum behavior, where interdependence is partial, showed weaker and less consistent shifts. Taken together, polarization cues reliably heighten perceived division but do not uniformly translate into behavioral antagonism: even after cues that increase self-reported polarization, participants can still cooperate with ideological opponents when cooperation is perceived as mutually beneficial.

The Limits of Incumbent Blame Games in Times of Crisis: Experimental Evidence from Electoral Autocracy

Zeki Sarigil, Ayşenur Dal

Full text
This study investigates how blame-attributing crisis frames influence support for incumbents in an electoral authoritarian context, focusing on Türkiye. It asks how, to what extent, and under what conditions incumbents’ blame frames shape public attitudes toward the government. The study examines four crisis types (i.e., global warming , earthquake , economic collapse , and pandemic ) and compares the effects of religious , conspiratorial , and scientific framing across these contexts. Drawing on original data from seven pre-survey focus groups and a nationwide face-to-face survey experiment with 3,562 respondents, the findings indicate that the independent effects of blame frames are generally limited. However, the effectiveness of religion-based blame framing is highly context dependent. Compared with climate and economic crises, its estimated effects are more favorable in crises characterized by high uncertainty and relatively low perceived governmental responsibility, such as earthquake and pandemic. These findings suggest that the effectiveness of blame attribution is conditioned by the nature of the crisis itself. The study contributes to research on crisis framing, political accountability, and authoritarian durability.

Credible Prejudice? When and Why Mainstream Party Adoption of Far-Right Rhetoric Weakens Anti-Prejudice Norms

Ali Karčić

Full text
This article examines whether mainstream political parties adopt prejudiced rhetoric from the far right and how such shifts shape anti-prejudice norms. Building on existing work, I argue that mainstream parties are particularly influential in weakening anti-prejudice norms because they possess greater normative credibility and legitimacy than far-right parties. Empirically, the article combines cross-national observational evidence with two preregistered survey experiments in Germany and Denmark. Using parliamentary speeches from six European countries between 1994 and 2022, I document rising mainstream party anti-Muslim rhetoric and its association with weaker norms against holding far-right views. The experiments provide causal evidence for the argument. In Germany, exposure to prejudiced rhetoric weakens anti-prejudice norms, but only when voiced by mainstream parties, rather than far right parties, likely due to their normative credibility and legitimacy. In Denmark, comparable effects are absent, consistent with decades of mainstream accommodation having already normalized such rhetoric. The experimental results also show that rhetoric targeting Muslims weakens anti-prejudice norms toward out-groups more broadly, suggesting spillover effects. These findings have important implications for future scholarship: when mainstream parties adopt prejudiced rhetoric from the far right, they affect not only electoral politics, but also the broader social norm environment.

Do Political Discussion Partners Share Policy Priorities and Policy Frames?

Matthew T. Pietryka, Amber E. Boydstun, Taylor N. Carlson

Full text
To understand politics, people often rely on political discussion. Among the many ways that discussion may shape political perceptions, one possible mechanism is by promoting a shared sense of priorities— which issues are important. A second mechanism is by framing how people think about important issues. We examine these potential mechanisms by answering the descriptive question: Do policy priorities and policy frames tend to co-occur within political discussion networks? We answer this question by reanalyzing the 1996 Indianapolis-St. Louis Study in which participants—and their discussion partners—described the most important problem facing the United States in their own words. We show that the modal outcome is for people to hold both different priorities and different frames than their discussion partners. This finding stands in contrast to network scholarship suggesting that homophily is so often the norm. Yet we also show that people disproportionately share the same policy priorities as their discussion partners, and adopt the same frames in thinking about policy topics, especially when their policy priorities match. This finding suggests that some policy priorities are so embedded in certain frames of thinking that the topic and frame stay linked even as the topic may diffuse through a social network.

Public Opinion Quarterly

Voting for Democracy? Testing Political-Cultural Constraints on Democratic Backsliding

Christopher Claassen

Full text
Democratic backsliding continues to pose a clear danger to contemporary democracies. While a growing literature shows that voters often tolerate antidemocratic candidates when partisan or ideological incentives pull in that direction, we know less about whether support for democracy, institutional trust, political tolerance, and other democratic orientations ameliorate such temptations. We address this question with a systematic comparison of six measures of political culture as predictors of electoral resistance to antidemocratic candidates. Using candidate-choice conjoint experiments fielded in three democracies facing backsliding pressures—the United States, Poland, and South Korea—we find that several democratic orientations are associated with greater resistance to antidemocratic candidates. Among them, support for the core institutions and processes of liberal democracy stands out as the most consistent predictor. Its relationship with prodemocratic choice remains meaningful even in “cross-pressured” choices where partisan or ideological incentives pull voters away from the prodemocratic option. These findings suggest that political culture matters for democratic resilience, though some components of it matter much more consistently than others.

Political Psychology

Partisans misperceive and are influenced by dynamic norms

Trystan Loustau, Ashley Fabrizio, Liane Young, Jordan Wylie, Stylianos Syropoulos

Full text
Perceptions of social norms, and how those norms change over time, play a central role in shaping how people understand and respond to political divides. We developed and iteratively refined dynamic‐norm messages about bipartisan attitude change to reduce divisive outparty perceptions and attitudes in a US context. We first identified real‐world issues that gained support over time among both Democrats and Republicans and were judged as believable. Study 1 documented systematic misperceptions of attitude change across a range of social issues and linked these misperceptions to colder outparty feelings. We then tested intervention messages that adapted standard static‐norm templates with the addition of information about change over time (dynamic norm; Study 2). This intervention increased expectations of future common ground but did not reliably improve downstream outparty attitudes. Next, we tested a refined intervention message that isolated dynamic norm information (Study 3). The refined dynamic norm message increased expected common ground and expected future feelings of belonging among the outparty and was associated with small but reliable increases in outparty warmth and positive outparty impressions relative to control. These results suggest that emphasizing trajectories of public opinion without highlighting current partisan gaps may be a useful strategy for reducing polarization.

Government and Opposition

Culture Trumps Class and Place: How the Populist Radical Right Thrives on Sociocultural Conflicts in Western Europe

Mirko Crulli, Gianluca Piccolino

Full text
Using original survey data from seven Western European countries, this article investigates which social conflicts populist radical right parties (PRRPs) represent. Methodologically, it innovates by measuring individuals’ positions within conflicts combined with the importance they attach to them, capturing both attitudinal alignment and salience. The analysis tests whether traditional cleavages or new conflicts related to globalisation and cultural value change better explain PRRP voting. Findings show that PRRPs predominantly represent new conflicts – individuals opposing cosmopolitanism, LGBTQ+ rights and environmental sustainability – rather than class or territorial divides. While socio-structural traits such as being a production worker or rural resident predict PRRP voting, supporting these groups does not. This decoupling of structure and conflict positions suggests working-class and rural voters are motivated more by culturally reframed grievances than by class- or place-based solidarities. These results highlight the weak link between classic cleavages and PRRP voting, underscoring the centrality of cultural demarcation in today’s political conflict.

Why Northern Ireland’s Institutions Need Reform

Sean Haughey, Jon Tonge, Ben Rosher

Full text
In a previous volume of this journal, scholars of consociational power-sharing counselled against reform of Northern Ireland’s power-sharing institutions. Since then, repeated episodes of collapse and dysfunction have necessitated reconsideration of the case for institutional reform. This article argues that a sensible case now exists for reforms to enhance the stability and functionality of Northern Ireland’s consociation. Situating its analysis within broader debates on the evolution of consociational systems, it draws on elite political discourse and original public opinion data (N = 2,034) to assess support for reform and to consider associated trade-offs. We show that changing electoral and ethnonational dynamics have occasioned shifts in elite political attitudes to reform, and highlight public appetite for reform, including in relation to one of the consociation’s most powerful vetoes. Sustainable reform, the article concludes, will be most feasible when elite receptivity to change, civic involvement and (subtle) external support converge.

Annals of the American Academy of Political and Social Science

Measuring Merit in College Admissions

Rebecca Zwick

Full text
I discuss the concepts of merit and meritocracy as they apply to college admissions in the U.S., paying particular attention to the question of whether standardized tests should be regarded as measures of merit. Several factors—the Varsity Blues cheating scandal, the pandemic, and an increasing awareness of systemic racism—have led to a dramatic move away from test requirements: Most U.S. colleges do not currently require admissions tests. Traditionalists have deplored this change, viewing test scores as a central pillar of meritocratic admissions. However, critics of the traditional view have advocated for emphasizing such qualities as creativity, ethical behavior, and communication skills in selecting applicants, arguing that these characteristics are, in fact, aspects of merit. Finally, some scholars have maintained that meritocratic selection need not involve either academic criteria or aspects of character. From this perspective, the definition of merit is entirely dependent on the goals of the institution.

Power and Organizational Opacity in College Admissions

Samuel Maves, Mario L. Small

Full text
Top American colleges balance ideals of meritocracy and racial diversity to make admissions decisions. But recent legal and political challenges undermine their ability to openly pursue racial diversity. What will be the likely effect of these challenges? In this speculative article, we argue that the long-term impact on student body composition is likely to be limited at schools that continue to prioritize racial diversity, as a result of organizational opacity : the capacity of complex organizations to obscure their activities from outsiders. We speculate that mounting external pressures will lead admissions offices to manipulate an increasingly complex set of organizational levers to pursue both meritocracy and diversity, at least to the extent that both continue to be valued as worthwhile ideals. While often effective, increasing opacity will also carry significant risks for colleges and universities, which we discuss.

Meritocracy and Higher Education in the United States

Harry Brighouse, AriEL H. Collins

Full text
This article disambiguates different conceptions of merit to consider whether higher education is currently, or could plausibly become, meritocratic. We argue that contemporary societies are not meritocratic in the commonly popularized sense and that because society is not meritocratic, college admissions could not be. Calls for college admissions to be meritocratic demand that they reward developed abilities, not native abilities, which makes meritocracy impossible in societies that maintain the institution of the family. Next, we explain why meritocracy, so understood, would be undesirable even if it were possible. Further, we argue that even societies that are not meritocratic may instantiate some of the damaging features associated with meritocracy, because those features are a consequence of people believing their society is meritocratic—thus, the irony that basing elite college admissions on “merit” may cause further harms because it exacerbates the (false) belief that society is meritocratic.

Stratification by Design: Meritocracy and the Reproduction of Inequality

Lauren A. Rivera

Full text
Meritocracy is often celebrated as a fair system in which social positions are distributed according to individual ability rather than inherited privilege. Yet, evidence shows that meritocratic systems routinely reproduce inequality. I argue that such outcomes are not failures of meritocracy but constitutive features of how it operates. Drawing on existing research, I identify three mechanisms through which elites sustain their dominance in ostensibly merit-based systems: consecration (power to define and evaluate merit), adaptation (unequal capacity to cultivate valued traits), and co-optation (strategic use of meritocratic ideals to resist challenges to privilege). I further contend that the underlying premise of meritocracy—that some individuals are more deserving than others—legitimizes social hierarchies and can result in the dehumanization of marginalized groups. I conclude by arguing that genuine fairness requires not only less biased measures of merit but also deeper structural transformations that reduce the high stakes of social stratification.

Inhabited Professions: How Cultural Norms of Excellence Anchor Inequality in Professions

Mary Blair-Loy, Erin A. Cech

Full text
To advance understanding of how cultural notions of merit in professions perpetuate inequality, we introduce the novel concept of “inhabited professions.” We argue that notions of professional merit play out within day-to-day interactions among workplace colleagues in ways that generate negative consequences for those who deviate from predominant conceptions of excellence. As a case, we focus on how a particular professional norm, the (de)valuation of diversity concerns, shapes gender inequality in STEM departments. Using data from 266 academic scientists nested in 14 departments, we find that women with a substantial gap between their personal concern for diversity and their colleagues’ beliefs about the importance of promoting diversity are significantly more likely to report chilly departmental climates, to feel disrespected, and to intend to leave the university than men in their departments. While women defenders of diversity are treated as less worthy, men diversity proponents are buffered from these negative consequences.

Meritocratic Pay Beliefs in the Contemporary United States

Jake Rosenfeld, Letao Wang

Full text
What do workers think should determine their pay? And what do they think does determine their pay? We analyze prior surveys of U.S. workers and an original 2025 survey to assess pay beliefs, finding widespread and long-standing support for meritocratic pay principles. Since the early 1990s, majorities of full-time workers subscribe to the idea that pay should be based at least in part on job performance, and in 2025 nearly half of workers report that individual performance should be the main factor determining their pay. Results are generally consistent across key sociodemographic and labor market characteristics, although women are significantly more likely to believe their performance should be the most important determinant of their pay, and public sector workers and union members are less likely to believe that their pay is performance-based. We conclude by assessing the policy implications of these meritocratic pay norms.

Caste and Meritocracy in India

Bishnupriya Gupta, Kaivan Munshi, Swapnil Singh

Full text
The caste system in India acts as a barrier to meritocracy. For centuries the lowest castes were disadvantaged socially and economically. Evidence from the colonial censuses of the early twentieth century shows large differences in literacy and occupation between the highest and the lowest castes. After independence, India adopted affirmative action in state-funded education and public sector jobs that benefited people in the so-called scheduled castes and tribes, who were the disadvantaged groups. We show that since the 1980s, there has been convergence in education and occupation between the upper castes and that population.

From State-Sponsored to Market-Driven Meritocracy? Changes in the French Education System and Higher Education Admissions

Agnès van Zanten

Full text
This article presents an analysis of changes to the concept of meritocracy in France, highlighting the interrelation of social configurations and how they influence the definition and enactment of merit. I present a macro-level analysis of changing conceptions of merit and a micro-level one of how these changes have been implemented in the admissions process of a prestigious higher education institution. I argue that in the past two decades, there has been a partial but significant replacement of a state-sponsored meritocracy by a market-driven one. This change has led to a hybridization of the aristocratic, scholastic, and market-oriented cultural criteria that are used to appraise students’ merit and also to the development of private services and devices alongside public ones. These processes have not altered the unequal pattern of vertical stratification in France but instead have introduced new “horizontal” movements in the social space.

The Siamese Crocodiles: Meritocratic Equilibria in African Perspective

Pierre Nguimkeu, James A. Robinson

Full text
The notion of meritocracy is in crisis in the Western world, with scholars and citizens arguing that it creates a divisive equilibrium with high levels of inequality and cultural polarization. We argue that the meritocratic equilibrium that is currently being scrutinized is more culturally embedded than its critics recognize. We show that in Africa, for example, beliefs, practices, and norms allow for the existence of an equilibrium that is genuinely meritocratic and also redistributive. We show how African cultures tend to combine individualism and communalism, and that in achieving this balance, Africans innovated different norms and mechanisms that attempt to harmonize the interests of the individual with those of the community. These mechanisms do appear to break down in parts of Africa where witchcraft is used as a mechanism for enforcing the social contract.

Meritocracy and the Networks It Forged: China’s Imperial Examination

Ying Bai, Ruixue Jia

Full text
Institutions that claim to reward merit often also serve as engines that drive the formation of important social networks. We examine this dual role in China’s imperial examination, one of history’s longest-lived systems of meritocracy. We draw on the exam performance data of 24,269 individuals across 112 national examinations in the Qing dynasty (1644–1905) to trace who among them rose to become top officials in the dynasty. Strong exam performance increased the likelihood of attaining high office, but advancement also depended on individuals’ ties to powerful examiners. Because performance and ties to powerful examiners were strongly complementary, individuals’ success appeared to rest on merit even as social networks played a role. And after the dramatic political changes of the 1840s, ties to powerful examiners still remained influential, although the influential examiners themselves changed. These findings help to explain why such systems retain legitimacy even when networks matter greatly.

Latin America’s Persistent Disorder: Meritocracy Without Mobility

Juan Camilo Cárdenas, Leopoldo Fergusson

Full text
Latin America combines strong beliefs in meritocracy with persistently high inequality, crime, and informality. Explaining this “meritocracy without mobility” requires understanding how institutions, culture, and social norms jointly sustain an unfair and inefficient equilibrium. We argue that this equilibrium rests on three mutually reinforcing foundations: deep mistrust of public institutions, low interpersonal trust, and an untamed individualism that privileges private over collective solutions. These cultural and normative forces weaken provision of public goods, normalize rule-breaking, and preserve belief in meritocracy despite low social mobility. Meritocracy is largely imagined but nonetheless fits this individualistic culture, legitimizing the status quo. It is also perversely validated by limited mobility in the widely accepted informal, and at times illegal, economy. These dynamics help explain why reforms often fail to provide widely beneficial outcomes, as they cannot surmount weak institutions and entrenched beliefs, social norms, and practices. The State and Gender Equality: Sweden—Model or Aberration?

Lena Edlund

Full text
The Nordic countries are leaders in gender equality in the workplace and in the home. Why is this the case? State capacity, this article argues. For the most part, modern states seek to reduce the extent to which ascriptive traits establish status in the population, because ascriptive power hierarchies distort meritocratic talent allocation and thus economic progress. This article places gender equality in a long line of state-led policies that level the salience of ascriptive traits, on par with the abolition of noble and servile statuses, equality before the law, and the expansion of suffrage. I review the economic and political arguments for equality—including gender equality—in comparative historical context, focusing on Sweden as a case in point of how Nordic countries’ high state legitimacy and bureaucratic capacity have allowed them to chip away at gender roles, which are arguably the last bastion of ascriptive status.

Party Politics

Rain, temperature, and votes: Weather effects on electoral participation in Argentina

Fernando Antonio Ignacio González

Full text
Compulsory voting was designed to make electoral participation immune to individual costs, yet this paper shows that rain on election day still keeps Argentine voters home. Exploiting quasi-random variation in precipitation across 526 departments and 14 national election days between 2011 and 2023, I estimate two-way fixed effects models with department and election fixed effects using ERA5 reanalysis data matched to department centroids. Each additional millimeter of voting-hours precipitation (8am–6pm) reduces turnout by 0.08 percentage points ( p < 0.01); on a day of average rainfall this implies about a 0.2 percentage-point reduction. The estimate survives an extensive identification battery: balance tests, a 9-day placebo window with horse races, grid-cell clustering, and a validation of the reanalysis data against independent rain-gauge stations. Using newly constructed vote shares by ideological bloc, I find that rainfall does not bias the partisan outcome—it leaves the incumbent vote share, the left/Peronist bloc, and the left–right balance unchanged, in contrast to the partisan effects documented under voluntary voting in Europe—and it has no effect on blank ballots, null votes, or fragmentation. Weather thus operates through the extensive margin (whether to vote) rather than the intensive margin (how to vote). If even a century-old compulsory voting institution cannot fully insulate turnout from weather shocks, institutional reforms such as postal voting, extended hours, or remote electronic voting deserve consideration even where voting is mandatory.

Varieties of left-wing authoritarianism? The local foundations and intra-party heterogeneity of the German challenger party BSW

Eric Linhart, Jan Philipp Thomeczek, Manès Weisskircher, Benjamin Höhne

Full text
Research has emphasized both the existence of left-wing authoritarian attitudes among the European electorate and a lack of political supply. In Germany, this dynamic changed with the rise of the BSW. Advancing research on left-wing authoritarianism in general and the BSW specifically, we rely on original Voting Advice Application (VAA) data from six city elections in 2024, allowing us to study the supply- and demand-side at the local level. We highlight three findings: First, BSW’s local-level positions vary widely across policies, including left-wing, moderate, and (moderately) right-wing stances. Second, BSW’s intra-party heterogeneity is higher on socio-cultural than on socio-economic issues. Third, BSW voters’ answers to VAA questions are more right-leaning than their self-placement. Our findings on the BSW offer key insights into an empirically observable case of left-wing authoritarianism, highlighting nuanced perspectives on the ideology of an emerging political phenomenon.

Political Research Quarterly

The Evolution of Committee Agenda Control in the Antebellum U.S. House of Representatives

John Baughman, Chris Den Hartog, Craig Goodman

Full text
Gatekeeping is viewed as an essential element of committee power in the U.S. Congress, but its emergence is not well understood. The House of Representatives moved to a system of standing committees by 1825. We investigated whether that move gave committees gatekeeping power prior to the Civil War or layered committees atop the earlier Jeffersonian system centering consequential legislative action on the floor. Using original data from the House Journal , we find evidence of institutional layering, with elements of gatekeeping – jurisdictional clarity, positive agenda setting, and negative agenda setting – developing unevenly and largely through changes in the behavior of members rather than formal reform of the House to empower its committees.

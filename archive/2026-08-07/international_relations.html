Revolutionizing Time, Reforging Social Orders: State-Building Success and the Enforcement of Symbolic Programs in Revolutionary France

Megan A. Stewart

Full text
How do state builders reshape society? While existing work shows they often rely on violence, education, elite co-optation, and institution building, this article underscores the importance of symbolic programs. When intensely enforced, symbolic programs reshape social orders by forging new social bonds and socializing individuals into state builders’ governing principles. I test this argument using subnational variation in the enforcement of the French Republican calendar—a symbolic program that politicized time, labor, and rest, rightfully regulated by the state, rather than sacred duties governed by the Catholic Church—and its association with labor conflicts. In departments where the calendar was more strongly enforced, it fostered political understandings of labor and rest, reduced coordination costs, and increased mobilization potential. Using original data and a difference-in-differences design, I find that more intense enforcement of the calendar is associated with a higher likelihood of labor conflict onset, demonstrating the long-term social effects of symbolic state-building programs.

Review of International Organizations

Peer-to-peer accountability and knowledge production in OECD DAC peer reviews

Alice Iannantuoni, Simone Dietrich, Bernhard Reinsberg

Full text
The OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC) relies on peer reviews to promote accountability in development cooperation. For peer review to function as an accountability mechanism, it must first satisfy a core requirement: peer review evaluations must accurately reflect donor practices. We examine whether DAC peer reviews interpret donor behavior on tied aid in ways that are consistent with observable aid-tying practices and shared DAC standards. Using a newly collected corpus of 143 DAC peer reviews from 1994 to 2020, we find a strong, systematic relationship between the reviewed donor’s aid-tying practices and the resulting content of the review documents. This linkage is driven primarily by explicit recommendations to untie aid and discussions of the standard, rather than by positive recognition of aid tying. Contrary to concerns that peer reviewers may intentionally soften evaluations and criticism to protect their own interests, we find no evidence that reviewers who themselves exhibit higher levels of aid tying produce evaluations that deviate from actual practice. Our findings demonstrate that DAC peer reviews generate credible peer evaluations that strengthen accountability dynamics in development cooperation. These insights contribute to literatures on peer reviews in global governance, the politics of international organizations, and foreign aid.

The power of locality: National staff enhance performance in international organizations

Steffen Eckhard, Sebastian Korb, Daniel Baumann

Full text
International organizations (IOs) rely on locally hired staff to translate global mandates into local action, yet the performance implications of localization remain contested. Addressing this gap, this study combines natural language processing (NLP) with a novel dataset of 5,230 project evaluations and staffing information from five United Nations organizations (2007–2022). Building on current research on local staff employment in IO projects together with insights from expert interviews, we test two dominant competing arguments: local capture versus local knowledge. Multilevel and fixed-effects analyses show that projects with higher shares of local staff perform significantly better, but only once local employees constitute at least 60% of the team. The results advance theory on IO effectiveness by shifting attention from local discretion to staff composition, demonstrating that locally embedded knowledge, when institutionally represented in staffing, constitutes a key organizational resource for adaptive implementation.

Democratizing from Within: British Elites and the Expansion of the Franchise

Chitralekha Basu, Carles Boix, Sonia Giurumescu, Paulo Serôdio

Full text
abstract: The authors develop a theory of democratization that relies on political and electoral calculations to explain the franchise choices of political actors in Great Britain. Given their location in the policy space, left-leaning (liberal) politicians are more likely to be supported by newly enfranchised voters, so they favor a broader franchise than conservative politicians do. The preferences of liberal politicians are conditional on the interests of both enfranchised and disenfranchised electors. As those interests become more heterogeneous, policymakers are more reluctant to expand the franchise because they may find it harder to attract new voters while keeping their current supporters. To evaluate this theory, the authors estimate the franchise preferences of British members of parliament (mps) based on their votes on franchise-related issues between 1830 and 1918. The authors then link these preferences to the mps’ personal and constituency characteristics. In line with their theory, the authors find that partisanship, declining inequality, and World War I were crucial factors in democratizing Britain.

Who Sets the Agenda? Diplomatic Experience and Small Power Influence in the United Nations

Sabrina B. Arias

Full text
abstract: Why are some small powers effective in setting the UN agenda, even in the face of large power opposition? The author argues that small states are more likely to be represented by experienced diplomats, who, over time, accumulate influence that can be deployed to shape the international organization’s agenda. To test these claims, the author assembled a data set of proposed agenda items and the tenure of all states’ ambassadors from 1946 to 2018 and conducted interviews with diplomats from forty-nine states. The author finds that smaller powers have more diplomatic experience than larger powers; that individuals with more diplomatic experience are more successful at agenda setting even when accounting for material power; and that random shocks to ambassadorial tenure, such as the death of an ambassador, have negative effects on diplomatic experience and effectiveness. These insights contribute to understanding the role of individuals and diplomacy in international relations and the ways in which small powers influence international politics.

The Myth of the Traditional Gender Gap? Evidence from Catholic Countries

Evelyne Brie, Dawn Langan Teele

Full text
abstract: In rich democracies today, women are less conservative than men. Yet for most of the twentieth century, politicians, pundits, and scholars believed that family life and religiosity tied women to conservative causes and parties. Recent scholarship has reexamined this traditional gender gap hypothesis but has done so in cases in which electorate-wide shifts to the left make it least likely that a conservative gender gap would emerge. This article scrutinizes a hard case, Quebec, a Catholic province in Canada that experienced a dramatic shift to the right after suffrage. Using Gallup polls and ecological inference techniques, the authors show that political geography drove gender preference gaps, with rural women voting more conservatively, and urban women more liberally, than men. In two other Catholic contexts, Austria and Argentina, they show that after suffrage women were not more conservative than men in general, undermining the traditional gender gap thesis in several crucial cases.

Peasants and Parliaments: Institutional Foundations of Agrarian Reform in Eighteenth-Century Eastern Europe

Brendan McElroy

Full text
abstract: An influential literature attributes Europe’s economic precocity to what might be termed “good” institutions, such as representative assemblies, which shielded property from governmental predation. This account fails to contend with the diversity of property rights in preindustrial Europe, many of which, like serfdom, were obstacles to growth. The author proposes a role for some—but not all—early representative assemblies in fostering growth by eliminating these “bad” property rights. An assembly’s capacity to extinguish property rights is a product of its decision-making efficiency, which in turn is associated with majority voting and centralized agenda control. The author tests these claims through a controlled comparison of progrowth agrarian reforms in the Russian Baltic province of Livonia and the Danish-ruled duchies of Schleswig and Holstein between 1795 and 1805, finding that the Livonian diet’s efficient organization was key to the successful transformation of serfdom. This analysis suggests that we must evaluate preindustrial representative institutions on the basis of their capacity to orchestrate complex exchanges of property rights and rents.

South-South Immigration and Redistribution Preferences: Evidence from Colombia

Josep Serrano-Serrat, Ignacio Jurado

Full text
abstract: Research on the effects of immigration on redistribution preferences mostly focuses on Western countries and neglects the Global South. The authors argue that immigration shocks may have different effects in South-South migration than they do in South-North contexts. On one hand, the effects should be more muted in the former due to minimal cultural differences and limited development of the welfare state. On the other hand, the impact might be larger due to high economic inequality and scarce resources. To investigate these differences, the authors follow a two-fold strategy. First, they exploit the 2015 Venezuelan immigration shock in Colombia using a difference-in-differences strategy, complemented by an instrumental variable. The authors find null-to-positive effects. Second, a survey experiment reveals that priming citizens on immigration has positive effects on citizens’ social spending preferences. The authors attribute this effect to deservingness considerations. These results challenge some prevailing theories and sug-gest that increased immigration does not necessarily reduce redistribution preferences in South-South contexts.

Journal of Peace Research

Attitudinal impacts of polarized protests: experimental evidence from abortion protests in Poland

Amalie Nilsen

Full text
Extensive literature suggests that protests can change citizens’ attitudes through several informational mechanisms. However, protests often occur around deeply polarized issues that are resistant to attitudinal change. In such contexts, do protests instead deepen political and affective divides? Leveraging evidence from an original, preregistered survey experiment conducted in Poland, this study examines how abortion protests influence citizens’ attitudes toward the abortion law, as well as their sentiments toward others holding different views on abortion. The findings indicate that while abortion protests do not significantly change respondents’ positions on the abortion issue itself, they increase in-group favoritism among anti-abortion sympathizers. No comparable effects are observed among pro-abortion supporters. These results highlight that the attitudinal impacts of protests extend beyond attitudes toward the protest issue itself, notably by strengthening positive in-group sentiments among status quo defenders. These insights are valuable for understanding counter-reactions to liberal protests and are especially relevant in the context of rising polarization and the ongoing illiberal backlash observed in many countries worldwide.

Diplomats in Camouflage: A Dataset of China’s Military-Diplomatic Engagements

Noel Foster, Zenobia T. Chan, Jackie S. H. Wong

Full text
Face-to-face diplomacy is central to international politics, yet existing research focuses largely on civilian leaders and diplomats. We introduce an original meeting-level dataset of China’s public military-diplomatic engagements from 2000 to 2023. Drawing on four authoritative Chinese-language party, state, and military outlets, the dataset records over 2,500 engagements involving senior Chinese military leaders and foreign counterparts. It classifies engagements as receptions in China, outbound visits, multilateral organization meetings, and defense and security fora, while preserving information on participants, timing, location, sources, and reported content. Official reporting frames these engagements primarily around cooperation and exchange. An illustrative analysis of outbound visits finds little evidence that China concentrates senior-level military outreach on U.S. allies. Outbound visits are instead positively associated with pre-existing security-specific and economic relationships with China. This dataset enables future research on how military diplomacy complements civilian diplomacy, signals strategic priorities, and shapes security cooperation, crisis management, and political influence.

Political Studies

The undemocratic face of greenwashing and the duty to counter-frame

Corrado Fumagalli

Full text
Normative research on greenwashing has largely focused on the environmental and economic consequences of false or misleading green claims. This article introduces a new perspective that sheds light on its democratic costs. First, I argue that by interfering with the transmission of a public political culture through deceptive claims, greenwashing constitutes a particularly problematic form of corporate capture of democratic rhetoric. I further contend that greenwashing should also be examined against ordinary patterns of language use, given their capacity to shape the range of communicative possibilities available. At the normative level, I then defend a duty to counter-frame: a duty of ordinary citizens to play their part in avoiding reinforcing the communicative conditions that facilitate corporate capture of public rhetoric. This duty aims to initiate processes of reciprocal reason-giving about responsible language use within everyday conversations.

Too burdensome to be acknowledged? How sexism is rendered invisible to women candidates through seven mechanisms

Clémence Deswert, Robin Devroe, Elise Storme, Emilie van Haute

Full text
Drawing on an inductive analysis of 21 interviews with women candidates at the 2024 elections in Belgium, our article questions how the perpetuation of sexism in politics is facilitated by its invisibilization, even among its victims. First, we show that despite an awareness of the prevalence of sexism in politics, women candidates often struggle to clearly identify its expressions. We show how the type of target (candidates’ profile), the type of sexism (benevolent vs hostile), and the context of its expression (online vs offline) affect the type of mechanisms women candidates turn to, but that it ultimately all leads to the same outcome: the invisibilization of sexism and its disconnect from candidates’ experience. We argue that the mechanisms uncovered can serve as an analytical grid in other contexts. Our findings call for a reconsideration of how sexism in politics is defined, understood, measured, and addressed by actors and institutions.

International Studies Quarterly

Mutually Hurting or Mutually Beneficial Stalemates? Alternative Logics Shaping Conflict Outcomes

Nicola S Williams

Full text
Scores of intrastate conflicts end without military victory or formal peace agreement, yet civil war scholarship lacks a mechanism to explain how these non-decisive outcomes emerge, recur, and endure. Existing “win, lose, or draw” rationalist bargaining frameworks treat settlements as the default outcome when neither party prevails militarily, assuming impasse reliably produces the ripe moment Zartman describes. Yet as conflict systems grow more complex, negotiated settlements continue to decline. This theory note introduces mutually beneficial stalemates—an equilibrium in which conflict parties calculate that maintaining the status quo yields higher returns than renewed fighting or settlements that risk commitment failures—as a mechanism to explain how non-decisive outcomes evolve and are sustained. Where Zartman’s mutually hurting stalemate describes a painful impasse generating pressure toward settlement, the mutually beneficial stalemate explains how parties distribute some benefits of peace—limited or no violence, selective cooperation, and hybrid governance arrangements—without the risks of formal resolution. I develop a logic of joint conflict avoidance that shows how parties protect existing gains and preserve organizations against the risks posed by both escalation and resolution. I extend the framework to multi-actor conflicts, where rebel proliferation and external intervention reinforce non-decisive equilibria.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

Risk mitigation versus gains maximisation – The two logics of smaller power hedging amid intensifying great power rivalry

Alexander Korolev

Full text
How can smaller powers use hedging effectively to navigate the intensifying great power rivalry? Why can some smaller powers hedge successfully between competing great powers while others fail and suffer serious harm? The burgeoning hedging literature is largely silent about which hedging patterns are likely to be more successful and why. This research contributes to the field by discerning and specifying two distinct modes of hedging – “risk mitigation” and “gains maximisation” – and their implications for hedging outcomes under intensifying great power rivalry. It argues that while these two modes might seem similar, and the extant literature has so far failed to separate them, primarily conceiving hedging as risk minimisation, they have different operational logics and are likely to have different consequences for the hedging states. It probes the two concepts using cases of smaller power hedging in Southeast Asia (Malaysia and Vietnam), exposed to the China–US rivalry, and in the post-Soviet space (Georgia and Ukraine), exposed to the Russia–North Atlantic Treaty Organization rivalry. It further argues that as great power rivalry intensifies, abandoning the gains maximisation and consolidating the risk-mitigation logic of hedging can help mitigate threats posed by great powers and minimise the chances of hedging failure.

Party organisation and the strategic use of federal narratives in electoral campaigns: Evidence from Assam, India

Pratyush Paras Sarma

Full text
This article examines how party organisation shapes the construction of federal narratives during electoral competition, drawing on fieldwork from the 2024 parliamentary elections in Assam, India. Using interviews, campaign speeches, and party materials, it compares four parties across two constituencies, Dhubri and Guwahati: the Indian National Congress, the Bharatiya Janata Party, the Asom Gana Parishad, and the All-India United Democratic Front. The findings show that parties construct representations of their relationship with the Centre within campaign discourse. These vary systematically with organisational form and constituency context, particularly across settings shaped by citizenship anxieties and urban governance concerns. The article argues that federalism functions as a conditional rhetorical resource, highlighting how party communication in multi-level systems is shaped by organisational capacity and local political conditions.

Let Them Eat Pie: Addressing Sample Selection in Multiparty Elections

Ali Kagalwala, Thiago M. Q. Moreira, Guy D. Whitten, Yongzhi Xu

Full text
Elections are central to the study of politics. When studying parties’ vote shares across districts, scholars are encouraged to use compositional-outcome models in order to test their theories about what factors shape the dynamics of electoral support. Existing compositional modeling approaches incorrectly deal with scenarios in which not all parties compete in every electoral district. Because unobserved factors that affect a party’s decisions to contest a district are likely correlated with its performance in districts where the party fielded candidates, failing to account for partial contestation is likely to result in sample selection bias. Addressing sample selection in a compositional setting is challenging because the outcomes are in log-ratio form, and thus the errors often deviate from normality. To deal with these issues, we introduce a novel maximum likelihood approach which accounts for this type of sample selection and demonstrate through simulations that our method outperforms commonly used solutions, including the conventional Heckman correction. We illustrate the utility of our approach by analyzing the 2017 and 2019 U.K. parliamentary elections in English constituencies.

The Power of Prognosis: Improving Covariate Balance Tests with Outcome Information

Clara Bicalho, Adam Bouyamourn, Thad Dunning

Full text
Scholars frequently use covariate balance tests to test the validity of natural experiments and related designs. Unfortunately, when measured covariates are unrelated to potential outcomes, balance is uninformative about key identification conditions. We show that balance tests can then lead to erroneous conclusions. To build stronger tests, researchers should identify covariates that are jointly predictive of potential outcomes; formally measure and report covariate prognosis; and prioritize the most individually informative variables in tests. Building on prior research on “prognostic scores,” we develop bootstrap balance tests that upweight covariates associated with the outcome. We adapt this approach for regression-discontinuity designs and use simulations to compare weighting methods based on linear regression and more flexible methods, including machine learning. The results show how prognosis weighting can avoid both false negatives and false positives. To illustrate key points, we study empirical examples from a sample of published studies, including an important debate over close elections.

Comparative Political Studies

Concessions and Protest Demobilization

Sasha de Vogel, Jessica S. Sun

Full text
How and why do concessions, or promises to implement reform in the future, affect protest mobilization in autocracies? Concessions are a routine response to protest and generate a puzzling empirical regularity: Although autocrats often renege once protest abates, concessions can produce demobilization. We develop a model showing that concessions demobilize by exploiting heterogeneity among protesters. A subgroup of moderates, who derive symbolic value from the autocrat’s promise to reform, and a subgroup of radicals, who value only implemented reforms, must coordinate to sustain protest after a concession. Concessions undermine the between-group coordination necessary for protests large enough to prevent reneging. Some moderates demobilize in response to promised concessions, and in turn, declining turnout demobilizes radicals. Even autocrats in highly repressive regimes may concede because concessions and repression demobilize through distinct mechanisms. However, concessions lose efficacy as moderates lose trust in the autocrat. Morocco’s Feb20 movement illustrates the theory.

British Journal of Political Science

Education for Mobilization: The Effects of Knowledge about Labor History on Union Support

Roxanne Rahnama, Jonne Kamphorst

Full text
The US working class is less organized along class lines than in other advanced democracies. We propose a new explanation: the absence of education on historical class struggle and labor organizing weakens support for the contemporary labor movement. To test this, we conduct two survey experiments exposing respondents to educational videos about the history of an American union and present-day union drives. Our surveys focus on non-college-educated whites in the US South, where union membership is low and racial conflict has historically constrained class-based solidarity. We find that exposure to labor history increases enthusiasm for unions and cross-racial class coalitions. These effects appear to be driven by greater political efficacy, better understanding of unions’ material benefits, linked fate with the working class, and increased allyship with marginalized groups. Our findings suggest that labor history education can shift political attitudes and foster greater class-based mobilization, even in contexts historically resistant to such organizing.

Can Authoritarian Electoral Propaganda Compete with the Opposition? Experimental Evidence from Russia

Michael Rochlitz, Koen Schoors, Nikita Zakharov

Full text
We examine the effectiveness of electoral propaganda in an autocracy, using Russia as a unique setting. Exploiting the unexpected release of a pro-regime propaganda video about Vladimir Putin shortly before the presidential election, we conducted a field experiment by randomly promoting the video to voters via a telephone survey and measuring self-reported voting behaviour after the election. Our results show that the pro-regime treatment successfully mobilized offline media consumers to vote for Putin, but failed to persuade online media users, and even backfired among respondents dissatisfied with the regime. Using electoral statistics for all major Russian cities and instrumenting public interest in the propaganda video with rainfall, we find a positive, albeit moderate, causal effect of the video on Putin’s vote share. As an alternative treatment, we used a viral anti-corruption video by opposition leader Alexei Navalny, which consistently undermined regime support among both offline and online media consumers.

Perspectives on Politics

Trump, the International Criminal Court, and the Future of Global Impunity

Daniel Krcmaric

Full text
The US has traditionally played a key role in fighting global impunity for atrocity crimes, but the policies of Donald Trump’s administration suggest that a dramatic shift in American priorities may be underway. This article examines America’s commitment to addressing impunity in the context of US relations with the International Criminal Court (ICC). The Trump presidency, I argue, showcases a curious mix of continuity and change in America’s relationship with one of the world’s most prominent international institutions. In particular, Trump’s general strategic approach to the ICC represents continuity with past American presidents, but the brazenness of the specific tactics Trump has used to oppose the ICC represents a notable change. The reverberations of recent American attacks on the ICC raise implications for the future of global impunity that will be felt in three distinct areas: in the US–ICC relationship, among ICC adversaries, and among ICC allies.

The Autonomy Paradox: Artificial Intelligence and the Foundations of Political Behavior

Christopher J. Anderson, Elena Pro

Full text
Decades of research in political behavior show that citizens struggle with information, rely on cognitive shortcuts, and face barriers to participation. They are increasingly using AI tools to make sense of political information and express views, while interacting with platform curation systems that shape how views circulate through democratic politics. Although these tools appear suited to address the limits of democratic citizenship, they do more than transmit content. They help interpret information, articulate views, and affect what is prioritized, seen, and circulated. In doing so, these systems shape the formation of political judgments and citizens’ capacity to translate them into action. As a result, they can reduce barriers to participation while weakening the independence of political judgment, or they can support reflective preference formation while constraining citizens’ ability to act on those preferences effectively. We call this tension the autonomy paradox. To make sense of it, we focus on how citizens engage with AI systems in ways that affect belief formation and political action. We develop a framework and research agenda for identifying when AI supports autonomous citizenship, when it substitutes for citizens’ judgment or agency, and why the same systems produce different outcomes across political tasks depending on how citizens use them.

Good Governance as Responsible Sovereignty: Francis Deng and the Conflict–Development Nexus

Connor O’Brien

Full text
The South Sudanese UN official, diplomat, and scholar Francis Deng is widely recognized for his intellectual contributions to the Responsibility to Protect (R2P) principle, most notably through his 1996 coauthored publication, Sovereignty as Responsibility: Conflict Management in Africa. However, existing scholarship has paid limited attention to how Deng conceptualized the subject of responsible sovereignty: good governance. Through a situated intellectual history, this article reconstructs Deng’s conception of good governance, exploring its reception before and beyond R2P. I contend that Deng reformulated good governance as primarily concerning conflict management. Deng’s thought and advocacy in the late 1990s then shaped the expansion of the good governance agenda, the rise of human security, and the synchronization of humanitarian and development activities through efforts to bridge the so-called relief–development gap. Hence, I portray Deng as a novel theorist and practitioner of the conflict–development nexus, who sought to enlist the international financial institutions for conflict management, prevention, and resolution prerogatives. In so doing, this article destabilizes existing grand narratives of the post–Cold War international order that hinge on end of history triumphalism and neoliberal ideological diffusion.

European Journal of Political Research

Online gendered violence and visibility in electoral autocracies: Evidence from multi-level elections in Türkiye

Jungmin Han, Müge Finkel, Steven Finkel, Firat Duruşan, Erdem Yörük

Full text
Online platforms have become indispensable tools for political candidates to gain visibility, particularly for female candidates in electoral autocracies who often lack access to traditional networks, party hierarchies, and media platforms. We argue that online visibility, while a resource for female candidates, disproportionately exposes them to online violence that functions as a mechanism of political control, reinforcing patriarchal exclusion from the political sphere. Our analysis draws on more than three million tweets directed at 1,834 candidates across the 2023 parliamentary and 2024 local election campaigns in Türkiye, providing a rare opportunity to observe how these dynamics unfold at both the national and subnational levels. We find that while online visibility increases abuse for all candidates, the effect is markedly stronger for female candidates, who are disproportionately targeted across diverse forms of violence, including profanity, demeaning and ridiculing language, physical threats, and ethnic and religious hate speech. Moreover, our results suggest that in local elections, where candidates rely more heavily on personalized campaigns, increases in visibility are more strongly associated with female candidates’ exposure to online violence.

Losing elections, losing trust? A quasi-experimental investigation of the winner-loser gap on political trust

Nicholas Charron, Jana Schwenk

Full text
Voters’ experiences of winning or losing elections significantly influence their attitudes, particularly satisfaction with democracy, while other outcomes such as trust are less studied. Additionally, studies often rely on observational data, limiting causal conclusions about effects. We make three key contributions to this literature. First, we use a quasi-experimental design based on the implementation of the European Quality of Government Index Survey during elections across several European countries and regions to demonstrate causal effects of elections on trust. Second, we introduce a novel theoretical argument that voters of populist parties are especially sensitive to electoral outcomes. Third, we explore three ways to define ‘winners’ and ‘losers’: government/opposition status, vote margin changes, and results relative to recent polling. Our findings show that elections generally boost trust, with a pronounced winner-loser gap driven by winners, particularly when measured as entering government. Additionally, populist party voters exhibit greater sensitivity to these effects.

Preferences or principles? The ‘noncompliance penalty’ and the limits of public support for the rule of law

Taylor Kinsley Chewning, Amanda Driscoll, Martín Gandur, Jay N. Krehbiel, Michael J. Nelson

Full text
When will individuals support government noncompliance with the written law? A bedrock principle of the rule of law is that politicians who fail to comply with written law will face political consequences. Drawing on survey experiments fielded in two consolidated democracies, we introduce the noncompliance penalty , the loss of public support for a government action that contravenes the law. The results show that citizens do penalize governmental noncompliance with the law. However, we find that this penalty decreases if government noncompliance advances the policy interests of favored protest groups, and mixed evidence that this penalty increases with professed support for the rule of law. Perhaps surprisingly, our results suggest that citizens with the strongest commitment to the rule of law can become the most accepting of noncompliance if it advances their policy goals. These findings have important implications for understanding whether the public will punish politicians who disregard the formal letter of the law.

Political Science Research and Methods

How patronage communicates: information campaigns and government responsiveness in China

Haemin Jee, Kaiping Chen

Full text
How do social relationships among government officials shape information flows within bureaucratic hierarchies? Classic principal–agent theories emphasize information asymmetries as a source of inefficiency, yet we know far less about the actual nature of communication within bureaucracies. We argue that patron–client ties embedded within formal hierarchies play a central role in structuring internal information flows. Lower-level officials with patronage ties to higher-level leaders have incentives to conduct informal information campaigns that signal diligence and competence as a way to compete for political favors. Using newly assembled data from China—including a rare corpus of internal emails between city-level patrons and county-level clients, as well as original datasets on county governments’ public responses to citizen complaints—we find that client counties communicate more frequently with higher-level officials, more often highlight their diligence in addressing complaints, and more actively defend themselves against accusations. However, client and non-client counties perform similarly in how they respond publicly to citizen complaints. These findings reveal how patronage relations can increase upward information flows without impacting public-facing performance. More broadly, these results suggest that communication in nondemocratic settings may serve to strengthen informal political ties.

Policymaking activities of caretaker governments in Western Europe

Mingyi Zhang

Full text
In parliamentary democracies, coalition government formation often takes a certain amount of time, during which the outgoing incumbent government remains in office as the “caretaker” until the new government can be formed. While the literature has reached a consensus that caretaker governments retain the policy status quo, empirical evidence remains limited. This article uses original data on government bills from 238 cabinets in 11 Western European countries as descriptive evidence to challenge this long-standing assumption. I propose a timeframe-based, political definition of the caretaking period within the coalition lifecycle and discuss the normative issues with its presence. The over-generalization of the prevalent policy status quo assumption raises critical issues concerning practical caretaking rules, as well as coalition governance and bargaining.

Racialization of the democratic peace in foundational embedding models

Juho Kim

Full text
One of the most prominent theories in modern international relations is the democratic peace theory, which posits that democracies tend not to engage in armed conflicts with fellow democracies. Recently, Rathbun, Parker, and Pomeroy examined this effect at a microstructural level, performing surveys, computational studies, and meta-analysis. Of particular originality in their work was embedding analysis, which I review and offer a systematic critique of. Then, I propose a new and improved methodology that addresses its issues while leveraging foundational embedding models. My replication results agree with their findings that the democratic peace effect is racialized, that is, it is associated with racial and ethnocentric motivations. The findings offer a timely guide in conducting embedding analysis in political science research.

How moral values underpin and affect attitudes on ethnic discrimination

Ida Bruun Nørregaard

Full text
When political elites and activists promote anti‐discrimination policies, they often frame discrimination as violating liberal moral values such as fairness, care for vulnerable groups, and equality. However, this approach may fail to resonate with conservative voters and could even alienate them. Through two separate studies, this article uses a multi‐method approach to examine the moral values held by supporters and opponents of discrimination and test how conservative and liberal value appeals influence acceptance. The findings reveal that values like loyalty, care for the in‐group, authority, and sanctity appear in acceptors' justifications and that framing discrimination as a violation of these values, associated with conservatism, reduces acceptance across the political spectrum. Contrastingly, appealing to values associated with liberalism backfires and increases Republicans' acceptance. While liberal values were central in shifting social norms towards anti‐discrimination, appealing to conservative values may be more effective in reducing contemporary acceptance of discrimination.

When propaganda resonates

Xiaoxiao Shen

Full text
Existing scholarship on propaganda in authoritarian regimes centers on whether it persuades or coerces. Within the persuasion camp, researchers have examined how factors such as message source, content, credibility, and audience demographics shape propaganda effectiveness. This study shifts attention to psychological needs. Drawing on evidence from China, it integrates multi‐round survey experiments, a virtual lab‐in‐the‐field experiment using a mobile news app developed by the author, text analysis of state media, and field interviews. The results show that propaganda appealing to the need to belong, the need to maintain self‐esteem, or the need to express core values is most effective among individuals for whom the corresponding need is particularly strong—but generalized propaganda, even when psychologically engaging, is not. The findings suggest that propaganda is most persuasive when targeted to stable psychological orientations rather than designed for broad consumption. These findings have implications for understanding how authoritarian regimes sustain public support: propaganda effectiveness depends not only on content or reach, but also on how closely its psychological appeals correspond to the stable motivational orientations of its audience. The shift in authoritarian communication strategies toward psychologically targeted appeals makes the mechanisms identified here increasingly relevant to understanding contemporary propaganda.

Social dominance orientation

Felicia Pratto

Full text
Social dominance orientation (SDO) refers to how much a person generally opposes group‐based equality and supports group‐based dominance. SDO is measured as an individual difference and pertains very broadly to people's socio‐political attitudes and perceptions, to endorsement or rejection of political parties and candidates, and to behaviors that enforce organizational hierarchies. This paper reviews empirical evidence published from 2010 to 2024 concerning social dominance orientation, first presenting evidence of its stability over time and measurement integrity, next showing that, across numerous cultures and languages and ways of measuring group prejudice and stereotypes, SDO correlates with prejudice favoring people in dominant groups rather than people in subordinate groups, and that this effect is stronger within dominant groups than subordinate groups. Similarly, we review evidence that people higher on SDO are more anti‐social and tend to take hierarchy‐enhancing rather than hierarchy‐attenuating roles. We then delve into the psychology of SDO and summarize the evidence for the relation of SDO and closed cognitive styles, low scores on humility‐honesty, and low concern for other people rather than the self. Using existing adult and child development studies, we outline two processes by which higher SDO may develop: one based on learning to categorize and to rank categories as superior/inferior, and one based on parents' emphasis on obtaining high status to gain approval. We conclude by outlining the next questions that might be pursued.

Affective regimes and ontological security: When empathy threatens the state

Naomi Head

Full text
The ontological security of states may be threatened by the politics of empathy. Empathy—as a contested affective‐political field—has a capacity to both consolidate and unravel dominant understandings of identity, security, political subjectivity, and self‐other relations. This article connects a politics of empathy to patterns of state violence and resistance in situations of conflict and authoritarian politics, arguing that the distribution of feeling that underpins a politics of empathy is structured by positionality, power, and social context. The article argues that states construct “affective regimes” through which they seek to manage the legitimate subjects of empathy in order to maintain their ontological security, and focuses on empirical testimony from the Syrian uprising in 2011 and the Assad regime's strategies of violence to explore these affective dynamics. It reveals ways in which the state's efforts are always resisted and transgressed by citizens, generating counter‐narratives of security‐identity and affective relations. A politics of empathy offers analytical tools for understanding the construction and effectiveness of states' affective regimes to maintain ontological security as well as being normatively essential to the task of challenging forms of state power, political violence, and generating alternative imaginings of ontological security and subjectivity.

Research & Politics

From the courtroom to the public: Judicial influence on People’s attitudes toward same-sex marriage in Japan

Robert Nordström, Sou Shinomoto, Antonio Benasaglio Berlucchi

Full text
Can judicial action change public attitudes? While most empirical studies on the societal impact of court rulings have focused on the United States, particularly its Supreme Court, the influence of courts in other contexts remains largely unexplored. This study examines Japan—the only G7 country that does not legally recognize same-sex marriage—and explores how judicial rulings on same-sex marriage influence public attitudes. Using data from an original survey incorporating natural and experimental treatments, the analysis finds that while a court decision has no significant effect in a natural environment, experimental exposure to a reminder of the ruling can shift respondents’ views. The findings suggest that judicial action on same-sex marriage may have limited but measurable impacts on public attitudes, even in unconventional settings, and therefore contribute to discussions of external validity regarding the societal influence of courts.

European Political Science Review

How considered interests evolve in deliberation: evidence from Swiss citizens’ juries

Alexander Matthias Geisler

Full text
Citizens increasingly demand involvement in policymaking, driving the rise of participatory processes like deliberative minipublics. This article introduces a novel longitudinal survey measure to determine the extent to which citizens are more considerate of others’ interests as a result of their participation in such forums. The study explores how participants’ considered interests vary across demographics and process-related factors measured throughout five Swiss minipublics (2019–2023) on different topics and validated using word-embedding techniques on their transcripts. While results show that minipublic participants considered interests different from their own, individuals belonging to less advantaged groups did not consistently show greater consideration of others’ interests. Interest inclusiveness was higher among older individuals, low-income earners and participants in minipublics linked to referendums. This article explains how and why participants in minipublics consider interests beyond their own, which is essential for designing more inclusive participatory processes that engage people from diverse backgrounds in increasingly polarized societies.

Who cares about politics under authoritarian rule? Evidence from China

Yida Zhai

Full text
Departing from mainstream research on political interest in democratic contexts, this study investigates its evolution in an authoritarian setting. To disentangle temporal dynamics, it applies the age–period–cohort interaction model to repeated cross-sectional data from five waves of nationally representative surveys conducted in China. The results demonstrate distinct effects across age, period, and cohort dimensions. Age effects indicate that younger individuals consistently exhibit lower levels of political interest than older adults. Period effects reveal a downward trend in political interest among the Chinese public between 2002 and 2019. Importantly, cohort effects suggest that, contrary to the linear evolutionary predictions of modernization theory, individuals who underwent political socialization during critical historical periods—such as the Great Famine and its aftermath (1959–1963) and the late Cultural Revolution (1974–1978)—as well as those born in the late 1970s and throughout the 1980s, exhibit heightened political interest. These findings contribute to theoretical reflections on the evolution of political interest.

Government and Opposition

When Do Shadow Ministers Become ‘Realities’? Evidence from the UK, 1964–2024

Nicholas Allen, John Bartle

Full text
A characteristic feature of Westminster-style democracy is the convention that the opposition frontbench forms the basis of the new executive following changes in the party of government. Yet we know little about how this convention operates when it comes to individual appointments. This paper examines how frequently opposition frontbenchers have become ministers in the UK, the archetypal Westminster system. Across seven incoming governments, we find that around four-fifths of opposition frontbenchers were appointed to ministerial office, and roughly half retained responsibility for the same policy portfolio they had shadowed. At the government level, prime ministers’ idiosyncratic preferences affected overall levels of continuity, but these choices were constrained by electoral and party factors. At the individual level, we find no evidence that prime ministers retained their supporters in greater numbers, but party seniority, prior ministerial experience and time served in an opposition post were closely associated with portfolio continuity.

Opposition Distinctiveness in Parliaments: A Longitudinal Analysis

Tom Louwerse, Rick van Well

Full text
There are concerns about the distinctiveness of opposition party behaviour in parliaments, but the literature offers mixed findings. This article analyses how distinctive the opposition is from the government in its voting patterns and speech-making in four parliamentary democracies (Canada, Denmark, the Netherlands, United Kingdom [UK]) over a long time span (1945–2020). Our results show a diversity of trends in opposition distinctiveness. Overall, the distinctiveness in terms of parliamentary voting behaviour increased up to the 1980s, and slightly decreased or remained stable thereafter in most countries. Opposition sentiment in speech-making follows a different pattern, particularly in the UK and Canada, where the increasingly positive sentiment of governing party MPs and the increasingly negative sentiment of opposition parties has led to significant divergence over time. These trends in opposition distinctiveness in voting and speech-making are shown to be related most strongly to opposition parties’ prior governing experience, in particular in Denmark and the Netherlands.

Party membership motivations and political activism in a polarized new democracy: Evidence from South Korea

Gi-Woo Roh, Jungsub Shin

Full text
This study investigates party membership motivations in South Korea, a highly polarized new democracy, and how they distinctively shape intra-party and campaign participation. Using survey data from 1,410 Democratic and People Power Party members, we evaluate the General Incentives Model alongside negative partisan motivation. Results show that while altruism is the primary reason for joining, higher affective polarization significantly intensifies negative partisan motivation, making it a key membership driver, particularly among conservatives. Crucially, engagement mechanisms are not monolithic: selective benefits predict both forms of participation, whereas collective policy motives specifically foster campaigning. Conversely, social norms motivation deters visible campaigning due to polarization-induced cross-pressures. Furthermore, negative partisan motivation reduces routine intra-party engagement without increasing campaign involvement. Ultimately, membership surges driven by out-group hostility produce “detached members,” indicating that expanding party rolls in polarized contexts does not automatically deepen democratic participation or organizational capacity.

Ethno-regionalist parties beyond Western Europe: Kurdish parties in Turkey

Şenol Arslantaş, Düzgün Arslantaş

Full text
While there is an extensive literature on ethno-regionalist parties (ERPs) in Western Europe, Kurdish ERPs in Turkey remain undertheorized. Focusing on party ideology, organizational structure, and electoral strategy, this article advances three findings. First, ideologically, Kurdish ERPs consistently articulate a left-leaning program centered on labor and democratization, while increasingly integrating post-materialist tenets such as feminism and environmentalism. Second, organizationally, they function as movement parties with a highly resilient structure. Third, strategically, they expand beyond their ethnic stronghold through broad leftist alliances and a “Turkification” ( Türkiyelileşme ) process, becoming pivotal national actors. These findings underscore the need to account for the interaction between ethno-regional mobilization, political-institutional constraints, and party-movement linkages in understanding ERPs in the European periphery.

Ambition and access: Linking regional opportunity structures and career-ambitious youth party members

Clint Claessen, Stefanie Bailer, Tamaki Ohmura

Full text
Youth party members are primarily driven by intrinsic moral and social motives, yet ambitions for political and professional careers vary considerably across individuals. Building on findings linking parties' national opportunity structures to having an increase in career-ambitious youth wing members, this research note examines opportunity structures at the regional level. Using a self-collected survey of youth party members in Germany (2017–2018, n = 1216), we show that career-ambitious members are particularly frequent in youth parties that are organizationally strong with numerous local and (sub)regional branches where ambitious youth wing members can make a career. Therefore, this research note provides a key insight into the motivations of young people, and especially those pursuing a political career, for joining youth parties and speaks to a broader literature concerned with political ambition, party organization, and careers.

Political Research Quarterly

I or We? Linked Fate, Self-Interest, and African American Opinion Toward Reparations

Tatishe M. Nteta, Jesse H. Rhodes, Bel Corder, Seth K. Goldman, Yuen J. Huo, Efrén Pérez, Linda R. Tropp

Full text
Recent years have witnessed renewed calls for reparations to advance racial equality in the United States. In response, a number of localities and institutions of higher education have provided reparations to the descendants of slaves. However, the policy remains controversial as evidenced by a clear racial divide characterized by overwhelmingly White opposition to reparations and strong majorities of African Americans supporting the provision of cash payments to the descendants of slaves. While much attention has been paid to empirical explorations of White opinion, in this paper we investigate the determinants of African American opinion, with a particular focus on the influence of measures of economic self-interest and linked fate in structuring African American opinion. Using data from two large national surveys of Americans, we find consistent evidence that linked fate is a strong and consistent predictor of African American support for reparations, while self-interest is a weaker and less consistent predictor, controlling for a wide array of political, socioeconomic, and attitudinal factors. These findings are reinforced by quantitative and qualitative content analysis of open-ended responses by African Americans to the question why they support reparations. Our results suggest that African Americans’ views on reparations are more strongly associated with the well-being of the African American community and less to do with the individual economic benefits of the policy.

Belief and Betrayal: Institutional Trust in the Shadow of Religious Violence

Tang Xi, Chang Li, Kalsoom Paracha, Shaukat Hussain Bhatti

Full text
Religious violence erodes the moral and social fabric that binds communities, striking at the very heart of societal cohesion. As fear and intolerance spread, the foundations of institutional trust weaken, leaving nations vulnerable to deep-seated divisions and governance challenges. Present investigation analyzes the asymmetric impact of religious violence on institutional trust in 10 selected emerging countries (India, Indonesia, Pakistan, Nigeria, Egypt, Brazil, Turkey, South Africa, Mexico, and the Philippines). Departing from conventional analyses that rely on standard econometric techniques and often overlook regional heterogeneity, the present investigation uses the cutting-edge Quantile-on-Quantile method. This approach facilitates a nuanced examination of cross-country variation, enhances analytical precision, and reveals intricate, nation-specific behavioral dynamics. The results exhibit a strong negative association between religious violence and institutional trust in most sample countries. In contrast, South Africa and Mexico exhibit a mixed association. These findings underscore notable variations across quantiles, highlighting the asymmetric nature of the relationship between the variables. They emphasize that policymakers must undertake thorough assessments and formulate strategies tailored to each country’s specific social and regional contexts, ensuring that the multifaceted and sensitive link between religious violence and institutional trust is addressed effectively and in a locally responsive manner.

The Leadership Premium: Committee Leadership Status and Political Donations to Incumbent Legislators in South Korea

Sinjae Kang, Kyungrim Chung

Full text
This study investigates the effect of committee leadership positions on political fundraising in the South Korean National Assembly from 2004 to 2019. Drawing upon theories of legislative power and campaign finance, we examine whether acquiring standing committee chairmanships significantly increases political contributions received by legislators, even within Korea's highly restrictive regulatory regime that prohibits corporate donations. Utilizing OLS regression and difference‐in‐differences with Panel Matching methodology, we find compelling evidence of a substantial leadership premium. OLS estimates reveal that committee chairs consistently outraise non‐chairs by 16%–21%; Panel Matching estimates confirm an immediate increase of approximately 1.15 log‐points (a 216% increase) following chair appointment. Event study analysis validates our identification strategy by demonstrating parallel pre‐trends and sharp treatment effects at the moment of leadership transition, with effects dissipating after chairs leave their positions. Split‐sample OLS estimates reveal a cross‐over pattern: the premium is significant for Targeted and Mixed committees in the first half of the legislative term but shifts to Universal committees in the second half, suggesting that the relevant dimension of heterogeneity may be whether a committee holds jurisdictional authority over concentrated regulatory or budgetary stakes. These findings demonstrate that the link between institutional power and political money persists even under highly restrictive campaign finance regimes—reflecting structural features of legislative organization that transcend specific regulatory designs. The individual‐as‐proxy mechanism we identify, whereby organized interest groups circumvent the corporate donation ban through coordinated individual‐level contributions, suggests that regulation reshapes the channels of strategic giving without eliminating its underlying demand.

Differences in EU citizen voter registration: Evidence from French local elections

David Gouard, Meredith Winn Lombard

Full text
The 1992 Maastricht Treaty guarantees European Union citizens the right to vote in local and European elections held in their country of residence. Yet, few mobile European Union citizens actually exercise this right. In France, which hosts the second-largest number of EU immigrants, only 26.2% of this population were registered to vote for the 2020 local elections. This article uses traditional explanations of immigrant electoral participation to understand the low electoral participation of foreign EU citizens. We combine census, electoral and local data to examine the determinants of registration rates for the nine largest EU nationalities in France. Our findings suggest that existing theories of resistance, transferability and exposure account for differences in electoral registration, while also suggesting that municipality size is an important factor.

Representation of environmental issues: Is there a link between campaign and legislative behaviour in the European Parliament?

Clara Faulí Molas

Full text
This article assesses the link between Members of the European Parliament's campaign salience and legislative behaviour, focusing on environmental issues. Environmental issues were discussed by several MEP candidates during the 2019 campaign and they were important for voters. Nonetheless, it is unclear whether MEPs were consistent with their campaign commitments in their parliamentary votes. MEPs’ environmental salience is computed by coding 50,393 campaign tweets and their legislative behaviour is assessed by examining roll-call votes on amendments related to emissions reduction. Results indicate that MEPs who focused more on environmental issues during the campaign cast more pro-environmental votes. This indicates that the European Parliament can exercise its representative role in domains relevant for EU policy.

Reproductive Rights, Reframed: Federalism and Vote Choice in the 2024 Election

Spencer Goidel, Joseph A. Aistrup, John C. Morris, Martin K. Mayer, R. Bruce Anderson, Robert C. Kenter

Full text
The overturning of Roe v. Wade sent shockwaves through the American political system. Running on women’s reproductive health care rights, Democrats vastly outperformed expectations in the 2022 midterm elections. The 2024 US presidential election provided a test of whether broad public support for reproductive health care access would continue to translate into electoral gains for Democrats. Using a novel set of survey questions, this article examines attitudes toward abortion and the abortion pill (i.e., mifepristone) as well as preferences over whether policy should be decided federally or by individual states (i.e., locus of authority). Our findings provide partial support for the expectation that locus-of-authority preferences shape vote choice. Whereas support for a constitutional protection for abortion remained strongly associated with voting against Trump regardless of locus-of-authority preferences, the pattern for mifepristone access more closely matched our expectations. Among voters who supported mifepristone access, those who were comfortable leaving the issue to the states were significantly more likely to support Donald Trump. His emphasis on state control may have functioned as strategic ambiguity, especially on a less-polarized reproductive health issue such as mifepristone access.

Teaching Election Inversions in the Electoral College: Two Simple Models

Ruoxi Li, Rebecca Simpson

Full text
The Electoral College is an important concept in many undergraduate American politics courses. Although students often understand the basics of the “House-seats-plus-two” rule and the “winner-take-all” system, many struggle to understand election inversions, in which the winner of the popular vote loses the Electoral College vote and therefore loses the presidency. This article presents two models using only foundational arithmetic to explain election inversions, including an example illustrating how a Republican candidate could also win the popular vote but lose the Electoral College vote. A survey of student-learning experiences showed positive results: students found the models accessible and helpful, and they reported greater confidence in the integrity of US presidential elections and in American democracy after working with the materials. These models can be incorporated into a range of American politics courses to help even first-year undergraduate students make sense of the US presidential election system.

Democracy for Whom? Herrenvolk Democracy and the American Founding

Jessala A. Grijalva

Full text
As the United States marks the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence amid democratic erosion, this article asks why 250 years of democratic development have not produced a multiracial democracy. It argues that the United States is a herrenvolk democracy—a regime in which democracy coexists with racialized limits on political membership and governing power. By examining colonial law, the Declaration, the Constitution, and the Naturalization Act of 1790, I show how the founding joined popular sovereignty to racial exclusion in ways that continue to shape struggles over belonging, representation, and coercive power. The legacy of 1776 includes both the Declaration’s democratic promise and a durable model for how democracy can coexist with exclusion.

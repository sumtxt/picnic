Migration information campaigns as bureaucratic performances: governing through visibility

Ludmila Bogdan

Full text
Drawing on a mixed-methods analysis of 97 migration information campaigns implemented across Europe between 2019 and 2026 and more than thirty interviews with migration governance practitioners, policymakers, and international organization staff, this article reconceptualizes migration information campaigns as bureaucratic performances. Rather than informing migrants, campaigns operate as visible governance outputs through which states, international organizations, and implementing agencies stage action, secure legitimacy, and reproduce migration governance systems. The analysis shows that migration information campaigns have become routinized instruments of migration governance whose design is structured around risk framing and strategic ambiguity, and whose primary audiences are often institutional rather than migrant populations. Across governance contexts, campaigns perform distinct functions: signaling cooperation in countries of origin, demonstrating compliance in transit settings, and projecting responsiveness in destination countries. The article argues that migration information campaigns persist not because they influence migration decisions, but because they are institutionally useful. Their value lies in producing visibility, legitimacy, and auditable evidence of action in contexts where governing migration outcomes is difficult. In doing so, the article challenges dominant evaluative approaches in migration studies by showing that policy instruments may endure even when they are widely acknowledged to have uncertain or limited behavioural effects. Migration information campaigns, it concludes, are not failed interventions; they are successful performances of governance.

International Migration Review

The Impact of National Pride and Migrant Occupational Status on Attitudes Toward Settlement-Oriented Emigration: Evidence From a Survey Experiment in China

Xiang Lu, Bingrong Zeng

Full text
While migration studies have examined host-society attitudes toward immigrants, home-country views of emigrants remain underexplored. We address this gap using an online survey experiment in China that varies (i) the salience of national pride and (ii) the prospective emigrant's occupational status. Making national pride salient lowers support for — and increases negative emotional reactions toward — settlement-oriented emigration, especially for lower- and mid-skill profiles; effects for the high-status profile are smaller and statistically uncertain. Net of the prime, attitudes are also more negative toward higher-status profiles than toward the low-skill profile. We interpret these patterns within a social identity framework, drawing on Social Identity Theory, the “brain drain” literature, and Hirschman's exit–voice–loyalty model, while emphasizing a mechanism of identity salience consistent with Self-Categorization Theory. The study advances international migration scholarship by showing how national identity and occupational status shape home-country evaluations of settlement-oriented emigration and by foregrounding emotions as a mechanism linking identity salience to policy-relevant attitudes.

Analyzing Online Migration Forums: An Introduction to Natural Language Processing for International Migration Research

Nari Yoo, Donghun Kim, Sou Hyun Jang, Eric Fong

Full text
In contemporary migration studies, online forums are common spaces where people exchange information and seek advice about international migration. This methods note introduces the Reddit community r/IWantOut as a data source for studying migration aspirations and presents a validated natural language processing (NLP) pipeline for analyzing it. The forum's community rules require posters to encode age, gender, origin, and intended destinations in a fixed title format, so users label their own migration aspirations in a semi-machine-readable form when they post. Using 156,313 submissions from 2009 to 2026 (58,892 after cleaning), we demonstrate three analytical approaches: (1) time-series analysis to identify temporal shifts in discourse volume, (2) dictionary- and rule-based extraction, together with Named Entity Recognition (NER), to recover origin–destination pairs and sociodemographic attributes from the structured titles, and (3) large language model (LLM)-based zero-shot classification, used only for classifying migration motivations. Validation against human-coded labels across three LLMs (GPT-4.1, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, and DeepSeek-V3) showed that GPT-4.1 achieved the highest agreement (mean κ = 0.566), with substantial agreement on push factors, moderate agreement on pull and enabling factors, and fair agreement on constraining factors. Applying this approach to 56,242 international migration posts, we found that enabling factors (individual-level resources) appeared most frequently (77.3%), followed by constraining factors (49.3%), pull factors (37.2%), and push factors (23.6%). For each method, we provide practical guidance on implementation, data access, and validation, and reflect on methodological limitations and ethical considerations.

Who Governs Migrant Precarity? Intermediated Exclusion Under Conditions of Intensified Vulnerability

Fu-Hsuan Chen

Full text
Who governs migrant precarity, and through what mechanisms is it sustained in contemporary labor migration regimes? This article conceptualizes intermediated exclusion as a governance logic through which accountability is displaced, producing migrant precarity. Drawing on a nationwide employer survey (n = 3,747) and qualitative evidence from labor brokers, local officials, and migrant workers in Taiwan, it identifies three modes of delegated governance: institutional outsourcing, household-level ambiguity, and hybrid flexibility. These arrangements shift responsibility away from the state, embedding precarity through wage opacity, dependence on brokers, and the policing of ‘runaway’ cases. The article shows that delegated governance not only fails to protect migrant workers, but also contributes to the organization of vulnerability through dispersed forms of authority. Beyond Taiwan, the findings suggest that migrant precarity is less an exception than a patterned outcome of how labor migration governance is enacted in practice.

Population, Space and Place

Late‐Life Mobility in a Rapidly Ageing Middle‐Income Country: Spatial Patterns and Local Demographic Change in Brazil, 1980–2010

Fernando Antonio Ignacio González, Jean Sleifer

Full text
How do older adults reshape the spatial demography of a middle‐income country undergoing one of the fastest population‐ageing transitions on record? Brazil offers a particularly informative case: its share of population aged 60 or above rose from 6.2% in 1980 to 10.8% in 2010 and is projected to reach 25% by 2050, and a nationally administered pension regime makes benefits fully portable across the country's 5565 municipalities. Using large‐sample microdata from four Brazilian Demographic Censuses (1980–2010, 84 million records harmonised to 4298 stable comparable areas) linked to municipal economic accounts, we document a senior counter‐current with the hallmarks of counter‐urbanisation. Senior inter‐area mobility declines with age yet remains substantial through retirement ages, and it is not local boundary‐hopping: the median senior move covers roughly 100 kilometres and nearly a third crosses state lines. Older Brazilians move against the working‐age tide—the largest metropolitan areas lose retirees on net, while small and medium‐sized interior destinations receive them at rates reaching 5%–6% of initial population per 5‐year window. Receiving areas expand demographically—in total population, working‐age cohorts, employment, housing stock and services activity—but GDP per capita and agricultural value added grow more slowly, a pattern consistent with retirees transmitting purchasing power to destinations whose productive base does not scale with the inflow. These associations are robust to geographic‐amenity controls, pre‐trend conditioning and microregion fixed effects and geographically weighted regressions show they hold across virtually the entire national territory; we are explicit, however, that positive selection of destinations bounds them above any causal effect. We discuss what the Brazilian counter‐current implies for population geography and for other middle‐income countries tracing the same demographic curve.

Intermediaries and Informal Migration Pathways of Filipino Migrant Workers in Belgium

Ferlie Rose Ann N. Famaloan

Full text
This study examines the migration trajectories of Filipino migrant workers in Belgium, asking how migrants rely on intermediaries across different informal migration pathways. Drawing on Fresnoza‐Flot's humanizing approach to migration studies and based on 20 in‐depth interviews, it traces how migrants navigate legal precarity through intermediaries. Empirically, intermediaries become salient at two critical junctures: (1) facilitating entry into Belgium through invitations, referrals and circulation of information and (2) assisting migrants, to varying degrees, to navigate Belgian legal, social and labour systems after arrival. By centering migrant narratives, the study presents three informal migration pathways: jump ship migration as a shift from regular to irregular status; short‐term visa overstay as legal entry that extends beyond authorized stay; and cross‐border labour mobility as movement through multiple countries before settling in Belgium. Migrant narratives matter analytically because they show how intermediaries shape the terms under which legal precarity is navigated, how legality is interpreted and recalibrated over time and how informal pathways are produced relationally rather than as isolated individual acts.

Disrupted Trajectories: COVID ‐19, Transnational Youth Mobility and Unequal Transitions to Adulthood

Simon Chambers, Anita Harris, Loretta Baldassar

Full text
While the impact of COVID‐19 on youth development has been widely studied, less is known about its effects on transnationally mobile young adults. This longitudinal study of Australian outbound and inbound mobility examines how the pandemic disrupted this population's experiences abroad as they navigated transitions to adulthood. It draws on two waves of survey data collected using a convenience sample between July 2020 and September 2022. Our findings reveal that COVID‐19 presented significant challenges to young adults living away from home, with disruptions to their future plans, social connections, and well‐being. However, these impacts were not uniform. The pandemic magnified existing inequalities, with those on short‐term visas, women, and those already facing financial precarity experiencing greater negative effects. Moreover, individuals who experienced higher negative effects also reported increased struggles in achieving personal milestones associated with transitions to adulthood. This study highlights how crises (like the pandemic) amplify the vulnerability of transnationally mobile youth, a population often assumed to be—or actively striving to be—independent and self‐sufficient. It underscores the need for targeted support and interventions that address their specific challenges, including more equitable and inclusive migration and education policies that recognise the differentiated and overlapping vulnerabilities faced by this population.

Europe as a Normative Horizon: Migration Aspirations Among Japanese Self‐Initiated Migrants

Miyako Hayakawa

Full text
This article examines migration aspirations among highly educated Japanese nationals considering migration to Europe, with particular attention to how Europe is imagined as a normative horizon. Drawing on interviews with migration experts and Japanese aspiring migrants, the study shows that migration aspirations are shaped not only by structural constraints in Japan—such as economic stagnation, demanding work cultures, and gender inequality—but also by perceptions of Europe as a space associated with democratic values, social inclusion, gender equality, and alternative ways of organising work and everyday life. By focusing on aspirations rather than realised mobility, the article demonstrates how imaginaries of Europe and perceived constraints in the country of origin interact to shape migration motivations prior to actual movement.

Persons Enough to Punish: Illegality, Deportability and the Hierarchy of Personhood in US Immigration

David Niculae

Full text
This paper argues that contemporary United States immigration law and policy construct a hierarchy of personhood for undocumented migrants which better explains their status as opposed to a classic binary framework of citizen/migrant. It develops a four‐layer conceptual framework of personhood including baseline personhood, membership personhood, administrative personhood and social personhood. These layers are argued to be unevenly distributed across legal domains, creating a strong and impactful hierarchization of individuals. Historically situating the current regime within the late‐twentieth‐century turn towards employer sanctions and one‐time legalization, the expansion of deportable offences, the restriction of non‐citizens' access to public benefits, and post‐9/11 securitization, the paper demonstrates the rise of an ‘immigration industrial complex’. It then draws on critical scholarship relating to deportability, the illegality trap, and crimmigration to argue that illegality is actively produced as a tool for governance that keeps migrants exploitable. A section on doctrinal analysis of constitutional and administrative law shows that undocumented migrants are recognized as persons in some contexts, yet denied secure access to the social state and membership. The paper further identifies mechanisms of personhood‐thinning and reads their combined effects through a republican non‐domination lens. It concludes by sketching reforms aimed at thickening personhood and reducing domination to escape the illegality trap and move towards a just immigration law framework.

Can Hong Kong Be Home? Sub‐Saharan African Students' Notions and Aspirations of Home in a Global City

Phillip Thebe

Full text
African student migration to China has increased markedly in recent years, driven by China's internationalisation agenda. While existing scholarship has focused largely on African students' educational motivations and precarious transcultural navigation in mainland China, far less is known about how African students experience Hong Kong as a potential site of belonging. This paper asks: Can Hong Kong be home for African students? Drawing on a 3‐year ethnography (2019–2023) with African higher‐education students, complemented by ongoing informal engagements in 2024, the study examines students' everyday experiences, the opportunities and constraints they encounter and the strategies they employ to cultivate belonging. The findings reveal Hong Kong as a distinctive socio‐political and institutional space where shifting governance, racialised encounters and transnational ties intersect to shape aspirations and senses of home. Rather than a fixed endpoint, home emerges as a negotiated, ongoing process. The paper contributes new empirical insights into African student mobility and advances theorisation of belonging by conceptualising home as fluid, iterative and shaped by mobility, marginality and aspiration.

Gratitude That Never Was: Migration, Labour and Discontent in Au Pair Experiences

Bengi Sullu

Full text
Conceptions of the ‘ideal immigrant’ as not only compliant but also grateful have long shaped US immigration discourse and material realities. Young immigrant women engaged in service and care work frequently report encountering expectations that they should feel grateful for the opportunities provided by their employers. In this study, I examine the narrated lived experiences of Turkish au pairs, young women on temporary work visas providing child care in U.S. households, with particular attention to how their discontent builds up from the point of hiring through the decision to quit, and the role that perceived expectations of gratitude from the host families play in this process. I employ a qualitative research design drawing on publicly available YouTube videos created by Turkish au pairs since 2020, as well as on in‐depth conversations with a former au pair conducted between 2023 and 2024. Engaging the literature at the intersection of labour, immigration and affect, I use narrative analysis to unpack how resentment emerges at the nexus of blurred work‐life boundaries, undercompensated labour, and social isolation. These tensions become increasingly unmanageable when au pairs realise that their host families, implicitly or explicitly, expect gratitude as part of the labour relationship. Subtle or explicit refusals to perform gratitude by au pairs destabilize labour arrangements and relationships built on unequal foundations and complicate Global North–South encounters, particularly in the context of post‐2015 Turkish youth migration to the United States. In this context, Turkish au pairs seek to understand themselves as participants in cultural exchange, a distinction that is frequently, and perhaps strategically, overlooked by host families. This research advances understandings of the affective dimensions of migration by illuminating how emotional labour expectations intersect with gender, culture, age, economic precarity and centre–periphery relations in transnational care work.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Policing Queer and Racialized Identities: An Intersectional Approach to Study Police Bias

Emanuele Murgolo

Full text
Queer people in the United States have been historically over-policed, in a long-standing history of criminalization and oppression by law enforcement. Despite scholars having extensively used administrative records of police stops to uncover racial disparities and bias in law enforcement practices, virtually no studies exist doing the same to examine the discriminatory policing of queerness—by itself, and in the intersection with race. In this article, I fill this gap by analyzing administrative records of ∼3,100,000 stops in the United States between 2018 and 2025, looking at whether civilians perceived as LGBTQ+ are disproportionately targeted by the police in post-stop decisions. Through multiple tests, I find consistent evidence that being queer is associated with a higher probability of being searched, arrested, and be victims of police use of force. Then, using an intersectional approach, I test whether the interaction of race with queerness worsens police discrimination. Results show that bias gets worse for racial minorities who are also LGBTQ+, indicating that a dynamic of intersectional, double disadvantage can be found in police discrimination. This research underscores the importance of applying an intersectional framework to police studies, and the ongoing role of policing in reproducing the marginalization of historically oppressed communities.

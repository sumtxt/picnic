American Sociological Review

Relational Roots of Civic Fields: Capital Conversion and Organizational Reproduction in a Developing Country

Jose Eos Trinidad

Full text
Social scientists have long studied the emergence of civic organizations through two dominant perspectives: demand-side theories arguing that these groups fill gaps left by government and market failures, and supply-side theories emphasizing resource mobilization and dependence. However, these theories insufficiently account for how contemporary nonprofits and social enterprises arise in increasingly networked, professionalized, and competitive environments. This article interrogates the relational roots of civic fields, centering the concrete ties and interactions through which new organizations are incubated, resourced, and legitimated. Using the case of a dozen new civic organizations in a developing country, I show how founders leverage interpersonal and interorganizational ties to convert social capital into intellectual, economic, human, and symbolic capital. However, this ability for capital conversion depended on actors’ positions in the field and dispositions embedded in their habitus. These processes contribute to the field’s organizational reproduction —that is, reproduction of professional practices, funding models, and state-aligned approaches mirroring founders’ shared social (dis)positions. As civic organizations play increasingly prominent roles in development and governance, understanding their relational origins helps explain whose visions of social change gain organizational form.

Sociological Methods & Research

Marginal Structural Effects for Exponential Random Graph Models

Scott W. Duxbury

Full text
Exponential random graph models (ERGMs) are now a staple for social network analysis due to their ability to parameterize effects from endogenous network structures, such as two stars and triangles. However, interpreting coefficients for structural ERGM terms poses two problems. First, it is not possible to change the value of a structural term “holding all else constant” in models with nontrivial dyadic dependencies. Second, parameters for structural ERGM terms may be affected by scaling (noncollapsibility), which can alter the direction, size, and significance of structural coefficients. While the first issue is known in the literature, the second has not been previously reported. This study introduces a methodological framework based on marginal structural effects (MSEs) to address both problems. MSEs capture the discrete marginal effect of an endogenous network structure by calculating the difference in probability when comparing two potential ties that differ only by the value of a structural term. MSEs provide an intuitive interpretation for structural network effects and are robust to the effects of scaling. Extensions to comparisons between models, interactions with exogenous covariates, and analysis of samples of networks are discussed. An example is provided using the largest AddHealth school network to demonstrate how the framework can be applied.

The Influence of Societal Changes on the Contribution of Genetics to Fertility Behavior

Agnes Fauske

Full text
Highlights The heritability of fertility behavior altered during the profound societal changes that occurred from the 1950s onward in Norway. Societal changes modify the heritability of age at first birth and number of children, and the change in heritability varies by gender and outcome. A theoretical explanation that suggests norms weakened and opportunities broadened across the latter part of the twentieth century and into the early twenty-first century, thereby increasing heritability, falls short. Results provide some support for the hypothesis that the importance of genes increases during periods of societal change, when linked to the transition to dualearner families and family policy implementation. The study highlights the importance of considering societal structures and gender differences when investigating genetic influences on fertility behavior.

Proving Their Loyalty: A Research Note on the Effects of Serving in World War II on Immigrant Outcomes

Jeff Chan

Full text
Highlights This study assesses the effects of World War II military service on outcomes for immigrant veterans. Linked, full-count U.S. Census data are used in a triple-differences design. Immigrant veterans are more likely to become citizens and marry U.S.-born spouses. There is little evidence of differences in labor market or educational outcomes relative to U.S.-born veterans.

Assortative Mating, the Division of Labor in Marriage, and Analytic Choices: A Reply

Pilar Gonalons-Pons, Christine R. Schwartz

Full text
Highlights We use the large samples of U.S. Census and ACS data from 1940–2013 to conduct a reanalysis. The evidence for changes in the division of labor after marriage is robust. The evidence on assortative mating is more variable, even in large samples. Our best estimate is that assortative mating explains 29% of earnings homogamy trends. The definition of newlyweds and treatment of missing values are consequential.

To See or Not to See: Another Look at the Mechanisms Behind Growing Earnings Homogamy in the United States

Yifan Shen, Tim F. Liao

Full text
Highlights Changes in assortative mating matter more for earnings homogamy than prior work had reported. The prior 10.8% estimate was unstable owing to random noise in the samples. Reducing noise in the samples yields a larger estimated contribution of assortative mating. Cohorts with higher earnings homogamy following marriage were already characterized by higher homogamy at marriage entry. Earnings assortative mating changed in a nonlinear trajectory after 1970.

Having a child through medically assisted reproduction: a social stratification perspective

Alice Goisis, Alina Pelikh, Maria Palma, Pekka Martikainen, Niina Metsä-Simola, Hanna Remes

Full text
Prior research documents marked social disparities in births through medically assisted reproduction (MAR), which are often attributed to unequal access to treatment. However, social inequalities may also arise from processes operating after MAR treatments begin. We examine this possibility using population-level administrative register data from Finland, a context where MAR treatments are generously subsidized and access barriers are relatively low. Focusing on all childless women who initiated MAR treatments between 1995 and 2019, we analyze whether and why women with tertiary education are more likely than less-educated women to have a child through MAR. The results show that, among childless women who start MAR treatments, those with tertiary education are more likely to have a child after MAR than women with below tertiary education. This educational gradient is partly explained by differences in early treatment discontinuation, uptake of more invasive and more successful MAR treatments, and per-cycle treatment success rates. The results support the hypothesis that social stratification processes in MAR outcomes continues after the start of MAR treatments.

Racism and parent stress in the context of police violence

Ann Meier, Claire Kamp Dush, Alexandra VanBergen, Sierra Clark, Hyunjae Kwon, Chalandra Bryant

Full text
This project assessed parental stress in 2020 and 2021 during a period of racial trauma marked by high-profile, race-motivated murders of people of color. We leveraged the Contextual Model of Family Stress Re-Imaged to understand parents’ stress about children’s interactions with police in the context of racism. Findings indicate higher policing stress among Black parents. To measure racism, we assessed county-level racial segregation and socioeconomic privilege, respondents’ perceptions of community climate, discrimination experiences, and the salience of George Floyd’s murder and its aftermath in their lives. The associations between Black race and discrimination experiences on the one hand and parent policing stress on the other were both direct and indirect. Black respondents and those who experienced more discrimination found more salience in the racialized milieu after George Floyd’s murder, and this was directly associated with more parent policing stress. Other indicators of racism were associated with parent policing stress indirectly. Those living in more socioeconomically privileged contexts reported a more favorable community climate, and this was associated with less policing stress. Those living in more racially segregated contexts experienced the racialized milieu as more salient, which was linked to more policing stress. These findings suggest marginalizing contexts of racism are risk factors for Black parents’ compromised well-being.

Framing inclusion: discursive logics of entitlement in immigrant social rights advocacy

Mara Junge

Full text
This article investigates how civil society organizations (CSOs) advocate for immigrant access to social assistance in politically charged environments. It explores whether the frame choices of CSOs are shaped by their relationships with government bodies and examines the implications of these narratives for representing a highly diverse group of non-citizens. The article introduces a novel two-dimensional framework that categorizes advocacy frames along two axes: “scope of inclusion” (Targeted Inclusion vs. Universal Inclusion) and “justification for entitlement” (Individual Justification vs. Structural Justification). Findings from document analysis reveal a predominant reliance on frames that call for the inclusion of specific groups basing entitlement on individual characteristics independent of CSOs’ relationship to the state. The most radical frame type (Structural Justification for Universal Inclusion), however, is more frequently used by independent CSOs. Analyses of interviews underscore that CSOs navigate a trade-off: aligning with targeted, individualistic frames can achieve incremental policy gains, but potentially reinforces stratified notions of “deservingness”, contributing to internal hierarchies within immigrant communities. Conversely, advancing structural justification for universal inclusion carries the potential to challenge systemic inequalities yet risks limited immediate resonance within the political mainstream. These insights highlight the tension between short-term policy success and long-term systemic change in CSO advocacy strategies. The empirical findings, alongside theoretical discussions, extend beyond this specific policy field, offering broader insights into civil society strategies in adverse contexts.

European Sociological Review

How do people view income inequality in China? An interactive framework of inequality and economic growth

Shiyuan Li, Airan Liu

Full text
Economic inequality has become a pressing societal challenge worldwide, yet less is known about how individuals perceive and evaluate it. Drawing on longitudinal data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS 2012–2018), this study uses modified hierarchical linear models to examine how objective inequality interacts with economic growth in shaping subjective inequality. The results show, first, that the average association between objective inequality and respondents’ severity ratings of inequality (SRI) is weak and not statistically significant. Second, economic growth moderates this association: objective inequality is associated with higher SRI only under low-growth conditions. Finally, this moderating role of economic growth is salient only among socio-economically disadvantaged individuals. These findings enhance our understanding of how individuals view economic inequality in China’s post-high-speed growth era and provide broader comparative implications for societies characterized by different configurations of inequality and economic growth.

Learning by moving: Physical motion accelerates category learning

Luke Rosedahl, Yuka Sasaki, Takeo Watanabe

Full text
Categorization is crucial for survival, helping animals forage, navigate, and avoid predators—all tasks involving movement. Yet, dominant theories of category learning treat it as a purely cognitive or perceptual process. Here, we challenge this view by demonstrating that physical movement can enhance category learning—but selectively. Using immersive virtual reality, we found that performing large actions, such as swinging a lightsaber, substantially improved learning of categories requiring integration of multiple cues (e.g., distinguishing aggressive from noncancerous lesions), while having minimal impact on categories involving independent evaluation of features (e.g., sorting by color and shape). This selective enhancement reveals a previously unrecognized motor contribution to categorization and may explain why prior studies missed it, opening avenues for optimizing training in critical categorization tasks.

Action features dominate cortical representation during natural vision

Heejung Jung, Hunar Jain, Xiaochun Han, Ma Feilong, Jane Han, Deepanshi Shokeen, Cara Van Uden, Isabella Hanson, Andrew C. Connolly, Tor D. Wager, ... (+2 authors)

Full text
Cortical resources are allocated into systems specialized for processing ecologically relevant features of the world. These features are typically studied in isolation using controlled experimental stimuli, making it difficult to assess the relative importance of different kinds of features that tend to overlap during natural vision. In the current study, we evaluated the relative contributions of action, agent, and scene features in predicting cortical activity while participants viewed a 1-h nature documentary. We tested four sets of model features: semantic vectors capturing the observed action, agent, and scene features derived from an annotation of the stimulus, as well as low-level visual motion-energy features. We used banded ridge regression to fit vertex-wise encoding models from the combination of all four sets of features. While each set of features predicted neural activity in the expected areas, the action features predicted more widespread activity. A variance partitioning analysis revealed that the action features captured the most unique variance in neural activity across a much larger extent of cortex than the agent or scene features. Our findings suggest that cortical activity during dynamic, natural vision is dominated by features for understanding the actions of others.

Heterogeneous impacts of temperature extremes on climate attitudes and Green voting

Jacopo Bassetto, Roman Hoffmann, Raya Muttarak, Jonas Peisker, Piero Stanig

Full text
In the past decade, the public in Europe has become more concerned about climate change and expresses stronger proenvironmental policy preferences. Based on data from the European Social Survey (2012–2022, 25 countries), we show that the increased exposure to temperature extremes has contributed to the observed changes in climate change attitudes and electoral support for environmentalist policy platforms. For each additional degree of excess temperature experienced during warm spells measured over the previous 12 mo, we find that the probabilities of being worried about climate change, of feeling responsible for climate change, and of voting for Green parties and Green coalitions increase by 4.0 [CI95: 2.5, 5.5], 2.5 [CI95: 1.1, 3.9], and 2.8 [CI95: 0.9, 3.7] percentage points respectively. We also document significant heterogeneity across individuals, based on gender and education, particularly between highly educated women and low-educated men. While the latter group consistently exhibits the lowest proenvironmental attitudes and voting, it is this group that shows the strongest increases when exposed to temperature extremes, converging toward the levels of their more educated counterparts. Further regional heterogeneity analyses reveal that the effects of warm temperature extremes are smaller in less prosperous or economically disadvantaged regions, as well as in regions with greenhouse gas emission levels above the median. The findings suggest a nuanced interplay between individual factors and contextual influences in shaping climate attitudes of European citizens and their motivations or reservations toward greater climate action.

Equity dimensions of future vulnerabilities to climate hazards: Long-term spatial population projections for New York City consistent with Shared Socioeconomic Pathways

Deborah Balk, Jenna F. Tipaldo, Leiwen Jiang, Hamidreza Zoraghein

Full text
Projections of future population are important for a myriad of reasons. Large cities, states, and countries routinely produce near- and mid-range population projections. In the context of climate change, the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) framework is used at global and national scales to project long-term population to inform future emissions scenarios and understand future climate-related exposures and impacts. Much less consideration has been given to long-term projections at smaller scales, even though climate impacts are place based and cities now host most of the world’s population. Cities and their metropolitan regions are complex, heterogeneous in terms of population, environmental, and built features. Thus, using global or national-scale approaches overlooks important aspects of urban futures. Cities are diverse, fluid population centers home to migrants, with employment opportunities yet substantial inequality. These quintessential city-centric features are ignored in global/national-scale population projection methods, including long-term ones consistent with the SSPs. Leveraging state-level SSP-based projections, here we extend that approach for New York City, currently home to 8.8 million persons. Combining multistate and multiregional demographic projection and spatial downscaling models, we project county-level population by racial/ethnic groups and age–sex to 2100 and downscale to 100 m. Such projections allow for an equity lens to inform assessments of future vulnerabilities to climate hazards. Four of the five SSPs project that the population of New York City will be smaller and much older at the end of the century, though these futures are dependent on demographic assumptions about fertility and especially migration.

Protestantism and insurance—an elective affinity? A multilevel comparative-historical analysis of the confessional insurance gap

Sebastian Kohl

Full text
While there are many studies and theories about the rise of social insurance and the public welfare state, the prior emergence and growth of private life and nonlife insurance are largely understudied, mostly due to a lack of data. This article fills this gap by testing whether there is an elective affinity between the rise and evolution of private insurance and Protestantism that is irreducible to typical factors external to religion (gross domestic product, education, demography, public welfare). Drawing on multiple new datasets, the paper tests whether Catholic–Protestant differences in insurance demand persist when controlling for standard covariates external to religion across nineteen OECD countries (1870–2020), across states in the confessionally mixed federations of Germany, Switzerland and the USA (1876–2020) as well as across US counties and European cities around 1909. The article finds Lutheran and Reformed Protestant areas to have had higher private insurance penetration and density than Catholic ones, controlling for standard covariates. This relationship is more pronounced in life than nonlife branches, more pronounced for insurance demand than insurance company activity, and more consistently found in Europe than within the USA. More generally, the article suggests that the confessional insurance gap may provide a missing empirical link to better understand the emergence of Alpine and Maritime insurance cultures, different premodern poor-ethics traditions and the different worlds of modern welfare states and pension systems.

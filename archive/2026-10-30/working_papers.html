Entertainment television and a wildlife documentary produce equal engagement but opposite conservation behavior

Alexandra Boren, Diogo Veríssimo, Chase Alexander Niesner, Jiaying Zhao, Nicolas Ara, Alejandra Echeverri

Full text
Entertainment media reaches billions of people and shapes how they understand the natural world, yet its consequences for real conservation behavior remain largely untested. Because mainstream entertainment often reaches broader audiences than conservation documentaries, it may shape pro-wildlife attitudes and behaviors as powerfully as explicitly educational media. In a preregistered experiment, we randomly assigned participants (n=400) to view an episode of a wildlife conservation documentary (Blue Planet II) or a commercial fishing reality television program (Wicked Tuna), both portraying the same marine domain through opposing narratives. Using multi-group structural equation modeling, we show that the front end of narrative persuasion operated equivalently across programs: narrative transportation predicted character identification, which predicted attitude toward the episode, and identification extended even to non-charismatic fish. This shared engagement then diverged sharply at attitude formation: greater engagement with Wicked Tuna produced a more instrumental view of human-fish relationships, whereas Blue Planet II did not. These attitudinal effects were associated with a divergence in behavior. Signing a program-congruent petition, rather than the experimental condition itself, most strongly predicted which charity participants donated real money to, consistent with a small public commitment channeling a narrative-induced identity into costly action. Notably, pro-fish attitudes mobilized donations most strongly among viewers whose media was not already conservation-branded. Narrative engagement therefore carries limited intrinsic conservation valence, and its effects depend on the protagonist and perspective an audience is given. Reaching non-self-selected audiences through identity-matched entertainment may yield larger behavioral gains than intensifying appeals to audiences already primed toward conservation.

Sustainable Transport and Urban Inequality: A Mixed Methods Study of Selective Siting and Procedural Justice in Berlin

Katja Salomo, Sarah George, Vanessa Rösner, Viktoria Scheidler

Full text
Urban sustainability policies are widely promoted as key instruments for mitigating climate change and improving urban quality of life. At the same time, concerns have grown that such interventions may reproduce socio spatial inequalities through selec-tive siting, uneven benefits, and exclusionary planning processes. This paper examines both the distribution and lived experience of sustainable transport measures, combining a citywide spatial analysis with a qualitative neighborhood case study. Using 1 km × 1 km grid level data for Berlin, we analyze the socio economic correlates of multiple transport sustainability measures, including ride sharing stations, EV charging points, cycling infrastructure, traffic calming measures, and low traffic zones. Spatially Lagged X (SLX) models reveal policy specific patterns: infrastructure ena-bling alternative modes of transport tends to cluster in and around more affluent but also, in some cases, more diverse neighborhoods, while grids with higher child poverty are systematically less likely to feature traffic calmed street space. Low traffic zones are more prevalent in affluent areas, too. Complementary focus group discussions conducted during the implementation of a low-traffic zone show that residents from diverse and poorer neighborhoods as well as small business owners frequently interpret such street space interventions through the lens of gentrification and displacement. Participants describe feelings of alienation and criticize unclear communication and tokenistic participation. Our findings suggest that gentrification concerns may become a significant barrier to public support for urban sustainability transitions.

Does Power-Sharing Reduce Polarization and Improve Performance? Evidence from the Tunisian Parliament

Robert Kubinec, Sharan Grewal

Full text
Power-sharing arrangements like grand coalitions and unity governments are often touted as a way for transitional democracies to reduce political tensions and improve government performance. Yet there are few empirical tests of their effectiveness. We theorize that power-sharing is more likely to incentivize postponing polarizing issues, rather than resolving them, delaying the necessary but controversial reforms in order to preserve the coalition. We test this hypothesis in the case of Tunisia, which had been considered the Arab Spring's success story for its pursuit of power-sharing. To measure polarization, we examine all roll call votes from Tunisia's first and second post-transition parliaments (2011-2019). Employing a time-varying ideal point model, we show that power-sharing merely masked polarization, while also postponing critical political and economic reforms. The case of Tunisia suggests that even in homogeneous countries with voluntary grand coalitions, power-sharing is no panacea for a bitterly divided polity.

The Price of Opportunity: School Quality and Housing Prices in the Dayton Metropolitan Area

Karna Roy, Andrew Zhang

Full text
This study examines the relationship between school performance and housing prices in the Dayton Metropolitan Area. The analysis uses data from 40 geographic subunits across seven school districts, combining housing prices, Ohio Performance Index scores, and socioeconomic characteristics. A baseline regression is first used to estimate the association between school performance and housing prices while controlling for socioeconomic characteristics, followed by a district fixed-effects model that accounts for unobserved characteristics shared by geographic subunits within the same school district. Late evolutionary origins of necessary key abilities for true ritual

Claudio Tennie, Rohan Kapitány

Full text
Human ritual is difficult to trace directly in deep time. We approach its evolutionary origins indirectly by asking when the necessary cognitive prerequisites for ritual were first plausibly present in the archaeological record – to impose a terminus post quem for true ritual. We argue that true ritual, unlike mere “ritualized” biological behaviour and unlike possible precursors, requires know-how copying: the causal reproduction of behavioural and/or artefactual forms derived from the observation of models, which crucially includes forms that individuals would not realistically perform without having observed such models. Because spontaneous know-how copying in non-human great apes is nearly or completely absent, ritual was probably not inherited from our last common ancestor. We therefore look to the hominin archaeological record. We argue that the earliest stone tools – and especially the Oldowan - did not require know-how copying, and so do not by themselves positively evidence the necessary cognitive capacity required for true ritual. However, this cognitive capacity may become apparent in the middle to late Acheulean, roughly 800,000–500,000 years ago, when knapped stone forms become more diverse and complex, and where their underlying know-how cannot parsimoniously be explained in other ways. That is, the middle to late Acheulean positively evidence for the first type(s) of know-how copying, and as a result, we suggest this period as the earliest plausible window the necessary cognitive faculties required for true ritual. The inferred presence of know-how copying at these times suggests the last common ancestor of humans, Neanderthals and Denisovans, were the first plausible candidates for the earliest true ritual. We leave open the possibility that true ritual may also require symbolism as another key element, though we speculate that symbolism may have emerged more recently, perhaps between 500,000 and 150,000 years ago. If so, full true ritual should be sought mainly within that later bracket. Our dates are not presented as final, but as a cognitive-archaeologically derived guide for narrowing the search. These dates may change in the future with new primatological or archaeological evidence or when new types of analysis become available.

Healthism as the ideological substrate of orthorexia nervosa: health, morality, and self-discipline in contemporary food culture

Safiia Yessova

Full text
Orthorexia nervosa (ON), commonly described as a pathological preoccupation with healthy or “pure” eating, has attracted growing attention across psychology, psychiatry, nutrition studies, and the medical humanities. While existing research has predominantly approached orthorexia through individual psychopathology, personality traits, eating behaviors, and diagnostic classification, comparatively less attention has been paid to the ideological environment in which orthorexic practices become meaningful, legitimate, and socially reinforced. This article argues that healthism constitutes an important ideological substrate of orthorexia nervosa. Drawing on Robert Crawford’s concept of healthism and interdisciplinary research on orthorexia, the article conceptualizes orthorexia not simply as an individual fixation on healthy eating but as a potential pathological intensification of culturally normalized imperatives of health optimization. Healthism transforms health from one human value among others into a moral obligation and increasingly locates responsibility for health within individual lifestyle choices. Within this ideological environment, food becomes morally classified, bodily discipline becomes evidence of responsible citizenship, and failure to maintain an “optimal” lifestyle may generate guilt, anxiety, and stigma. Social media and digital wellness cultures can further amplify these processes by circulating idealized representations of clean eating, fitness, bodily control, and self-optimization. The article proposes a conceptual pathway linking healthism, moralization of food, individualization of health responsibility, self-surveillance, dietary restriction, and orthorexic tendencies. It concludes that understanding orthorexia requires attention not only to individual psychological vulnerability but also to the broader sociocultural conditions that normalize obsessive health practices.

Two-Person Additively-Separable Sum Games

Somdeb Lahiri

Full text
We consider a sub-class of bi-matrix games which we refer to as two-person (hereafter referred to as two-player) additively-separable sum (TPASS) games, where the sum of the pay-offs of the two players is additively separable. The row player’s pay-off at each pair of pure strategies, is the sum of two numbers, the first of which may be dependent on the pure strategy chosen by the column player and the second being independent of the pure strategy chosen by the column player. The column player’s pay-off at each pair of pure strategies, is also the sum of two numbers, the first of which may be dependent on the pure strategy chosen by the row player and the second being independent of the pure strategy chosen by the row player. The sum of the inter-dependent components of the pay-offs of the two players is assumed to be zero. We prove the existence of equilibrium for such games and show that the set of equilibria for such games is the projection on the set of strategy pairs of the solutions of a pair of linear programming problem that are dual to each other. This result is a generalization of the corresponding and well-known result for two-person zero-sum games. We also show that a (randomized or mixed) strategy pair is an equilibrium of the game if and only if there exist two other real numbers such that the three together solve a certain linear programming problem. In order to prove this result, we need to appeal to the existence of an equilibrium for the TPASS game. The technology we use to prove our results, consists of the duality theorems and the complementary slackness theorem of linear programming.

Hippocampal Neuronal Overflow and Cortical Reconstruction: A Hypothesis on the Formation of Neural Networks Unique to the Human Brain

Jue Wang

Full text
This study explores the neural basis of humans’ unique cognitive abilities, focusing on the extensive neuronal migration in white matter that continues for nearly 18 months after birth. Traditional views attribute these migrating neurons to the ventral telencephalon during the fetal period, but this cannot adequately explain their scale and duration. We propose the “Hippocampal Neuronal Overflow and Cortical Reconstruction” hypothesis: hippocampal neurogenesis in human infants peaks perinatally, and under rich environmental stimulation the survival of new hippocampal neurons increases markedly. When their number exceeds the hippocampus’s capacity, they overflow into the white matter and migrate without a fixed route, occupying channels used by normally migrating neurons and displacing them. We term both groups “hippocampal-type neurons.” The depletion of normal neurons from the migration stream may also trigger compensatory proliferation at their source. Hippocampal-type neurons distribute across the frontal, insular, and parietal cortices and parts of the temporal cortex, where they may form a novel neural network. This overflow may be a developmental mechanism unique to humans, and the network may underlie human-specific language, memory transformation, and advanced cognition, offering a new perspective on the uniqueness of human brain development.

Full text
This article presents a comparative case study examining variation in team design and performance across 13 teams within agile software development. Full text
Environmental problems rarely respect national borders, and addressing them requires coordinated action across societies with very different economic and institutional conditions. Such coordination requires two things: a shared understanding of which problems matter most, and a willingness to sacrifice some economic well-being to address them. This raises two basic questions: is there common ground in how different societies rank environmental priorities, and are people willing to bear economic costs to address the highest-ranked ones? Calibration and subgroup fairness of a childhood stunting risk model: a cross-sectional analysis of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey 2022

Sweety Akter

Full text
Background: Childhood stunting affects roughly one in four children under five in Bangladesh. Machine learning models are increasingly proposed for risk stratification, but published reports typically emphasise discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUROC) and rarely examine calibration or subgroup performance. This study evaluated a stunting risk model on population health survey data, with calibration and subgroup fairness as primary outcomes rather than secondary considerations. Methods: We conducted a secondary analysis of the 2022 Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS) Kids Recode. The outcome was stunting, defined as a height-for-age z-score below -2 standard deviations using the WHO 2006 growth standards. Of 8,784 child records, 13 had implausible anthropometric measurements and 4,666 had missing outcome data, leaving 4,105 children in 670 enumeration areas (weighted prevalence 23.2%). Thirteen predictors were restricted to information plausibly available before anthropometric assessment. A regularised logistic regression was compared with a gradient-boosted tree model (XGBoost). Knowing or Believing? Political Interest, Institutional Trust and Turnout in the Arab World

Houda Es-sqalli

Full text
Does trust in national institutions make people more likely to vote, or does attention to politics matter more? The literature points both ways for the Arab world, where elections take place in contexts of contested legitimacy. Using Wave VII of the Arab Barometer (2021-2022), this paper tests the association between self-reported turnout, trust in the national government and political interest among 19,525 respondents in the nine countries with valid data on all items. In logistic regressions with country fixed effects, a one-point increase in political interest (four-point scale) is associated with a 7.1 percentage-point higher probability of reporting having voted, against 2.3 points for trust. The interest effect is positive in every country but varies significantly in size, from above 10 points in Morocco and Lebanon to near zero in Jordan and Sudan. A cluster analysis of trust in four institutions shows that only broad, consistent trust goes with higher turnout; selective trust looks much like distrust. Results hold across 128 alternative specifications. All estimates are associational.

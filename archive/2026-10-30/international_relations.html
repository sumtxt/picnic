Beyond the Battlefield: Localized Civilian Clashes and Political Attitudes in Violent Conflict

Carly Wayne, Sivan Hirsch-Hoefler, Masanori Kikuchi, Daphna Canetti

Full text
In protracted conflicts, civilians frequently encounter low-level, hostile actions from adversary civilians in nearby communities, yet the political consequences of these events remain understudied. We investigate this question in the Israeli–Palestinian context, examining how community-level exposure to hostile actions by Israeli settlers shapes Palestinian political attitudes in the West Bank. Leveraging a novel data set containing thousands of geo-coded conflict events from 2010 to 2020 paired with forty-six Palestinian public opinion polls in the West Bank, we find that district-level increases in settler hostilities are significantly associated with heightened political militancy among local Palestinians, associations that are larger and more robust than those from local Israeli military actions. A second empirical strategy examining changes in Palestinian attitudes before and after the establishment of nearby settler outposts reveals that this form of civilian land expropriation likewise leads to modest increases in political militancy among Palestinians in proximate towns. These findings highlight the distinct political impact of localized, low-level civilian actions and territorial expropriation—central but often overlooked features of sectarian territorial conflicts—offering insights for territorial and ethnic disputes globally.

When Firms Lead States: Financial Intermediaries and the Politics of Sovereign Green Bond Issuance

Monica Widmann

Full text
Sovereign green bonds typically appear only after corporate green bond issuance has accumulated—a sequence at odds with the policy consensus that sovereign borrowing anchors debt market development. I argue that the inversion reflects market building by global investment banks. The four banks that authored the Green Bond Principles in 2014 used early private corporate transactions to build the verification practices, pricing conventions, and investor networks needed for a functioning market, then served as lead managers when sovereigns entered, transferring accumulated expertise across the public–private divide. States entered a market whose institutional infrastructure they did not design. The Cox proportional hazards model shows that accumulated corporate issuance significantly accelerates sovereign entry, while government agency issuance delays it by substituting for direct sovereign participation. A bank-level triangulation shows that founding-bank presence at sovereign entry reflects green-bond-specific expertise transfer rather than general dominance in sovereign debt underwriting. The findings identify financial intermediaries as a distinct channel through which sustainable finance reaches sovereign borrowers and show how privately constructed market conditions shape the terms on which states engage with global climate finance.

Review of International Organizations

Trading migration? Explaining mobility provisions in preferential trade agreements

Philipp Lutz, Sandra Lavenex, Paula Hoffmeyer-Zlotnik

Full text
Notwithstanding a generally unfavorable political climate regarding international migration, preferential trade agreements (PTAs) increasingly include provisions facilitating the temporary movement of specific categories of workers. Today, approximately seven in ten newly concluded PTAs contain clauses that enable the mobility of workers and businesspeople—particularly business visitors and intra-corporate transferees, who constitute a substantial share of skilled temporary migration in advanced industrial economies. This trend is striking given states’ longstanding emphasis on preserving sovereign control over migration policy. This article investigates the drivers of mobility provisions in PTAs and the implications for broader economic and migration policy. We develop and test three theoretical logics: (1) a trade logic grounded in interests resulting from economic interdependence; (2) a politics logic centered on migration control objectives; and (3) an institutional logic emphasizing path dependence and diffusion. Drawing on the MITA dataset, which codes mobility provisions in PTAs globally from 1960 to 2020, we find that economic interdependence and institutional path dependence are the primary forces behind mobility provisions, while migration control prerogatives have little explanatory power for either the presence, scope, or depth of these provisions. Our findings underscore the economic rationale behind mobility provisions in trade agreements, highlighting a pattern of selective liberalization for highly skilled workers and an increasingly fragmented landscape of migration governance.

Parliamentary Elites in Authoritarian Regimes: new data

Felix Wiebrecht

Full text
Authoritarian politics are largely defined and shaped by elite politics. Yet, our knowledge about political elites in nondemocracies remains confined to those in particular regimes or, cross-nationally, leaders at the highest echelons of decision-making. This article introduces a novel dataset, Parliamentary Elites in Authoritarian Regimes (PEAR), that fills this gap and provides comprehensive information on members of parliament (MPs) across nondemocracies. With currently more than 230,000 observations on individual MPs from 882 legislative terms in 133 authoritarian regimes from 1945 to 2024, PEAR is based on the largest individual-level demographic database on national legislators ever assembled and provides unprecedented information about these types of elites in authoritarian regimes. The dataset records MPs’ names, gender, age, party affiliation, incumbency status, and constituency, and uniquely classifies legislators into ruling-party members, regime-supporting elites, and opposition politicians. These are then aggregated into legislature-level variables in the country-year format, showing, for instance, the share of women in parliament, the MPs’ mean and median ages, the share of young and elder MPs, and legislatures’ retention rates. I first present how the dataset was created from publicly available sources, including parliamentary websites, electoral commissions, and election observer reports, then highlight descriptives, and validate it against existing datasets. I then illustrate an application of the data, generating new insights into the reshuffling of elites in authoritarian regimes. PEAR will be an invaluable resource for our understanding of autocracies.

The impact of drone warfare on suicide bombings in Pakistan

Luqman Saeed, Michael Spagat

Full text
Drone strikes against militant groups have become a central counterterrorism tool, yet their effectiveness remains debated. We use weather-based instruments and a major drone-base closure to identify the causal effect of Central Intelligence Agency drone strikes in North Waziristan, Pakistan, on suicide bombings nationwide during 2008–2016. Our instrumental variables strategy improves on recent work by combining cloud cover interacted with precipitation—which directly impedes camera-based targeting—with a base closure providing qualitatively different variation. Focusing exclusively on suicide bombings allows us to test specific theoretical mechanisms and provide evidence no existing study offers: quantification of the total programme effect, heterogeneity between decapitation and non-decapitation strikes, and systematic analysis of attack target types. We find each drone strike causes approximately one additional suicide bombing within the subsequent month, with 18%–23% of all suicide bombings during this period causally attributable to the drone programme. Strikes eliminating militant leaders provoke reactions roughly 8 times stronger than ordinary strikes. These reaction attacks predominantly target security installations, not civilians, contradicting theories emphasizing capacity loss or undisciplined violence and instead supporting our proposed mechanism: militant groups strategically “green light” pre-prepared suicide attacks as face-saving responses to authority challenges. We formalize these predictions as four testable hypotheses—blowback effects, decapitation amplification, strategic targeting, and rapid response—all of which receive empirical support. Our findings complement recent evidence that drone strikes increase terrorism and that leadership targeting disrupts organizational control, while providing novel evidence on suicide bombing dynamics, decapitation heterogeneity, and strategic target selection. The results suggest policymakers should anticipate month-long vulnerability windows after strikes, particularly following leadership kills, and harden security targets rather than assume militants will shift to softer civilian targets.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Presenting the Updated Non-State Actor Dataset

David E. Cunningham, Leo Bauer, Olivia Bauer, Kristian Skrede Gleditsch, Idean Salehyan

Full text
This data feature presents an update (Version 4.0) to the Non-State Actor (NSA) data, which provide information on the characteristics of rebel groups in all state-rebel group dyads in the Uppsala Conflict Data Program/Peace Research Institute Oslo Armed Conflict Dataset (ACD). The previous version of the NSA data ended in 2011, and Version 4.0 extends coverage through 2024, adding more than 250 new dyad spells and aligning pre-2011 data with Version 25.1 of the ACD. The added period is a consequential one for the study of conflict: the number of conflicts and conflict fatalities have risen substantially, while international conflict management efforts have declined. We describe the coding process and present descriptive analyses showing marked increases in rebel strength relative to the government and in rebel territorial control over the post-2011 period. We also discuss applications of the data to research on rebel governance and violence against civilians, two areas where scholarship has grown substantially since the data were last updated. The update further allows the range of data projects built on the NSA to be extended, supporting research on how the changing character of contemporary conflict shapes its dynamics and outcomes.

International Studies Review

The Co-optation of Civil Society: How International Organizations Manage Contention

Olivier Nay

Full text
How do international organizations manage social discontent while opening their governance processes to civic actors? This essay conceptualizes co-optation as a mode of contention management through which international institutions selectively integrate critical voices into participatory arrangements that channel, moderate, and reframe dissent in more regulated forms of engagement. Moving beyond rationalist accounts that treat co-optation as a strategic exchange between rulers and challengers, the article develops a relational framework attentive to sustained interactions among bureaucratic agents, civic actors, experts, and intermediaries. It argues that co-optation operates through institutional infrastructures and cross-boundary chains that stabilize cooperation, organize access, socialize civic actors, and expose critical claims to filtering and normative alignment. These chains do not eliminate the agency of co-opted actors. Rather, they make agency part of the process through which critique becomes institutionally acceptable. The article contributes to international studies by showing how international organizations manage contention through relational forms of capture, and how advocacy engagement can help stabilize institutional authority.

Ontological Security Research in International Politics: Assessing the Evolution of a Research Tradition

Amir Lupovici

Full text
Ontological security has become one of the most dynamic and theoretically rich bodies of scholarship in International Relations (IR), guiding research across levels of analysis and diverse domains of world politics. Given its flexibility, inclusiveness, and epistemological and methodological plurality, however, scholars have raised questions about its coherence and analytical distinctiveness. Existing reviews have thoughtfully mapped its richness and scope, but they have been less able to address a central puzzle: How can ontological security research remain theoretically coherent while becoming increasingly plural? This article addresses this puzzle by drawing on Larry Laudan’s philosophy of science. It explains why Laudan’s framework, rather than the more familiar alternatives—Kuhnian paradigms and Lakatosian research programmes—is better suited to capturing this kind of plural scholarly formation. It argues that ontological security scholarship is best understood as a developing research tradition and theory complex: an open and plural formation that connects different theoretical strands, remains anchored in a relatively stable conceptual core, and evolves through empirical and conceptual problem-solving. Viewed through this lens, the plurality of ontological security research should not be read as fragmentation, but as evidence of a productive research tradition whose development depends on synthesizing theories, integrating concepts, and opening new analytical pathways. The article shows that ontological security scholarship contributes to IR by generating alternative accounts of empirical phenomena, connecting levels of analysis, and bridging epistemological approaches. By tracing this development, the article demonstrates why ontological security research remains coherent despite its diversity and how it equips scholars with conceptual tools to pose and address significant questions in world politics. In doing so, it highlights both the overlooked relevance of Laudan’s framework to IR and the broader disciplinary contribution of ontological security research.

Polarising punchlines: The influence of intergroup humour on affective political polarisation

Yara Sleiman, Maria Grasso, Charles Pattie, Laura Serra, Carlo Hofer

Full text
How does political humour influence affective polarisation? In today’s media landscape, humour is increasingly used to ridicule political opponents, potentially deepening partisan divides. At the same time, humour may mitigate polarisation by evoking positive emotions and softening reactions to political critique. To date, it remains unclear whether intergroup political humour affects polarisation differently from equivalent non-humorous content. We address this question using a survey experiment in Great Britain ( n = 2011 ) with a 2×3 factorial design varying in message tone (humorous or non-humorous) and target (in-group, out-group or non-political). We find that serious in-group criticism increases affective polarisation, as measured by trait-based evaluations. Notably, this effect is significantly attenuated when the same messages are delivered humorously. Humour, therefore, reduces polarisation in situations that would otherwise provoke defensive reactions among group members. Mediation analyses provide suggestive evidence that this effect operates through increased message discounting and reduced negative emotional responses.

Gaining confidence – Examining the effects of participation in deliberative mini-publics on self- and other-regarding democratic beliefs

Frederik Pfeiffer, Tim M. Daw, Vera Telemo

Full text
Research on the participation effects of deliberative mini-publics has often focused on political opinions and knowledge, but less is known about the effects on confidence in deliberation and political talk. Given widespread concerns about eroding tolerance and trust in democracy, deliberation should ideally contribute to citizens’ ability to engage in political talk, as well as the confidence that such talk is possible and worthwhile. Following Dahl’s strong principle of equality, we argue that it is important that citizens believe that other people are capable of contributing. Thus, this study asks whether participation in mini-publics fosters confidence in political talk, measured by internal political efficacy, confidence in others’ democratic abilities and tolerance for opposing views. Using before–after surveys from three Swedish mini-publics in 2024, we find significant increases in participants’ confidence in their own abilities and those of co-participants, despite high baseline values. Effects on perceptions of people in general and tolerance for disagreement were mixed. When comparing cases, we find stronger effects when participants had more deliberation time and when the issue of the assembly was more politicised. The findings suggest that mini-publics can enhance confidence in political talk, depending on their design, duration and context.

Does policy align with the preferences of the affluent because they are better informed? An empirical test

Sirus Håfström Dehdari, Jesper Lindqvist, Anders Sundell

Full text
Research shows that affluent citizens see more of their preferred policies realized than the less affluent. If indicative of unequal influence over decisions, then this poses a problem for democratic equality. But one theory suggests that the affluent are better informed and therefore prefer policies that were going to be implemented anyway. If so, it would be a question of unequal information, not influence. We test this theory on the case of monetary policy in the United Kingdom, where information effects are especially likely: The policy-maker (the Bank of England) has a well-defined goal of price stability, there is broad expert agreement on how to achieve this, and the general public is not well informed of inflation dynamics. Survey data from more than 120,000 UK respondents from 2001 to 2026 reveal that monetary policy aligns more with the preferences of high-income earners, who are better informed about monetary policy and the state of the economy. Nevertheless, information can only explain about a quarter of the gap in preference–policy alignment between income groups, even in this most-likely setting. The unequal policy alignment identified in previous research is therefore unlikely to be a mere artifact of informational asymmetry and remains an important problem for political equality.

International Theory

Collective intentionality in international relations: a reductionist view

Moritz S. Graefrath, Marcel Jahn

Full text
The concept of collective intentionality has come to play an increasingly prominent role in the theoretical study of international relations (IR). In particular, scholars regularly treat two kinds of collectives – states and groups of states – as forming intentions and engaging in the joint pursuit of shared goals. In this piece, we intervene in the burgeoning IR debate on the ontology underlying this practice by making the case for a reductionist view of state intentions and joint interstate action. Drawing on Michael Bratman’s work on social ontology and original supporting arguments, we develop a sophisticated, non-summative reductionist account which overcomes many of the theoretical concerns that previously led scholars to reject reductionism as a viable ontological view within IR. Our account promises to advance both the empirical and normative study of international politics in several ways: it entails guidelines on how to study the origins of foreign policies and grand strategies; it lays the groundwork for novel views on interstate cooperation as it takes place in formats such as coalitions of the willing or security concerts; and it provides a promising framework for assessing collective moral responsibility and its relation to individual responsibility.

International Studies Quarterly

The Weight of the Law: How International Law Affects Foreign Policy Preferences

Janina Dill, Benjamin A Valentino

Full text
Studies have shown that international law can influence attitudes toward foreign policy. We know less about how law exerts this influence. We develop four theoretical models to explain how international law affects foreign policy attitudes. Drawing on survey experiments with 4,900 US citizens about arming a foreign government involved in a civil war, we find some support for three of the models: When respondents are told that sending arms would be illegal, they are more likely to believe that the United States would (1) face sanctions, (2) break a commitment, and (3) contribute to harmful moral outcomes. We also examine whether international law amplifies these concerns beyond the effects they might have without international law. We find that sanctions reduce support more strongly when they result from violating international law than when they result from ordinary political retaliation by other states. However, illegality does not strengthen the effects of violating commitments or causing harmful moral consequences. This suggests that international law primarily shapes public opinion indirectly. Rather than serving as an independent reason to oppose a policy, violations of law draw attention to moral and practical concerns that citizens might otherwise overlook.

Making Sense of Disrupted International Order

Jeff D Colgan

Full text
The apparent turmoil in the US-led liberal international order has left international relations scholars without a settled framework for explaining—or teaching—global governance. This Special Section addresses that gap by developing subsystems theory as an analytical approach for understanding change and continuity in an interdependent, contested international order. Subsystems—collections of state and nonstate actors organized around a single core governance question—offer a more disaggregated unit of analysis than the “issue areas” central to regime theory, allowing analysts to identify variation in strategic benefits and punishments for noncompliance that issue-area-level frameworks obscure. We situate subsystems theory alongside other post-regime-theory approaches, particularly regime complexity and weaponized interdependence. We argue that subsystems theory offers complementary analytical leverage, especially for tracing de facto cross-issue interactions and nonstate behavior. The Section’s three articles apply this framework to election monitoring, international finance and antitrust enforcement, and oil sanctions and maritime safety, demonstrating the theory’s utility across strikingly different empirical domains. Read together, the articles illuminate two concepts central to any theory of global governance—punishment and participation—and show how multiple, subsystem-specific international orders, rather than a single liberal order, increasingly characterize world politics.

Economic Statecraft Trumps Ideology: Experimental Evidence on US Sanctions against Chinese Investment

Scott Morgenstern, Francisco Urdinez

Full text
How does US economic statecraft shape public attitudes toward Chinese investment in third countries? As great power competition intensifies, middle powers face increasing pressure to align their economic relationships with geopolitical demands. How these pressures translate into policy depends critically on public opinion, yet we know little about how citizens in targeted countries respond to economic coercion. Using a pre-registered survey experiment conducted in Chile, we provide the first experimental evidence that US sanctions threats significantly reduce support for Chinese investment in a potential target country. Sanctions threats reduce positive evaluations by over 40 percentage points, a dramatic shift that persists across ideological groups, including left-leaning respondents typically skeptical of US influence. These findings suggest that, under conditions of credible economic threat, concrete costs can override abstract geopolitical loyalties, challenging theories that emphasize the primacy of ideological filters in foreign policy preferences. Our results speak to broader debates about economic statecraft effectiveness, demonstrating that threatened costs can reshape public preferences before any economic pain materializes, even among citizens predisposed against the sanctioning power.

Breaking the Conflict Trap: Nonviolent Mobilization and the Recurrence of Civil War

Margherita Belgioioso, Luke Abbs

Full text
We argue that large-scale nonviolent mobilization in post-civil war settings reduces the likelihood of conflict recurrence by diffusing a pluralist political culture and by incentivizing governments to respect political civil liberties, a process conceptualized here as horizontal democratic norm diffusion and vertical democratic norm change, respectively. Using causal mediation analysis and an instrumental variable approach with data from V-Dem and the Phoenix Historical Event Dataset, this study shows that post-conflict nonviolent mobilization lowers the risk of civil war recurrence through its positive effects on societal pluralism and governmental respect for political civil liberties. These findings contribute to the literatures on nonviolent resistance and peacebuilding by demonstrating the peace-promoting potential of nonviolent mobilization in post-civil war contexts and by clarifying the mechanisms through which it influences the recurrence of armed conflict.

The Medieval Peace Dividend: Post-Alfredian Anglo-Saxon State Formation, Political Networks, and Peace

Eric Grynaviski, Jittip Mongkolnchaiarunya, Sverrir Steinsson

Full text
Prominent theories of state formation hold that the origin of the state is rooted in warfare. Heightened insecurity incentivized rulers to develop institutional innovations in taxation, administration, and military organization. Emerging evidence from the medieval world, however, suggests a more complex picture. Early royal governments were tiny and had relatively small resource bases. During the early periods of state formation, existential security threats might divert the kinds of resources needed to enact comprehensive state reform. We refer to this as a security-reform tradeoff. Existential security threats can divert attention and resources away from comprehensive state reforms. We examine patterns of state formation in post-Alfredian Anglo-Saxon England, from the end of the ninth century to the late eleventh century, measuring political centralization using prosopographic data. We find a relationship between periods of peace and political centralization. We examine the causal mechanisms through a comparative case study of four Anglo-Saxon kings’ reigns.

Historical Border Changes and Contemporary Public Health Outcomes

David B Carter, Matthew Gabel, Alma Velazquez

Full text
Trust in government is a key predictor of adherence to government recommendations and accordingly plays a central role in compliance with public health policies. The COVID-19 pandemic underlined the importance of voluntary adherence to government public health recommendations. How trust deficits might be overcome to increase public health compliance depends crucially on its sources. We argue that the legacies of state-building and international politics in Europe are a significant source of variation in contemporary local adherence with public health recommendations. Building on research that links historical border changes to trust deficits in Europe, we show that more border changes in a locality relative to neighboring units affects two public health outcomes in Europe: COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy during the 2021 vaccine rollout and uptake of three established vaccines prior to the pandemic. We show that trust in government mediates the relationship between border instability and uptake of these established vaccines at the local level.

Discrimination in Bureaucratic Recruitment Under Civil Service Exams

Thorbjørn Sejr Guul, Jens Lemb

Full text
Meritocratic recruitment is a central tenet in Weber's theory of bureaucracy, which states that employees should be hired based on merit. However, extensive research has shown evidence of hiring discrimination against minorities, breaking with basic principles in bureaucratic selection. This paper examines whether ethnic and racial minorities are discriminated against when bureaucratic organizations use civil service exams to assess candidates' merit and fitness for positions. Drawing on data from the local government of New York City and a candidate list fixed effects approach, we find that Black and Hispanic candidates are slightly more likely to be hired than White candidates with similar exam scores, suggesting that these ethnic minorities hold a small advantage in bureaucratic selection. However, examining the starting compensation levels of new hires, we show that minorities are hired at lower salaries and are compensated less for high exam scores than comparable White hires. Our findings suggest that civil service exams might be an important lever for securing fair and unbiased bureaucratic selection but also that they do not prevent discrimination in later steps of the recruitment process.

Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory

Sick of change? Sickness absence following ownership conversions in Norwegian kindergartens

Håvard Thorsen Rydland, Dag Arne Christensen

Full text
The expansion of mixed-provider welfare systems has intensified debates about the consequences of public, nonprofit, and for-profit provision. Existing research focuses primarily on efficiency, service quality, and citizens' outcomes, while less is known about ownership conversions’ consequences for employees. We depart from research suggesting that ownership conversions can disrupt employee-organization value congruence as well as change the incentive structures. Leveraging population-wide administrative data from Norwegian childcare services (2015-2019), we examine whether ownership conversions are associated with changes in employee sickness absence. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design, we find that sickness absence increases following nonprofit-to-for-profit conversions. At the same time, public-to-for-profit transitions show little consistent relationship with sickness absence. These findings suggest that employee responses to ownership change are shaped less by the public-private divide than by differences in organizational missions and values. More broadly, the study highlights organizational value congruence as a potential mechanism linking ownership arrangements to employee well-being, and suggests that the nonprofit-for-profit boundary may be more consequential for employees than the conventional public-private divide.

Every Mile Counts: Geographical Distance as Administrative Burden in Intellectual Property Services

Shenghua Lu

Full text
While existing research has examined procedural and informational sources of administrative burden, this paper examines how organizational design choices about the geographic location of administrative authority institutionalize spatial burdens on firms. We leverage China's decentralization reform in trademark registration services as a natural experiment to test this theory. This reform reduced geographic distance by establishing local acceptance windows across the country from 2016 to 2019. Prior to this reform, trademark applications had to be submitted exclusively at the centralized Beijing office, imposing substantial geographic barriers on firms nationwide. Using a unique dataset covering over 37 million trademark applications from 294 Chinese cities, we employ a staggered difference-in-differences approach to estimate the causal effects of reduced geographic distance on trademark filings. We find that establishing local acceptance windows increased trademark applications by approximately 4.4 percent, or roughly 209,000 additional filings in 2019 alone. Moreover, the reform generated compound distributional outcomes: small and medium-sized enterprises responded more strongly than larger firms, and the new applicants drawn into the trademark system were concentrated in cities with thinner infrastructure. Mechanism analysis confirms that this increase is primarily driven by distance reduction rather than improvements in administrative efficiency or reductions in corruption. The findings provide large-scale causal evidence on the behavioral consequences of redistributing administrative service delivery across space, demonstrating that physical accessibility remains crucial for equitable service uptake even amid the digital transformation of administrative services.

Dueling Political Fictions and Public Administration and Democracy in an Era of Populism and Authoritarian Impulses

Michael W. Spicer

Full text
The political fictions used in political discourse, during the English Civil War period, remain relevant to contemporary public policy and administration. Mainstream public administration discourse has too often employed the fiction of a singular and unbounded popular sovereignty that emerged during this period, leaving public administration ill‐equipped to deal with the authoritarian impulses of contemporary populist governments. What is needed in public administration discourse, in dealing with these authoritarian impulses, is not an ethic of administrative neutrality, such as has traditionally dominated much of our field, but an ethic of constitutionalism that draws on the fiction of the individual that was also articulated during the English Civil War period by the English Levellers and subsequently by the founders of the American Constitution.

Population Aging and Citizen Preferences for Automation and Immigration: A Cautionary Tale for Advanced Aging Economies?

Sijeong Lim, Seiki Tanaka

Full text
Extensive research highlights public apprehension toward immigrants and robots, but few studies examine how demographic challenges, particularly population aging, influence these perceptions. The public may not naturally consider population aging when evaluating immigration or automation policies, as these issues are often discussed in silos. We thus hypothesize that priming the public to consider population aging will lead them to view automation and immigration more favorably. Using pre‐registered survey experiments in three countries—Japan, South Korea, and France—we investigate whether providing factual information about population aging alters attitudes toward immigration and automation. We find that in Japan, the world's most aged country, such information generates more favorable attitudes toward both immigration and automation. In Korea, the fastest‐aging country, it improves only immigration attitudes. In France, where the elderly population is average for the EU, we find no significant effect. We find, however, that these positive effects in Japan and Korea do not persist when respondents consider automation and immigration policies alongside other options like adjusting the retirement age. These findings suggest that the public's policy preferences in response to population aging are subject to country‐specific contexts, as well as to different issue linkages activated in policy discussions.

Lobbying Priorities in Agenda‐Setting: Agenda Breadth, Direction, and Responsiveness Across Economic and Citizen Groups

Frederik Stevens, Darren Halpin

Full text
Interest groups are key actors in shaping political agendas. Yet, we know surprisingly little about how they determine their lobbying priorities at the earliest stage of the policy process. This study advances a novel conceptual framework for studying interest groups' issue prioritization in the agenda setting stage along three complementary dimensions: agenda breadth, agenda direction, and agenda responsiveness. Drawing on original survey data from 630 EU level interest groups, our results show that issue prioritization is driven primarily by the type of constituents a group represents. Economic interest groups tend to pursue broader lobbying agendas, focus on defending the policy status quo, and are more responsive to internal member pressures when prioritizing undesired issues. Citizen groups, by contrast, typically adopt narrower agendas, prioritize challenging the status quo, and respond more strongly to external issue salience when determining their lobbying priorities. Overall, the study advances our understanding of how interest groups navigate competition for political attention and contributes to broader debates on agenda‐setting in contemporary democracies.

Innovation in judicial administration has proceeded slowly for many reasons. The attitudes of judges not‐inclined by training toward management, the tradition of Judicial independence, and the separation of powers are examined as contributing factors inhibiting judicial modernization. Federal Judicial reform has defended historically upon the leadership of a Chief Justine of the United State? willing to use the office to dramatize and promote the issues. The most recent period of dramatic change in judicial administration, from 1969‐1981, is reviewed with a focus upon the strategies for change employed by Chief Justice Warren E, Burger.

Climate, Nature, and the Uneven Expansion of Financial Governance

Paola D'Orazio

Full text
Financial authorities have developed extensive climate‐related policies, but nature‐related financial policy remains less widespread. Using data on individual policy instruments across 116 jurisdictions from 2000 to 2025, this paper compares the adoption, policy areas, binding status, and source of climate‐ and nature‐related policies. Nature‐related policies appear in 27 jurisdictions, compared with 60 for climate‐only policies. National nature adoption is positively associated with a larger existing body of climate and environmental financial policies, mainly through the broader environmental component rather than climate‐specific policy. Climate‐only policies are used more frequently in prudential supervision and credit allocation than policies that combine nature with climate or broader environmental concerns. Nature‐related policies have a higher mandatory share over the full period but a lower share than climate‐only policies in 2023–2025. In the European Union, broad EU‐level coverage coexists with limited national issuance. The paper contributes a common framework for comparing climate and nature policies and distinguishes EU‐level coverage from national policy development. The results show that the climate‐nature gap concerns not only adoption, but also where policies are used, how binding they are, and which level of authority issues them.

How the European Union Fell Out of Love With Open‐Source Software

Nora von Ingersleben‐Seip

Full text
This article explains why the European Commission diluted the 2004 European Interoperability Framework's provisions favoring open‐source software and open standards when it revised the framework in 2010. Using an extended Multiple Streams Framework with a technology stream, the study shows how pro‐openness policy entrepreneurs secured the 2004 provisions and why incumbent advocacy became more effective during revision. Drawing on annotated drafts and expert interviews, the study traces actors' lobbying strategies across the problem, politics, and policy streams and examines how technology‐stream developments altered the feasibility and political salience of competing solutions. The analysis shows how changing standards and licensing dynamics, incumbent advocacy, and declining political support for the earlier pro‐openness package enabled a new coupling around business‐model neutrality and licensing flexibility, diluting the 2004 provisions. Beyond the European case, the study informs debates on open‐source artificial intelligence and advances theory on institutional dynamics in regulatory governance.

Form Before Capability? AI Governance Adoption, Functional Capability, and the Limits of Index‐Based Evidence in the Global South

Anis Ben Brik

Full text
Governments across the developing world have adopted artificial intelligence governance instruments at high speed, though the relationship between formal adoption and the capability to operate those instruments remains poorly understood. This article asks whether the expansion of AI governance architecture across the Global South has been matched by the functional capabilities on which regulatory effectiveness depends, and what explains the answer. Drawing on panel data for 82 countries across five Global South regions and six waves of the Government AI Readiness Index, it reports that within the period of stable index architecture (2020–2024) measures of regulatory form rose by 20.3 points on a 0–100 scale while measures of functional capability rose by 0.4 points, a within‐country differential present in 90% of sample countries and surviving removal of the single step‐scored indicator of national strategy adoption. The pattern of the differential discriminates among explanations: formal adoption rose four times faster in states in the top quartile of administrative capacity than in the bottom quartile, where capability fell; the most aid‐dependent states adopted least; and the differential is unrelated to regime type. This is inconsistent with the view that legislation is simply quicker to produce than infrastructure and with the view that adoption is decoupled from performance for external audiences, and consistent with adoption conditioned on administrative capacity, in which capability in the weakest states erodes while capability in stronger states grows more slowly than the instruments that depend on it. Vietnam and India are developed as confirming and deviant cases. Cross‐national dispersion widened on both dimensions, and patterns that appear only when the 2025 wave is included cannot be separated from the redesign of the index in that year.

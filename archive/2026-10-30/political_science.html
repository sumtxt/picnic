When Politics Enters the Waiting Room: Far-Right Electoral Victories Exacerbate Discrimination in Access to Healthcare

MERLIN SCHAEFFER, ALESSIO ROMARRI, DINA ROSENBERG, KRZYSZTOF KRAKOWSKI

Full text
Far-right political shifts have been linked to increased racism, raising concerns about their impact on minority access to public services. This study investigates whether far-right electoral victories encourage discrimination by street-level bureaucrats, who are expected to act impartially. We conducted a nested research design in Italy, combining a field-experimental audit with a regression discontinuity design focused on municipalities near the electoral threshold for far-right mayors. Research assistants with native-Italian and West-African accents contacted municipal healthcare offices to request registration with a general medical practitioner. Compared to native-Italian callers, those with West-African accents faced significantly higher rates of scrutiny and dismissive responses. Leveraging close elections, we find that this disparity was exacerbated in municipalities where far-right candidates narrowly won. These findings demonstrate that far-right electoral success can undermine impartiality and equal access to essential services.

Economic Self-Interest and Support for Redistribution: New Evidence Using Election Results Data

JIEUN LEE, E. GRANT BALDWIN, G. AGUSTIN MARKARIAN, BENJAMIN J. NEWMAN

Full text
A formidable corpus of scholarship explores the role of economic self-interest in shaping public support for redistribution. One striking feature of this literature is its near-exclusive reliance on reported preferences in surveys, with little-to-no research analyzing revealed preferences in the form of actual voting on redistribution. Leveraging eight redistributive ballot initiatives across six American states and a total of 73,053 observations of revealed support for redistribution, we uncover consistent evidence that the economic standing of precinct voters is systematically related to their support for redistribution. We find that lower- to upper-middle-income precincts are each significantly more supportive of redistribution than upper-income precincts, with pronounced decreases in support associated with the increasing prevalence of high-income households subject to proposed tax increases. Finally, we observe the largest class differential in support for redistribution in more heavily Republican precincts.

Globalization and the Hinterland: International Trade and the Rural–Urban Divide

EDWARD D. MANSFIELD, OMER SOLODOCH

Full text
The growing political divide between rural and urban regions is often attributed to rural deindustrialization and import competition in manufacturing. Yet this cleavage emerged in many countries well before rural industrialization, pointing to underlying economic forces that existing accounts have overlooked. We argue that comparative advantage in agriculture is one such force: rural voters are more likely to support protectionist parties and policies when local agriculture is uncompetitive, since this sector is often central to their communities’ economic and social life. Using a new harmonized dataset of surveys from 123 countries and extensive data on voting behavior in the European Union, we find strong support for our argument. An additional analysis exploiting exogenous subnational variation in agricultural competitiveness in the United States post-NAFTA further corroborates our findings: the political divide between rural and urban voters is pronounced in uncompetitive regions but negligible in those possessing a comparative advantage in agriculture.

Generalizing Causal Effects with Noncompliance: Application to Deep Canvassing Experiments

Zhongren Chen, Melody Huang

Full text
Standard approaches in generalizability often focus on generalizing the intent-to-treat effect. However, in practice, a more policy-relevant quantity is the generalized impact of an intervention across compliers. While instrumental variable methods are commonly used to estimate the complier average causal effect (CACE) within samples, standard approaches cannot be applied to a target population with a different distribution from the study sample. This article makes several key contributions. First, we introduce a new set of identifying assumptions in the form of a population-level exclusion restriction that allows for identification of the target CACE (T-CACE) in both randomized experiments and observational studies. This allows researchers to identify the T-CACE without relying on standard principal ignorability assumptions. Second, we propose a class of inverse-weighted estimators for the T-CACE and derive their asymptotic properties. We provide extensions for settings in which researchers have access to auxiliary compliance information across the target population. Finally, we introduce a sensitivity analysis for researchers to evaluate the robustness of the estimators in the presence of unmeasured confounding and extend existing tests to evaluate instrument validity in this context. We illustrate our proposed method through extensive simulations and a study evaluating the impact of deep canvassing on reducing exclusionary attitudes.

American Journal of Political Science

Learning traps

Hassan Sayed

Full text
When constituents are uncertain about whether leaders' policy preferences align with their interests, policy choices generate a trade‐off between payoffs and information. Leaders can experiment with their preferred policy, but if the outcome is bad, constituents learn this and threaten office removal. I use a repeated model of political agency to analyze how this trade‐off shapes long‐lived, term‐unlimited leaders' policy choices. When there is uncertainty about the optimal policy for constituents, leaders implement “learning traps”: spatially intermediate policies that halt learning about what the best policy is. They experiment only when their preferences are very likely to be aligned with constituents, a contrast to the “gambling for resurrection” logic ubiquitous in the literature. I provide a case study that examines how the 1861 reforms ending Serfdom in Imperial Russia represented the logic of a “learning trap” and illustrate additional applications of the model to political contagion and economic policymaking.

The threat‐enhancing effect of authoritarian power sharing

Jack Paine

Full text
Power‐sharing deals can potentially solve the autocrat's commitment problem, yet often fail to prevent conflict. This article develops a dynamic model to examine a largely overlooked friction: Sharing power within an authoritarian regime bolsters the opposition's coercive capabilities. The resultant threat‐enhancing effect creates two distinct commitment problems for the opposition, which determine if and when power sharing occurs. First, the opposition cannot commit to refrain from leveraging its enhanced threat. The ruler prefers to incur a revolt than peacefully share power if the threat‐enhancing effect is large. Second, the threat‐enhancing effect tempts the opposition to wait for a future power‐sharing deal, which undercuts the opposition's commitment to revolt today. New dynamic implications arise: delayed power sharing, conflict, or an increased willingness for the ruler to share power are all possible. Scrutinizing the opposition's limited commitment ability contrasts with the predominant existing focus on the autocrat's commitment problem.

National Narratives in Comparative Politics: Theories, Methods, and Insights From Asia

Dean Dulay, Eun A Jo, Paul Schuler

Full text
In this introduction to the special issue, we define and explore the concept of national narratives in East and Southeast Asia. First, we define national narratives as collectively shared stories about a nation’s origins, boundaries and purposes. Second, we discuss three undertheorized elements of the narrative: the identity of the storyteller, the content of the stories, and contestation over the dominant narrative. Finally, we assess some methodological and measurement challenges in the literature before introducing the papers in this special issue. Drawing on cases from China, Vietnam, Indonesia, South Korea, Taiwan, Myanmar and the Philippines, we address questions related to the construction of personality cults and colonial memory. We also discuss how democratization impacts the identity of the storytellers, and how this impacts views on national boundaries and political violence.

State-Building at What Cost? The Consequences of Military Control in Medieval China

Joy Y. Chen, Erik H. Wang

Full text
A key challenge to state-building in conflict-prone societies is the proliferation of autonomous armed actors. As governments seek to enhance their monopoly over violence, consequences of military centralization deserve scholarly attention. We argue that centralization undermines state’s coercive capacity: by weakening the leaders of armed groups, it simultaneously erodes the military efficiency and discipline of these groups, which also serve as agents of coercion for the state. We leverage a centralization reform in China’s Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE) after a civil war, using unique data on soldier mutinies, rebellions by local generals, civilian uprisings, and battle outcomes. Difference-in-differences estimates reveal that centralization successfully reduced local generals’ rebellions, but led to increased soldier mutinies against the generals and more civilian uprisings. It also worsened battlefield performance. While research on state capacity has focused on its extractive and administrative dimensions, these findings deepen our understanding about another important aspect: the coercive dimension.

Bulwarks or Enablers? Political Elites’ Attitudes to Political Candidates with Armed Group Linkages

Juan Diego Duque-Salazar, Hanne Fjelde, Sandra Håkansson

Full text
Many elections feature candidates with ties to violent groups, yet little is known about the nomination stage. To understand whether political elites serve as gatekeepers against such candidates or whether other desirable traits lead selectors to overlook violent linkages, we study selectors’ preferences in candidate selection. Using an experiment with 11,392 Colombian local political elites, we examine how alleged armed group collusion is weighed against experience, track-records, popular support, and endorsements. We find that candidates with rumored armed group ties face a significant penalty in selectors’ evaluations. Some attributes, particularly experience, reduce this penalty, but none fully offset it. Candidates with ties to armed groups are also less rewarded for strong performance, suggesting that violent linkages diminish returns to otherwise desirable qualities. These findings indicate that the prevalence of violence-linked candidates is not primarily driven by selectors’ willingness to accommodate them, redirecting attention toward concealed linkages and constrained candidate pools.

Incubating Revolution: How Social Networks and the Built Environment Sustain Mobilization

Motasem Abuzaid, Kevin Mazur

Full text
How do challengers sustain mobilization under conditions of extreme repression? Social networks and the built environment are critical, interacting at two time scales. Settlement, chain migration, and everyday cooperation produce dense relational environments that shape motivations and constitute latent resources. When contention erupts, those dense social networks can be repurposed to sustain high-risk collective action, providing shelter, escape routes, information, and support. But their efficacy is limited without the organic built environment in which they form; its proximity, limited visibility, and constrained access amplify the effect of dense social ties in facilitating challenge under threat. We substantiate these claims using disaggregated data on protest in Damascus during the 2011 Syrian uprising, finding that neighbourhoods with both dense networks and organic built form exhibit significantly more frequent and confrontational mobilization. By analytically separating the social from the spatial, this article illuminates the mechanisms through which urban neighbourhoods become incubators of rebellion.

Trade-Offs in Authoritarian Participatory Digital Surveillance: Evidence From China’s Sharp Eyes Project

Ziwen Zu

Full text
How do authoritarian regimes overcome the limits of digital surveillance? They complement camera systems with citizen monitoring and reporting, creating participatory surveillance in which residents interpret what cameras capture and supply local knowledge technology alone cannot provide. I study this hybrid form of control in China’s Sharp Eyes Project, which gives selected residents access to camera feeds and encourages reports of suspected misconduct. I use a staggered difference-in-differences analysis of administrative and survey data from Chinese cities. The program increases citizen reporting by 11%, reduces crimes susceptible to community monitoring, and lowers trust in neighbors. Reports expand the state’s informational reach, but the system makes residents uncertain about how neighbors may interpret and report their behavior. The study places political participation at the center of digital surveillance and shows how authoritarian regimes turn relations among citizens into a source of state power while weakening the foundations of collective action.

British Journal of Political Science

Geoengineering, Moral Hazard, and Disrespect

Alice Baderin, Maxime Lepoutre

Full text
Scientists and policy makers are increasingly exploring solar geoengineering as a tool for combatting anthropogenic climate change. Yet these technologies have drawn intense ethical criticism. One of the most influential critiques – the ‘moral hazard’ objection – holds that pursuing solar geoengineering risks undercutting mitigation efforts; and that, as a result, governments should halt the development of solar geoengineering. This paper argues that the moral hazard objection faces a dilemma: either the proposed solution to the moral hazard effect (that is, stifling geoengineering) is unnecessary, or it expresses a morally troubling form of disrespect. Articulating this dilemma serves three purposes. It shows, first, that the moral hazard objection is substantially more difficult to vindicate than it initially appears. Second, it clarifies what would need to be shown, empirically, to overcome this ethical hurdle. Finally, and crucially, it sheds light, more broadly, on what it means to respect people’s agency when designing and implementing public policies.

Rebuilding Trust in Higher Education: Strategies for Addressing the “Diploma Divide”

Sebastián Royo

Full text
The Trump administration is using all the levers of executive power to intervene in universities, including multimillion-dollar cuts, investigations into federal regulation violations, visa withdrawals for international students, and ideological pressures. The outcome of this fight over the purpose of higher education could shape the sector for decades. This reflection essay argues that these attacks have been facilitated by a decade-long loss of public trust in higher education. It addresses two main questions: Why have colleges and universities lost the support of society, and what should they do to restore public trust? The essay argues that this loss of support is driven primarily by rising costs, perceived ideological bias, and questions about degree value. By situating these discussions within scholarship on polarization, populist backlash, and the “diploma divide,” this essay moves beyond causes to explore sector-specific solutions. These include improving educational access, redefining political messaging, demonstrating nonpartisanship, supporting their communities, redefining diversity to include social class, and bridging the cultural divide to protect the university as a democratic institution.

Possessive Familialism and the Crisis of Democracy in the United States

Tamara Metz, Nancy J. Hirschmann

Full text
Amid rising concern over a crisis of democracy in the United States, one point of political alignment across the political spectrum stands out: valorization of the family. This is especially striking because it would seem to conflict with the core ethos of the dominant neoliberal political rationality. We propose that both convergence and crisis are fueled by a logic of possessive familialism. Taking up recent feminist, Marxist, and critical analyses of neoliberalism to situate our claim and tracing the genealogy of this thinking to John Locke, we argue that possessive familialism more aptly describes foundational commitments and assumptions of liberal, and now neoliberal, politics. At the core of the governing rationality of our time, the solo, self-sufficient family obscures the realities of human interdependence and fuels capitalist extraction and accumulation. Feeding the fantasy that individuals, families, and polities survive—and even thrive—absent cross-cutting, overlapping nests of social supports, possessive familialism is the linchpin of neoliberal political rationality that starves democratic imagination, culture, and structures, and fuels the current crisis. We identify five elements of possessive familialism; illustrate its operations through several contemporary examples, including the case for marriage equality and the COVID-19 pandemic; and show how it threatens the very conditions of democratic politics in the US.

Gender inclusivity and perceptions of party ideology: Observational and experimental evidence

James Adams, David Bracken, Noam Gidron, Will Horne, Seonghui Lee, Diana O’Brien, Lie Philip Santoso, Kaitlin Senk, Randolph T. Stevenson

Full text
Do citizens use gender-based heuristics to infer political parties’ ideological positions? We posit that citizens view more ‘gender inclusive’ parties as more left-leaning than comparable male-dominated organizations. Cues about gender inclusivity, moreover, come not only from women’s presence in parties’ legislative delegations but also from other visible signals of women’s incorporation into parties – including as leaders and supporters – and through organizational rules promoting women’s inclusion. We present analyses of election survey data from 21 Western publics over time, which show that, controlling for the left–right tones of parties’ manifestos, citizens perceive more gender-inclusive parties as more left-wing and that the cumulative impact of the various dimensions of gender inclusivity is substantial. We next report experimental evidence from four countries, which provides further support for gender-based heuristics. Our findings highlight the importance of not just the message but also the messenger in shaping citizens’ assessments of parties’ ideological perceptions.

Contested terrain: How attitudes toward gender issues relate to voting behavior

Melanie M. Dietz, Sigrid Roßteutscher

Full text
While research increasingly links sexist attitudes to far-right support, public debates have moved well beyond women’s equality to encompass LGBTIQ+ rights. Yet studies have been slow to follow, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of attitudes toward trans, intersex, and non-binary people and how they associate with party choice, especially beyond far-right support. Drawing on original data from the German Social Cohesion Panel (2022/23), we are, to our knowledge, among the first to investigate a broader range of gender-related attitudes and find, first, that these constitute two empirically distinct dimensions, binary and non-heteronormative gender issues , that while conceptually intertwined, warrant separate examination. Second, while support for binary gender issues is widespread, non-heteronormative issues remain highly contested. Third, these dimensions relate differently to vote choice: attitudes toward binary gender issues show partisan sorting across the left-right spectrum, while attitudes toward non-heteronormative gender issues reveal a specific antagonism between the Greens and (center-)right parties. Finally, considering both dimensions together uncovers an asymmetry: voters of (center-)right parties form a cohesive conservative bloc, while supportive voters with different attitudinal profiles disperse across (center-)left parties.

Beliefs About Public Opinion and the Perceived Legitimacy of Policy Decisions

Nils D. Steiner, Claudia Landwehr

Full text
Democratic legitimacy is typically thought to depend on whether political decisions reflect public opinion, or majority preferences. Do citizens share this standard? And what if citizens are wrong about public opinion? We study how beliefs about public support for controversial policy proposals are associated with the perceived legitimacy of adopting them, and whether accurate polling information changes these judgments. In a representative survey experiment in Germany, respondents reported their own position on four such proposals, estimated public support, and rated the legitimacy of adopting each one. For a random subset of proposals, respondents received accurate polling information showing that the proposal lacked majority support. We find that perceived public support predicts legitimacy judgments even after accounting for respondents’ own policy preferences. Yet these perceptions are often inaccurate and shaped by respondents’ own views. Corrective polling information modestly lowers perceived legitimacy, especially among those who initially overestimated public support. The findings suggest that misperceptions of public opinion can distort, but accurate information can also partly correct, citizens’ assessments of democratic legitimacy.

What Drives Voter Bias Against Muslim Politicians? An Experimental Examination

Henning Finseraas, Rino Heim

Full text
Religious and ethnic minorities remain underrepresented in political office, but its reasons are debated. Using administrative data from Norwegian local elections, we show that candidates with Muslim names receive fewer personal votes, and propose that this is (partly) due to political beliefs about Muslims as politicians.To examine this claim, we identify salient political beliefs and implement a conjoint experiment that estimates anti-Muslim bias with and without information on these beliefs.In a low-information setting, respondents exhibit a substantial anti-Muslim bias, but providing belief-relevant information reduces this anti-Muslim bias by 2–5 percentage points. The largest reduction occurs when respondents learn candidates’ positions on minority-rights issues. This reduction is driven by right-wing voters and is large enough to eliminate the left-right gap in anti-Muslim bias. These findings show a strong political component in the anti-Muslim bias.

Public Opinion Quarterly

Do Gender-of-Interviewer Effects Differ Across Immigrant Generations? Survey Evidence from Italy

Christina Sarah Hauser

Full text
Interviewer gender has been shown to affect responses to survey questions on gender attitudes and norms, but does its influence vary across groups with different migration backgrounds? I study this question using the Between Origin and Destination (BOnD) survey, conducted in Lombardy, Italy, between 2024 and 2025. Consistent with gender-of-interviewer effects, a type of social desirability bias, female interviewers elicit more progressive responses on a summary index of gender norms and on an index of the acceptability of homosexuality, abortion, divorce, casual sex, and premarital sex. The effect is concentrated among natives and second-generation individuals; first-generation immigrants show little response to interviewer gender. One possible explanation is that social desirability pressure from a female interviewer operates through host-country social norms: Individuals more embedded in the Italian normative context are more responsive to cues from a female interviewer. These findings have implications for the design of surveys administered to mixed native-immigrant populations.

Investigating the Utility of LLM-Based “Silicon Samples” in Political Science Conjoint Experiments

Alice Malmberg

Full text
This research note is the first study to explore the viability of using LLM-generated respondents in forced-choice conjoint experiments. I generate naive “silicon samples” using three models, GPT 4o-Mini, Llama 3.2, and Magistral-small-2506, benchmarked to a large sample of live conjoint respondents, allowing for direct comparisons between human and silicon choices. The resulting analyses suggest that naively generated silicon samples are insufficient to simulate human decision-making in conjoint tasks; these results also suggest that there are systemic differences in the way demographic covariates shape LLM and human choices in experimental contexts. In total, this analysis makes two important contributions: it establishes a descriptive baseline understanding of LLM-generated respondent performance in conjoint experiments and suggests that naive silicon samples cannot accurately infer human choices using demographic traits in experimental decision making contexts. Finally, the implications of these results imply that LLMs consider covariates differently than live respondents do in micro-level decision making contexts.

Do felon voting rules affect criminal prosecutions?

Hannah Simpson, Sidak Yntiso

Full text
Felon disenfranchisement laws can alter the composition of the electorate. Accordingly, we argue that in states with such laws, elected prosecutors may be tempted to assign felon status strategically. We test our argument using criminal case data from Virginia and leveraging a sudden policy change—unexpectedly announced by the Governor on April 22, 2016—enfranchising all felons upon completion of their prison or probation sentence. Using well-known racial differences in partisanship as a measure of likely political alignment, we show that the announcement corresponded to an increase in criminal charge severity and sentence length for defendants who were politically unaligned with their prosecutors, and a decrease for aligned defendants. Evidence suggests that these changes may have been partially motivated by partisan concerns in the lead-up to the 2016 federal elections.

Polity size and women’s representation: evidence from two large-scale boundary reforms

Moa Frödin Gruneau, Jan Teorell, Kåre Vernby

Full text
Do larger polities hinder gender parity in elected office? We theorize that when populations grow faster than elected assemblies, a “seat congestion” effect intensifies competition for nominations, enabling men candidates to crowd out women. We test this argument using two nationwide natural experiments: Swedish municipal amalgamations in 1952 and 1973 that abruptly increased electorates. We track the effects in newly digitized historical panel data. Where electorate-per-seat ratios rose, growth in women’s representation slowed. Decomposition analyses show that expanding councils only partially offsets congestion. By identifying polity size as a systematic brake on descriptive representation, the study adds a demographic dimension to debates on institutional design and points to an overlooked cost of increasing jurisdictional size.

Putting your money where your mouth is: Expressions of Chinese nationalist sentiments are not just cheap talk

Charles Chang, Michael Masterson

Full text
Do nationalist expressions reflect a willingness to pay costs or are they cheap talk? This is important because theories expect nationalism affects a wide range of outcomes that pose costs to participants, including international conflict and participation in boycotts. However, past research has either relied on self‐reports that cannot rule out cheap talk or outcome measures at higher levels of analysis, such as international trade flows, that do not directly link individual‐level nationalist sentiment to behavior. In contrast, we measure both nationalist sentiments and costly behavior on an individual level using the content of millions of Chinese social media posts and the nationality of the brand of users' devices, for example type of cellphone, as a costly behavioral measure. We find that nationalist sentiment makes citizens less likely to use US and Japanese brands with an instrumental variable analysis.

Do “warm glow” feelings affect climate mitigation behaviors? A field experiment on consumer electric energy choices

Jennifer Jerit, Jason Barabas, Hwayong Shin, Molly Gahagen, Scott May

Full text
Addressing the climate crisis is going to require behavioral change in many aspects of daily life. A growing literature suggests the promise of “warm glow” messaging that features the pleasure and satisfaction one experiences from acting sustainably. Yet previous research has relied almost exclusively on self‐reported behavioral intentions, leaving open whether such appeals meaningfully affect real‐world, costly choices. We partnered with a local organization to conduct a field experiment on energy choices in Hanover, New Hampshire. Using a randomized roll‐out design in early 2025, we systematically varied the timing of postcard mailers that contain a warm glow message encouraging customers to increase the renewable content of their energy option. Analyses of administrative data on consumer energy choices reveal that warm glow messages delivered in a field setting have a null effect on adoption of higher‐renewable power choices, with a small number of customers changing to the greener but more costly option. Our study provides novel behavioral evidence on the real‐world impact of intrinsic motivation appeals in the climate domain.

Feeling the body politic: Interoception as a mechanism of political stress resilience

Mariana Von Mohr, Manos Tsakiris

Full text
Politics can substantially harm physical and mental health, especially in polarized contexts, making political polarization a major non‐medical cause of disease and suffering. We examined whether citizens can protect their health without undermining civic engagement. Across three studies with over 2000 participants during the 2022 U.S. midterms and 2024 presidential election, we show that interoceptive sensibility, that is, the ability to perceive and trust one's internal bodily signals, protects against politics' adverse health effects. Higher interoceptive sensibility consistently predicted lower emotional, physical, behavioral, and social health costs, controlling for political identity, demographics, and emotion regulation. During the charged 2024 election, it further predicted fewer negative emotions, more hope for the future, and less fear, particularly when their candidate lost. Interoceptive sensibility also correlated with political engagement and efficacy, suggesting it enhances rather than inhibits participation. Our findings highlight it as a promising trait for mitigating political stress without reducing civic involvement.

Philosophy & Public Affairs

What Do Mini‐Publics Prove?

Brian Kogelmann

Full text
Lottocracy is a novel political arrangement that replaces elected politicians with ordinary citizens selected at random. It is unlike any political system that currently exists, so it is natural to have anxiety about how it would work in practice. To mollify these concerns, many lottocrats point to the successful performance of mini‐publics as evidence that lottocracy would work well, if tried. This inference resembles a claim about external validity, I argue, and develop a framework for evaluating it by relying on work from the social sciences and philosophy of science about when such claims are warranted. With this framework in hand, I show that mini‐publics teach us little about how lottocracy would work in practice, although the strength of this conclusion depends on which specific version of lottocracy is under consideration. Mini‐publics, however, do provide evidence about the abilities of ordinary citizens more generally, undermining some of the strongest claims about their lack of political competence. The framework developed here applies to a broader class of arguments in political philosophy: arguments that use the performance of existing institutions to predict how similar but untried institutions would perform.

Research & Politics

“Manifestly unlawful”: How US troops think about the duty to disobey illegal orders

Charli Carpenter, Heather Kumove, Geraldine Santoso

Full text
US troops are required to disobey unlawful orders but obey lawful ones – yet social scientists know very little about how they draw that line or what drives the decision to actually disobey when it counts. We asked 818 active-duty American troops to define “manifestly unlawful orders” in their own words, as well as whether they would obey or disobey specific scenarios in which they might be asked to directly and intentionally target unarmed civilians. A majority of troops could provide clear examples of situations in which they would feel compelled to disobey orders. Of specific scenarios, over a third of US troops were prepared to disobey orders to target civilians, either with nuclear weapons or if asked to shoot civilian protesters. Controlling for gender, age, branch of service, party affiliation, voting behavior, rank, income, and education, we find simply thinking ahead about the concept of “manifestly unlawful orders” is the single biggest predictor of soldiers’ willingness to refuse to harm civilians.

Worth a try? The electoral consequences of symbolic legislation

Nicole Huffman, John V. Kane, David L. Stack

Full text
With the US Congress increasingly gridlocked, fewer bills are becoming law. Nevertheless, members continue to engage in what we call “symbolic legislating”—that is, introducing legislation that has effectively no chance of securing enough votes to become law. Despite its prevalence, little is known about the electoral implications of sponsoring symbolic legislation. It is possible that voters reward their legislator’s efforts to move the status quo closer to their position even if it has no chance of becoming law. On the other hand, because symbolic legislation is doomed to fail, it may decrease a legislator’s perceived effectiveness and, thus, their political support. Alternatively, it is possible that sponsoring symbolic legislation has no impact beyond a cue of the politician’s position. In this Research Article, we test these competing hypotheses using a survey experiment. Our experimental results clearly demonstrate that, assuming shared party and issue position, sponsoring symbolic legislation is strongly rewarded. Moreover, the effect is even stronger among voters who are most likely to attend to this information in the real world and operates similarly across parties.

Politics & Gender

Same Race, Different Paths: Socialization, Orientation, and the Black Partisan Gender Gap

Chryl Laird, Julian Wamble

Full text
Black women have long anchored Black political cohesion, yet little explains why a gender gap persists within a group marked by strong Democratic loyalty. Drawing on historical and sociological work, we argue that Black women’s collective-first orientation—shaped by gendered social roles across enslavement, Jim Crow, and modern civic life—heightens the political significance of the antecedents of Black Democratic partisanship: racial identity salience, perceived discrimination, and racially homogenous social networks. Using an original national survey of 4,200 Black Americans, we find that the partisan gender gap widens because the composition of their social networks more strongly shapes Black women’s political preferences. Accounting for racial identity salience and perceived discrimination substantially reduces the gender gap. These findings illuminate how racialized gender roles structure the pathways through which group-based norms translate into partisan behavior, providing a mechanism for intragroup variation and guiding more nuanced strategies for political engagement and mobilization.

Testifying in Texas: Reflecting on We Testify Texas , a State-based Abortion Storyteller Retreat

Renee Bracey Sherman

Losing the engaged without winning the disengaged: Personalism and the choice of political news

Troels Bøggild, Helene Helboe Pedersen

Full text
When political news centers on the policy work or private lives of individual politicians rather than on parties, it can obscure the collective actors citizens must judge at elections. The usual defense is that such personalism attracts readers, especially those otherwise unlikely to choose political news. We test this claim in survey experiments in Denmark, the United Kingdom, and the United States ( N = 3039). Respondents chose between an entertainment article and a political article whose subheadline attributed a new bill to a party, an individual politician (individualized), or the politician’s personal story (privatized). Personalism recruited no readers. Effects are negative in all countries and pooled amount to a significant five percentage point loss. The losses were concentrated among politically engaged citizens, while the least engaged, the very readers personalism is meant to attract, were unmoved. Party-centered news thus entail no audience sacrifice as citizens prefer the format that keeps parties visible.

Is sidestepping the judiciary seen as democratic? Evidence from Great Britain and Spain

Noam Titelman, Javier Sajuria, Lisa Zanotti

Full text
Executives often bypass the courts without public backlash and, sometimes, with public support. This research note evaluates one strategy employed by politicians for securing public backing when bypassing the courts: a majoritarian framing. We evaluate this strategy through candidate survey experiments in Great Britain ( n = 957) and Spain ( n = 995) that contrast this framing with institutional checks and balances. Causal Attribution and Trust Evaluations in the Multilevel Structure of Civil-Military Relations

Sou Shinomoto

Full text
Public trust in the military and the civilian leaders who control it is central to democratic governance and national security. However, we know relatively little about how such trust is formed. This study examines how citizens update their trust in each actor following military-related events and whether information about the multilevel structure fundamental to democratic civil-military relations influences this process. Existing research has largely relied on performance theories, which assume a straightforward causal link between event outcomes and trust, and on observational data that complicate causal identification. Building on attribution theory, this study refines performance-based accounts by highlighting the multilevel structure. It conducted three survey experiments and a supplementary survey in Japan, a consolidated democracy with a considerable armed force and a population with a relatively strict view on civilian control. The results indicate that event outcomes influence evaluations of civilian and military actors to a similar degree, although underlying mechanisms are asymmetrical depending on the outcome’s direction. Moreover, differing from theoretical expectations, information about the multilevel structure does not substantially alter the effect of outcome information on trust in most cases, although it shapes attributional reasoning. These findings challenge conventional understandings of trust and accountability in democratic civil-military relations.

Steven T. Zech

Full text
The United States seems to be experiencing a volatile period where its core values and institutions have come under threat, accompanied by what many observers identify as a rise in uncivil speech and behavior. Yet, much of this contentious rhetoric and behavior seemingly goes beyond instances of incivility and into something more serious, including threats, intimidation, physical assaults, insurrectionary riots, and assassination. An Analysis of Position-Taking and Position-Avoidance in the U.S. House of Representatives

Jason S. Byers, Laine P. Shay

Full text
Scholars of legislative politics have extensively explored the political factors that incentivize members of Congress to engage in position-taking. This has led to a well-developed body of work focusing on why legislators skip congressional roll-call votes. Current scholarship offers mixed conclusions on whether electoral considerations influence a representative’s likelihood of abstaining from a roll-call vote. To help clarify these mixed findings, we analyze a broader range of electoral forces. In this study, we examine all roll-call abstentions among members serving in the U.S. House of Representatives from the 85th to the 117th Congress. We find that electoral considerations can indeed impact abstention behavior; however, the effect depends on the type of election. Specifically, lawmakers vulnerable in their primary race are more likely to avoid taking a position on a roll-call vote, whereas electoral vulnerability in the general election appears to have little effect. These findings deepen our understanding of how elections influence representation.

Full text
This article explores the role of social movement activists in policy implementation. I introduce the mechanism of interpretation: by influencing how providers interpret policy mandates and apply discretion, or by directly providing services themselves, activists may expand service delivery beyond formal law. Legislators' Constituency Focus Across Electoral Systems

Maiken Røed, Martin Søyland

Full text
Previous studies argue that incentives to cultivate a personal reputation and maximize personal votes make legislators more likely to focus on their constituencies in candidate‐centered than party‐centered systems. Empirical analyses, however, have yielded mixed support for this hypothesis. We provide new tests of the relationship between electoral system characteristics and constituency signaling in speeches, holding individual legislators fixed. We examine speeches given by legislators from England, Scotland, and Wales in the European Parliament (EP) and in the UK House of Commons. This allows us to follow the same legislators (1) across an electoral system change in the EP in 1999 and (2) when they move between the EP and the House of Commons. In line with expectations, we find that constituency signaling in speeches is more likely when the same legislators are elected under candidate‐centered rules.

Full text
While increased decision-making complexity and membership predispose the European Union to protracted negotiations and legislative gridlock, still some legislative proposals are adopted faster than others. What explains this variation? Drawing on models of democratic responsiveness, we argue that politicisation potential – captured by public salience, opinion division, and planned European Union authority expansion – increases legislative duration.

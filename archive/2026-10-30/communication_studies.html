When it’s your own child: effects of media narratives on U.S. parents’ support for their LGBTQ+ teen

Marie-Louise Mares, Y Anthony Chen

Full text
Parents’ responses to their LGBTQ+ child are varied, often intense, and have important consequences for the child. To examine how media depictions may shape responses, a pre-registered online experiment of 494 parents tested effects of viewing two TV narratives, depicting either control (non-LGBTQ+) family interactions, parental rejection, gradual growth toward acceptance, or unalloyed acceptance, of a lesbian or transgender teen, on parents’ support and behavioral intentions regarding their own LGBTQ+ teen. Guided by theories of vicarious contact and inspiring media, we assessed mediation via identification with characters, and via feelings of inspiration and manipulation, testing baseline support as a moderator. Effects of condition were indirect-only, and contingent on baseline support. Among highly supportive parents, growth and acceptance narratives (vs. control) increased feelings of inspiration, which predicted positive behavioral intentions. Among less supportive parents, rejection and (unexpectedly) growth narratives reduced identification with depicted LGBTQ+ teens, indirectly decreasing support and behavioral intentions.

The integrative theory of supportive network (re)structuring: a case study in the context of LGBTQ+ hate speech

John P Crowley, Elizabeth A Hintz, Amanda Denes

Full text
Social support is a fundamental human need and communication process. Yet, scholars theorizing about social support have been limited by several pervasive assumptions: (a) people are able to recruit support when needed, (b) others are willing and able to provide support, (c) what counts as “good” support is mutually understood and desirable, and (d) support seeking is binary and static. Researchers must productively move beyond these assumptions to enhance the depth and breadth of theorizing about the process of support seeking. By addressing these assumptions and consolidating the literature concerning support seeking and marshaling, we set forth the novel, field-general integrative theory of supportive network (re)structuring (ITSNS) grounded in the interpretive paradigm. To accomplish this, we introduce process theorizing, forward three propositions forming the ITSNS’ heuristic framework, and offer criteria for evaluating the ITSNS. Finally, to illustrate the utility of the ITSNS in research practice, we offer a case study in the context of LGBTQ+ people seeking support following an experience of hate speech.

Deceit between Falshoods and Morality: Unpacking Audience Engagement with Disinformation

Sabina Mihelj, Stefan Janjić, Hanna Orsolya Vincze, Andreea Alina Mogoş

Full text
Disinformation is customarily approached through an epistemic lens and defined with reference to intentionally false or misleading information. Yet, this approach struggles to account for how disinformation functions as a communicative practice that acquires meaning through audience interpretation and interaction. To develop a more comprehensive understanding, we draw on the analysis of eighteen focus groups conducted in 2025 in three countries that have been under increased scrutiny internationally due to their vulnerability to pro-Russian disinformation, but received limited attention in existing research: Poland, Romania and Serbia. Focusing on responses to examples of pro-Russian disinformation concerning the Russia-Ukraine war and LGBTQ+ rights, we show that participants judged claims not only based on credibility but also based on moral rightness, as well as engaged in contestations over the boundaries of legitimate controversy. Building on this evidence, we argue that disinformation not only creates uncertainty over how the world is but often invites contestation over how the world should be and over where the boundaries of legitimate public debate should lie. We conclude by reflecting on what these findings mean for scholarly research on disinformation, for counter-disinformation efforts, and for the way we understand the impact of disinformation on democracy.

Exploring the Predictors and Outcomes of Perceived Algorithm Responsiveness in Social Media Feeds: Combining Longitudinal and Computational Methods

Samuel Hardman Taylor, David M. Markowitz

Full text
Perceptions of how algorithms curate content are integral to how people use social media and its effects. Building on the algorithm responsiveness process, we explore whether perceived algorithm responsiveness (PAR) was associated with the message cues of algorithmically curated content. We coupled longitudinal data from a 7-day, twice-daily diary survey with computational digital traces from Twitter (now X) to operationalize algorithmic message characteristics and to understand the antecedents and outcomes of PAR ( N = 10,337 tweets). Results suggested that message cues from algorithmic content in social feeds (i.e., closeness, popularity, valence, and format) were associated with perceived message personalization, but message personalization was the only message characteristic associated with PAR. PAR predicted feeling more connected to others on Twitter, along with message-level factors: closeness to the poster and message personalization. This work contributes to the algorithm responsiveness process, demonstrating that characteristics of algorithmically curated messages predict how individuals experience algorithms.

Do Experts Agree About Misinformation? Evidence From Three Annual Surveys

Sacha Altay, Sebastián Valenzuela, Philip N. Howard

Full text
How much do experts on the information environment agree about the scale, spread, and effects of misinformation? We drew on three annual waves of the International Panel on the Information Environment expert survey to examine agreement with seven popular claims about misinformation (2023: N = 262; 2024: N = 413; 2025: N = 442; spanning 76 countries). Agreement varied widely across statements and was stable across waves. About 60% of experts agreed that falsehoods spread faster than the truth and that misinformation is widespread. About half agreed that misinformation has a substantial influence on behavior and that people readily believe what they see online; about 40% agreed that more people have become misinformed over time; and only 25% agreed that misinformation is primarily a social media problem. The surveys also revealed a persistent divide between experts who saw misinformation as widespread and consequential and those more skeptical, with sharpest disagreement on its behavioral impact and increase over time. In 2025, some experts distinguished between misperception prevalence and misinformation severity. Overall agreement varied by respondent background: agreement was higher among experts on developing countries than those on developed countries, higher among STEM researchers than social scientists, and lower among political scientists than communication scholars. Expert consensus on misinformation is partial, varies across claims and experts, and is shaped by geography and discipline.

Practicing love with algorithms: How online communities narrate and make sense of human–AI intimacy

Fanjue Liu, Haiyan Hao, Kun Xu

Full text
As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes embedded in everyday life, emotionally intimate interactions with AI companions are becoming increasingly visible. Yet prior research has focused mainly on individual experiences, leaving limited understanding of how human–AI intimacy is collectively interpreted and negotiated in online communities. This study examines community discourse on RedNote, a major Chinese social and lifestyle platform, using 13,059 comments from 58 relevant posts. Combining BERTopic modeling with a human–large language model (LLM) hybrid emotion classification procedure, the analysis identifies three thematic clusters: intimacy as co-construction, intimacy as infrastructure, and intimacy as boundary negotiation. Co-construction centers on persona design, roleplay, and narrative world-building and is marked mainly by joy and curiosity. Infrastructure-related discourse is associated with frustration and sadness, while boundary negotiation reflects ambivalence toward intimacy with AI.

Testing Hypotheses From the Social Approval Theory of Online Hate: An Analysis of 110 Million Messages From Parler

David M. Markowitz, Samuel Hardman Taylor

Full text
We examined how social approval motivates online hate via the social approval theory, which argues social approval signals on hate messages predict (1) more hate speech, and (2) more toxicity. Using 110 million messages from Parler (2018–2021), we observed that upvotes on hate speech posts (messages broadcast on users’ profiles to followers’ feeds) predicted a lower probability of hate speech in the next immediate post but more in the following week’s posts. Additionally, upvotes on comments (messages embedded within conversational threads) negatively predicted hate speech in the next comment but positively predicted the degree of toxicity during the next week and month. Between-person effects revealed negative immediate short-term effects but positive longer-term effects for hate speech. For comments , social disapproval (downvotes) moderated these relationships, making them less positive at weekly through quarterly time-intervals and more strongly negative at 6-months. Social approval predicts online hate in time- and message-dependent ways.

Do Political Conversations Persuade or Polarize? Evidence From a Real-Time Randomized Experiment

Omer Ben Simhon, Eran Amsalem

Full text
Political conversations across lines of disagreement are central to democracy. While some individuals approach them as opportunities to learn, others treat them as battles to be won. Theory suggests these contrasting motivations may shape how individuals process information and respond to disagreement, yet their consequences for attitude dynamics during conversations remain unclear. We fill this gap through an experiment ( N = 466) that manipulates motivation in real-time video conversations between pairs of Israeli citizens holding opposing views on Israel–U.S. relations. We financially incentivized some participants to win the debate, others to learn their partner’s perspective, while a control group received no incentive. We find consistent evidence that a winning-oriented mindset reduces the likelihood of persuasion, while a learning-oriented mindset increases it. Notably, there is no evidence that motivation polarizes attitudes. Our findings suggest that a winning-oriented mindset undermines mutual understanding, whereas learning-based dialogue can help bridge political divides.

The Promise of Multistakeholderism: Transnational Influence and Legitimacy at the Internet Governance Forum

Lena Herbst

Full text
Multistakeholderism is widely regarded as a means to ensure equal representation for stakeholders, promote democratic processes, and enable effective governance. Given the interconnected nature of the internet, much of global internet governance is influenced by multistakeholder approaches with a prominent role of transnational actors. This raises questions about the effects of transnational influence on the legitimacy of multistakeholder internet governance. Therefore, this article explores how transnational influence affects the input, throughput, and output legitimacy of the Internet Governance Forum (IGF), a key node within the internet governance ecosystem. The analysis combines qualitative content analysis of 600 statements made during cybersecurity governance debates at the IGF from 2016 to 2023 with 18 expert interviews. The findings reveal that transnational actors affect the IGF's legitimacy unevenly, producing both legitimacy gains and legitimacy deficits. Broad stakeholder participation contributes to high levels of input legitimacy, yet the prominence of specific transnational actors in panel discussions and policy contributions raises concerns regarding throughput legitimacy, reflecting persistent inequalities in access, resources, and visibility. While transnational actors' contributions significantly shape IGF policy outcomes, their influence is disproportionately concentrated among Global North actors. This article contributes to scholarship on transnational influence, the legitimacy of international organizations, and multistakeholderism.

Governing for Digital Resilience: Rethinking Inclusion for WSIS+20 and Beyond

Audrey Yue, Jun Yu, Renae Sze Ming Loh

Full text
At the WSIS+20 juncture, this article asks what conditions enable individuals to participate meaningfully in digital life, and how governance arrangements should be assessed accordingly. Multistakeholder governance has distributed authority across actors, but not necessarily the capacity to shape or respond to digital conditions. We argue that assessing this gap today requires attention to the different forms of power through which these conditions are produced, and to individuals' experiences as evidence of how those governance conditions operate in practice. Building on socio‐ecological resilience theory and the capabilities approach, we reconceptualise digital resilience not as an individual trait but as a process shaped by governance conditions and by the substantive freedom people have to adapt, recover, and participate over time. We then introduce the DRIVE (Digital Resilience Indicators for Veritable Empowerment) framework, an indicator‐based diagnostic architecture spanning six drivers and five levels—individual, familial, communal, platform, and state—that shifts assessment from individual preparedness towards enabling conditions. Using fraudulent advertising on Meta's platforms as an illustration, we show how DRIVE can identify evidenced constraints, evidence gaps, and appropriate sites of remedy. Rather than offering a universal score, DRIVE provides a flexible architecture for context‐specific assessment of the conditions under which digital resilience develops.

The ethical trilemma in refugee protection: equity, sovereignty, autonomy

Eiko Thielemann

Full text
Disparities in the distribution of protection responsibilities are not just unequal but also highly inequitable. Such inequities can be expected to negatively impact both the scope and the quality of protection that states are willing to provide. The paper empirically demonstrates through the analysis of Europe’s responses to forced displacement from former Yugoslavia to Ukraine, that European states have struggled to effectively address the highly inequitable distribution of refugee responsibilities. EU policy makers find it easier to develop symbolic solidarity measures than to establish more substantive policy responses, i.e. they struggle to adopt the kind of cooperation that the public goods literature suggests is necessary to overcome collective action problems. This is due to the fact that substantive refugee solidarity initiatives, such as quota-based relocation, which are promising from an equity perspective pose fundamental challenges to both national sovereignty and the individual autonomy of protection seekers. How future EU policies will seek to address such solidarity dilemmas that lie at the heart of international refugee responsibility sharing efforts will tell us much about its commitment to effective refugee protection.

International Migration Review

High-Skilled Migration Revisited: Foregrounding the Multinational Enterprise in Migration Infrastructures

Aida Hajro, Milda Žilinskaitė

Full text
In this article, we extend migration-infrastructure scholarship by foregrounding multinational enterprises (MNEs) as structural actors that both co-produce high-skilled mobilities (direct effects) and reshape the broader conditions that render such mobilities feasible and attractive (indirect effects). On the direct side, we theorize three interlocking channels through which MNEs shape high-skilled migration: (1) recruiting, hiring, and relocating international talent; (2) generating organizationally mediated migration networks, shaped by senior corporate actors and enacted through international human resource management policies and practices; and (3) reinforcing the spatial concentration of global talent by strengthening clusters around global innovation and business hubs. These mechanisms are connected by a shared organizational logic—knowledge internalization—which links the three direct channels into a coherent account of firm-driven mobility. On the indirect side, MNEs influence the broader possibility space of high-skilled migration by reshaping political, economic, and sociocultural environments through corporate political strategies, global factory dynamics, and processes of forward and reverse diffusion. In this sense, MNEs may transform the contexts that, over time, orient and sustain subsequent high-skilled mobilities. Given their global reach and orchestration capacity, bringing MNEs more squarely into the analytical frame also matters beyond theory. It can inform policies better aligned with the organizational realities shaping contemporary migration.

Beyond the environmental burden narrative: environmental attitudes and green-job aspirations among Syrian refugees and Jordanians

Sijeong Lim, Hannah June Kim, Youcheer Kim, Youngwan Kim

Full text
There is a prevalent view of refugees as an environmental burden, a narrative that can exacerbate strained relations between refugees and host communities. This study examines the empirical basis of this perspective using an original, in-person survey of 510 Syrian refugees and 509 Jordanian nationals across four cities in Jordan: Amman, Mafraq, Irbid, and Zarqa. The findings indicate that environmental attitudes and practices are broadly similar among Jordanians and Syrian refugees. Syrian refugees report modestly higher levels of environmental knowledge, a pattern that may reflect exposure to climate-related risks during war and displacement. In addition, Syrian refugees express greater interest in green jobs than Jordanians do, a pattern that may be associated with their disadvantaged position in the labor market and their search for new employment opportunities. These findings suggest that, under the right institutional design, refugees can contribute to the host nation’s green economy transition.

A Dual Perspective on Community Mobility: Home‐Centric Place Networks and Older Adults' Mental Health in Shanghai During the Pandemic

Felicia F. Tian, Lei Xin, Mengying Zhou, Yuhan You, Chen Yang, Yang Chen

Full text
Older adults' community mobility reflects both spatial and relational aspects of aging in place within urban environments. To capture this duality, we introduce a home‐centric place network framework that links the home to community destinations older adults routinely visit. The spatial dimension is measured using standard movement metrics, while the relational dimension is defined as opportunities for social encounters (sociability) and operationalized as the overlap of neighbors' routine activities across community places. We tested this framework by collecting two waves of GIS‐based mobility data and an online survey from 64 community‐dwelling older adults in Shanghai, a rapidly aging megacity, during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Results showed that older adults' place networks were diverse, but both the frequency of out‐of‐home visits and sociability declined sharply following a major outbreak. Those who were more mobile and sociable before the outbreak experienced greater reductions, and sociability was associated with more negative expectations. As social places became associated with higher infection risk, the pandemic fundamentally altered the relational meaning of community mobility. Overall, our findings demonstrate that a home‐centric place network framework deepens population geography by linking physical mobility, social encounters, and the emotional well‐being of older adults.

Afghan as (Racialized) “Other”: Politics of Farsi Academic Knowledge Production on Migration in Iran

Aryasp Delvarani, Nader Talebi

Full text
This article critically investigates the politics of academic knowledge production on Afghan migration in Iran, posing the question: how does Iranian academia contribute to bordering strategies that marginalize Afghans? Drawing on a qualitative thematic analysis of 114 Farsi academic articles from the Iranian National Library's database—the point at which thematic saturation was reached within an initial corpus of 210 retrieved by keyword search—this study reveals how scholarly discourses construct Afghans as a racialized and securitized “other.” The analysis identifies five recurring themes: (1) measuring security, (2) measuring difference, (3) the pathology of presence, (4) measuring attitudes, and (5) racism and knowledge production on migration. These thematic findings illustrate how Iranian academic discourse problematizes migration, treating it as a deviation that justifies exclusionary practices. By pathologizing and racializing migration, academic narratives align with state‐driven bordering strategies that dehumanize vulnerable populations and normalize systemic inequalities. The study critiques the ethical failures of such exclusionary knowledge production, emphasizing its role in legitimizing state control and reinforcing public hostility toward marginalized groups. By connecting these findings to global debates on migration and the ethics of knowledge production, this work calls for a reflexive approach to studying migration, one that challenges the systemic injustices perpetuated by exclusionary discourses.

Migration and the Geography of Life Expectancy: Selective Sorting and Spatial Dependence Across 172 Countries

Ammar Abulibdeh

Full text
International migration has grown rapidly in recent decades, yet its relationship with population health is difficult to disentangle from the economic, structural and geographic characteristics of the places migrants move to. This study examines whether international migration is associated with population‐level life expectancy after accounting for confounding and the data's spatial structure. Data for 172 countries (189 in the spatial analyses) for 1990–2024 were assembled, combining migrant stock statistics from UN DESA, life expectancy estimates from Gapminder, and economic indicators from the World Bank. The analysis used cross‐sectional and panel data methods (pooled Ordinary Least Squares, country fixed effects, and first differences), spatial lag and spatial error models, spatial panel models, multiscale geographically weighted regression (MGWR), and an origin–destination analysis of bilateral migrant stocks. Migration and life expectancy were positively associated across countries (r = 0.42, p < 0.001), but the cross‐sectional migration coefficient was reduced by 47% after adjustment for GDP per capita, and no association was detectable within countries over time (fixed effects β = −0.13, p = 0.051; first differences β = −0.04, p = 0.744). Life expectancy was strongly spatially clustered (Moran's I = 0.68), and migration showed no detectable association with life expectancy once spatial dependence was modelled. MGWR indicated that the migration–life expectancy relationship was effectively global in scale and non‐significant, whereas unexplained place effects varied at a sub‐continental scale. Eighty percent of international migrants lived in a country with higher life expectancy than their country of origin. These findings suggest that the cross‐national association reflects the selective sorting of migrants towards already healthier places, compounded by spatial dependence, rather than an independent effect of migration on population health. More broadly, they extend the selective‐sorting framework from the subnational to the international scale and highlight the importance of geographic structure in interpreting migration–health relationships.

Moral Narratives, Fragile Rights and Educational Striving Among Floating Lives in Urban Vietnam

Nguyen Hoang Thien, Nguyen Hoang Yen

Full text
This study examines how Vietnamese returnees from Cambodia living on houseboats along a canal in urban Vietnam experience residence and citizenship in everyday life. Water‐based dwelling and cross‐border migration histories combine to produce a grey space in which residents are neither fully recognised nor consistently removed, despite holding Vietnamese nationality. Living on waterways outside planning compliance places households beyond the administrative categories used to distribute welfare, healthcare and residency verification, producing differentiated citizenship in practice. Residents respond through what the study terms moral infrastructure: hardship narratives, accounts of a life that burdens no one, and caregiving responsibilities used as practical grounds for negotiating tolerance with authorities. They also rely on manual work and relationships with ‘quý nhân’ and ‘mạnh thường quân’ (benevolent doctors, donors and volunteers) to meet everyday needs. Education, meanwhile, serves as a future‐oriented strategy through which families hope the next generation will gain the security and recognition they lack. Theoretically, the study extends Yiftachel's grey space framework beyond land‐based contexts and develops Holston's differentiated citizenship by showing that access to welfare depends on residency registration rather than nationality. It also proposes moral infrastructure as a complement to Simone's people‐as‐infrastructure. Practically, it calls for welfare eligibility to be decoupled from residency documentation, governance to be made more consistent and less discretionary and population frameworks to ensure that cross‐border return histories are not treated as a basis for administrative suspicion or unequal treatment.

Temporal Access and Gendered Care: Livelihoods, Staying and Migration in Vietnam's Coastal Mangrove Communities

Ly Quoc Dang, Huynh Van Da, K. David Harrison, Mai Phuc Thinh

Full text
Access to coastal resources depends not only on property, governance and proximity but also on whether ecological, household and bodily demands can be coordinated. This article examines how Khmer women negotiate mangrove and marine livelihoods in three coastal phum (villages) in Sóc Trăng, Vietnam's Mekong Delta. Drawing on an ethnographic case study, 13 narrative interviews (7 conducted as mobile interviews), participant observation and informal conversations, it examines temporal access, productive–reproductive coordination and staying or migration. Access depended on aligning tides, weather, routes, transport, bodily capacity, care and market waiting. Childcare, illness, cultivation and ritual obligations shaped whether and when livelihood work could occur. Women's local availability often sustained households while others migrated, although care could also organise collective movement. The article develops chronotopic care to explain this gendered coordination and cautions against equating livelihood continuity with resilience.

Reworking Im/Mobility Scripts Across Generations: Leaving, Staying and Returning in Rural Taiwan

Chi‐Wei Chen, Tialda Haartsen

Full text
Recent research on rural im/mobility has shown that leaving, staying and returning are meaningful life‐course processes rather than residual outcomes of migration. Building on this discussion, this article develops the concept of rural im/mobility scripts to examine the normative mobility pathways and meanings attached to leaving, staying and returning. These scripts are historically and spatially constituted, but are also reworked through biographical experience as conditions change. Drawing on life‐history interviews with 28 rural returnees and stayers in Yunlin and Chiayi, Taiwan, most born between 1971 and 1990, the analysis identifies 3 interrelated processes across the life course. First, during adolescence, participants inherited a taken‐for‐granted outmigration script in which leaving was highly institutionalised through educational expansion, parental expectations, urban success imaginaries and the devaluation of rural work, yet often weakly planned in practice. Second, in adulthood, they re‐legitimated staying and returning through locally grounded mobility, using transport, commuting, digital platforms, cross‐place markets and skills acquired elsewhere to build adequate lives and pursue local achievement from their hometowns. Third, as parents, they translated their own im/mobility experiences into a conditional expectation of self‐directed mobility for the next generation: children should know their hometown, see the world and ultimately decide whether to leave, stay and return. Following one generation from youth into parenthood, this article shows that rural im/mobility scripts are revised across the life course and translated into expectations for the next generation.

Home in Motion: Eritrean Diasporic Experiences and the Negotiation of Home Across Transnational Spaces

Tesfalem Naizghi Gebremichael, Alison Reid

Full text
This article examines how unequal legal, economic, and mobility conditions shape homemaking among Eritrea's contemporary ‘generation of asylum.’ Drawing on 160 survey responses and 13 in‐depth interviews, it compares Eritreans living in first‐asylum settings in Uganda and Ethiopia, categorised as OAUE, with those living in European destination settings in Italy and Sweden, categorised as OEUIS. The findings show that political estrangement from the Eritrean state can coexist with enduring affective attachment to Eritrea, while participants’ capacities to establish home in their countries of residence vary substantially. The comparison identifies a ‘proximity paradox’: participants living geographically closer to Eritrea in OAUE may experience weaker host‐country belonging than those living farther away in OEUIS because of greater legal insecurity, restricted mobility, and constrained economic opportunities. These inequalities can also stratify transnational homemaking. Regional cities, weddings and family reunions can provide temporary homing through cultural continuity and familial connection, but access to these spaces is unevenly enabled by travel documentation and economic security. Aspirations for return are similarly conditional on political and legal change rather than expressions of nostalgia alone. The article contributes to migration and diaspora scholarship by conceptualising temporary homing as a provisional and unequally accessible process shaped by migrants’ positions within unequal mobility regimes.

From # ExpelUngratefulAfghans to # ExpellingAfghansIsANationalDemand ; A Comparative Analysis of Digital Xenophobia and Anti‐Migrant Hate Campaigns in Iran and Pakistan

Arash Beidollahkhani

Full text
This paper examines the rise and function of anti‐Afghans immigrant digital movements in Iran and Pakistan, specifically #افغانی_نمک_حراموں_کو_بھگاو (#ExpelUngratefulAfghans) in Pakistan and #اخراج_افغانی_مطالبه_ملی (‘Expelling Afghans is a national demand’) in Iran, focusing on their development and impact from 2023 to 2025. Drawing on a mixed‐methods approach that includes discourse analysis, social network mapping and policy review, the study traces how coordinated hashtag campaigns have constructed Afghan migrants as existential threats to national identity, security and economy. These campaigns—often driven by small but hyperactive networks—do more than reflect public sentiment; they contribute to a discursive climate that may reinforce state‐led deportation drives, legal restrictions and securitized migration policies. By comparing digital hate ecologies in both countries, the study reveals converging patterns of bottom‐up xenophobic mobilization and top‐down amplification by state and media actors. This research offers a rare cross‐national perspective from the Global South on how online xenophobia reshapes migration politics in authoritarian contexts.

Segregated Spaces, Shared Lives: A Mixed Inquiry Into Afghan Immigrant Settlement in Iran

Leila Zandi‐Navgran, Hossein Afrasiabi, Rasoul Sadeghi, Abbas Askari‐Nodoushan

Full text
Immigrant residency and settlement remain contentious issues in destination societies. This study examines the residential perceptions and patterns of Afghan immigrants in Iran, addressing two central questions: What mechanisms and conditions influence the residential patterns of immigrants? How do immigrants perceive their residential circumstances? Using a Concurrent‐Embedded Mixed‐Methods approach, data were collected through fieldwork with 55 participants and supported by secondary sources. This design gathered qualitative and quantitative data simultaneously, with the quantitative insights embedded within the qualitative analysis. The categories identified from the analysis include: centralization, concentration, unevenness, immigrant‐dependent economic sectors, interpersonal and social boundaries, bonding/bridging social capital, civic and cultural participation, socio‐economic status, legal and policy constraints and settlement duration. Together, these categories point to residential segregation as the core category. The findings show that at the national level, Afghan immigrants are predominantly concentrated in major cities and metropolises due to state residential policies. At the regional level, they also settle mostly in provincial capitals due to government policies regulating the allocation of migrants. Locally, their concentration is highest in specific neighborhoods—often on the urban periphery—driven by socio‐economic conditions, social ties and duration of stay.

Europe Needs Filipino Workers: A Leverage for the Philippines?

Maruja M. B. Asis, Bernard Allan Garcia, Eunice Tejada

Full text
Compared to the established labour migration corridors between the Philippines and destination countries in Asia and the Gulf region, recent moves by European countries to recruit Filipino workers to meet current and future labour needs invite reflection on what this emerging trend implies for migration knowledge and theorising about migration, particularly the aspect of migration governance. As a relatively new terrain of engagement for both origin and destinations, this paper focuses on how key stakeholders from the Philippines view this growing trend: (1) how do different migration‐related stakeholders in the Philippines see the increasing demand for Filipino workers in Europe; (2) what kinds of engagements are developing between the Philippines and European countries on labour migration issues; and (3) what specific issues pose challenges for migration governance in the Philippines‐Europe labour migration corridor. Data for this paper come from expert interviews with participants from Philippine government institutions ( n = 3), private recruitment agencies ( n = 3), non‐government organisations ( n = 3), and Philippines‐based representatives of European destination countries ( n = 2). The findings add to the growing literature on origin states, open insights on their role in bilateral relations with destination countries, and how origin countries can leverage their resources to reduce power asymmetry vis‐à‐vis destination countries.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Exclusion Forms Connection: The Presence of Immigrant Linked Fate Among Nonimmigrants

Yoshira D. Macías Mejía, Lisa M. Sanchez

Full text
Immigrant linked fate is a new measure in the linked fate literature. In this study, we examine the predictors of immigrant linked fate, validate the measure of linked fate, and examine the role of immigrant linked fate in shaping immigration policy preferences among Latinos, Blacks, and Asians. We suggest that one must not be an immigrant to espouse immigrant linked fate. Therefore, “immigrant” linked fate has less to do with nativity and more to do with outsider status as the result of social exclusion. To test our hypotheses, we use the Collaborative Multiracial Post-Election Survey 2016 (CMPS) because it includes the measure of immigrant linked fate and a robust racial and ethnic group sample. The results suggest that Latinos, Blacks, and Asians have similar predictors of immigrant linked fate. We find immigrant linked fate is a distinct measure from racial linked fate and that immigrant linked fate predicts policy preferences.

Measuring White Identity

Geneva Valerie Cole

Full text
Scholars increasingly emphasize identity politics as a crucial factor influencing white political behavior, which is often conceptualized as a form of defensive solidarity. But does current measurement accurately capture this concept? This study reconsiders the measurement of white identity to more accurately reflect its conceptualization, distinguishing between identity and consciousness without imposing ideological interpretations of group membership. I develop and test adapted measures for white identity and consciousness, allowing for ideological variation, and find that existing measures disproportionately reflect whites who are racially conservative in part because of measurement. Testing the measures on support for Trump and affirmative action, I find that the effects of white identity are conditional on the ideological interpretation of group consciousness embedded in existing measures. This study challenges dominant assumptions about the inherent conservatism of white identity and contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of racial politics in the United States.

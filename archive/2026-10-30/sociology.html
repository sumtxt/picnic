American Sociological Review

What Do “Most People” Believe? Race, Gender, and the Generalized Other

Natasha Quadlin, Trenton D. Mize, Long Doan, Bianca Manago

Full text
When people form beliefs in social life, they often calibrate their own thinking against what they anticipate “most people” think. Although prior research demonstrates the importance of these inferences, scholars have yet to examine who is best proxied by “most people.” Given persistent inequalities in U.S. society that advantage White men, as well as theories of cultural hegemony, we hypothesize that “most people’s” imagined beliefs are closely proxied by those of White men. We test these ideas using an original survey experiment conducted with a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults ( N = 2,089) in which we adapt 64 core items from the General Social Survey. Respondents were randomly assigned to answer these questions either from the perspective of most people, or from the perspective of designated race-gender groups. Contrary to our hypothesis, we find strong and consistent evidence that White men are not seen as standard-bearers for most social issues. Instead, perceptions of what most people believe are highly aligned with the imagined beliefs of White women. Importantly, this pattern emerges not because White women are seen as influential, but because White women are perceived as less extreme than other groups. We also observe variation across respondents, such that respondents tend to center their own race-gender group in conceptualizing most people’s beliefs (with White women typically close behind).

Simply a Sign of the Season? Christmas Trees as Cultural Ciphers in Local Government Management of Diversity

Gary J. Adler, Damon Mayrl, Eric Plutzer, Jonathan S. Coley, Rebecca Sager, Elise Wolff

Full text
How do local governments symbolically manage the diversity of their residents? Drawing from an original representative sample of local government officials, we use a novel survey experiment with open-ended survey responses to examine preferences for cultural objects that symbolically manage religious diversity, an axis of difference that is increasingly salient to social conflict. Our experiment randomly varies the content of holiday displays on government property to measure diversity management strategies. Results show that government officials prefer the assimilationist strategy of a Christmas tree—by itself—over multiculturalist and exclusivist strategies, which both include sectarian religious content. Officials also evaluate Christmas trees as the content least likely to signal government endorsement of religion, compared to other holiday displays. We argue that these patterns can be explained by conceiving of a Christmas tree as a cultural cipher, a well-known cultural object that affords a range of meanings and resonates across social identities as innocuous. We argue that cultural ciphers are useful for an assimilationist approach to managing diversity because their ambiguity allows them to act as a vehicle of symbolic inclusion while also sustaining the meanings of majority groups. We also consider how cultural ciphers may lose their power for civic solidarity.

Incentivizing Equality? How Work-Family Policies Affect Parenthood Penalties in 27 Countries

Allison Dunatchik, Pilar Gonalons-Pons

Full text
Work-family policies are theorized to shape the division of paid labor after parenthood, but recent studies have cast doubt on their importance. Leveraging harmonized longitudinal data spanning 27 countries and an analytic focus on couples and couple dynamics, our study shows that work-family policies do shape how divisions of paid labor change with parenthood, and that they also moderate the importance of couples’ relative resources as a determinant of the division of labor. We find that moderate-length paid maternal leave and greater childcare availability reduce parenthood penalties in couples’ division of paid work time, while prolonged leave exacerbates penalties. Furthermore, by focusing on couples, we provide novel evidence that partners’ relative resources do not operate uniformly across work-family policy contexts, showing that policies can incentivize couples to divide paid work in ways their relative resource profiles would not otherwise predict. We find that moderate-length paid leave and greater childcare availability encourage egalitarianism among male-breadwinner couples, although extended leave encourages greater gender traditionalism among female-breadwinner couples. These findings reveal that classic theories of household specialization and bargaining are not universal, and highlight the need for greater theoretical attention to how country- and couple-level forces interact to shape gender inequality.

Population and Development Review

Foreign Aid and Teenage Childbearing

Heather Congdon Fors, Dick Durevall, Ann‐Sofie Isaksson, Annika Lindskog

Full text
Teenage childbearing has serious consequences for young mothers, their children, and society. This study estimates the impact of foreign aid projects on teenage fertility in Malawi. We combine georeferenced data on aid projects from 1998 to 2016 with individual‐level fertility data. Identification relies on spatial and temporal variation in aid exposure and survey timing, with controls for project placement and teenage childbearing among older women not exposed to the intervention. Results show that aid to sexual and reproductive health, HIV/AIDS, and education significantly reduce teenage fertility, while other aid types have limited effects. Women exposed to relevant aid during the critical age range were 11 percent less likely to have given birth as teenagers, with larger effects for women who were exposed for longer periods. The effect appears to operate mainly through increased school attendance and delayed marriage, but there is also some evidence for increased abstinence, contraceptive use, and family planning knowledge.

Sociological Methodology

How to Talk with the Academic Community about Contested Knowledge: A Confrontational-Reflexive Interview Model

Łukasz Remisiewicz, Nina Janiszewska

Full text
Interviewing academics about contested knowledge presents significant methodological challenges, as the interview situation often activates “official” professional repertoires that conceal ambivalence and the active construction of epistemic boundaries. To address these limitations, the authors propose and evaluate the confrontational-reflexive interview model (CRIM), a research design aimed at systematically eliciting reflexivity and analyzing boundary-work in situ . The model treats the interview not as a neutral information extraction tool but as a structured sequence of meaning-making occasions. Stage 1 establishes a baseline narrative in expert interviews and focus groups, which is subsequently disrupted by a standardized “confrontation stimulus”—a scholarly text explicitly challenging the dominant disciplinary consensus—forcing participants to negotiate their position against a credible internal critique. Stage 2, conducted approximately one month later via asynchronous written correspondence, introduces temporal distance to test the stability and revisability of these accounts outside the immediate pressure of the face-to-face interaction. Drawing on a study of political scientists’ discourse on conspiracy theories, the authors demonstrate that although standard interviewer probes often fail to unsettle “official” narratives, the combination of a third-party textual confrontation and delayed reflection successfully reveals the specific conditions under which experts reproduce, repair, or redraw the boundaries of legitimate knowledge. The authors argue that CRIM contributes to sociological methodology by offering a replicable framework for observing expertise as a contingent, interactional performance.

Religion and early fertility in the United States: changes during the second demographic transition

Jeremy E Uecker, Riley S Peterson

Full text
We assess how changes in early fertility varied among US women with different religious characteristics, and how the associations between religion and early fertility changed across birth cohorts from 1950 to 1993. Using data from the National Survey of Family Growth, we examine the timing of nonmarital, marital, and overall first births through age 24. Results from life tables and discrete-time event history models reveal that increases in early nonmarital first births were smaller among women who attended religious services more frequently in childhood, whereas early marital births declined at similar rates irrespective of childhood religious service attendance. Because early births became increasingly nonmarital across birth cohorts, women who attended religious services more frequently in childhood became less likely to have an early birth overall. Early fertility differences by religious tradition generally converged across cohorts, although women raised conservative Protestant continued to have higher likelihoods of early birth relative to women raised in other religious traditions net of controls. Women who were raised nonreligious remained similar to conservative Protestants in their likelihood of nonmarital childbearing across cohorts and became statistically similar to them in their overall early births in the latest cohort. Changes in early fertility during the second demographic transition therefore varied by religious background and reshaped the relationship between religion and early fertility.

Beyond economic bargaining power? Work-related internal migration and subjective well-being in couples

Elias Hofmann, Nico Stawarz, Nicolai Netz, Heiko Rüger

Full text
Bargaining theory posits that both partners’ utility shapes couples’ migration decisions. Yet, most empirical tests focus on individual incomes, thus overlooking non-economic factors in couples’ deliberations about spatial mobility. We argue that considering holistic indicators of utility, such as subjective well-being, can advance our understanding of couples’ decision-making. Using data from the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP, 1991–2021) and fixed effects panel regressions, we analyze changes in life satisfaction following work-related internal migration on both individual and dyadic levels. Moreover, we examine whether these changes depend on shifts in partners’ economic bargaining power (EBP, defined as female partners’ share of total household labor income), thereby evaluating the common assumption that couples’ bargaining centers around income. Our results show that, on average, both partners experience increases in their life satisfaction following migration. Distinguishing whether women’s EBP remains unchanged, decreases, or increases with migration, we find that women’s life satisfaction gains are only weakly related to changes in their EBP. In contrast, men’s life satisfaction only increases if their partner’s EBP decreases or remains unchanged, but not if it increases—a pattern consistent with the notion of gendered expectations about the within-couple income distribution hampering men’s well-being gains. Overall, our findings suggest that well-being is an important factor in couples’ migration decision-making and that income-based measures of bargaining power provide only an incomplete account of partners’ bargaining positions.

European Sociological Review

Institutionalized negative selection and structural teacher favouritism: demystifying the effects of cultural capital on educational achievement in China

Wensong Shen

Full text
Recent studies in China often report negative effects of cultural capital on educational achievement. This study revisits that claim by examining its effect, underlying mechanisms, and heterogeneity. Drawing on longitudinal data from the China Education Panel Survey, four main findings emerge. First, after accounting for negative selection, the supposed negative effects of cultural capital disappear. More broadly, cultural capital shows no significant overall effect on educational achievement, contrary to what cultural capital theory would usually predict. Nevertheless, cultural capital and educational achievement remain indirectly linked through institutional processes. Second, the analysis provides empirical support for a new concept, institutionalized negative selection, defined as a process in which low-achieving students select into extracurricular activities in pursuit of better educational credentials, a pattern supported by formal educational institutions. Third, although no evidence supports the conventional mechanisms that cultural capital reduces study time or enhances cognitive or non-cognitive learning skills, highbrow cultural participation is associated with greater structural teacher favouritism (i.e. praising and cold-calling on students in class), which in turn improves educational achievement. Fourth, institutionalized negative selection is found only among low-socioeconomic status (SES) students, supporting the cultural mobility model, whereas structural teacher favouritism is more pronounced among high-SES students and in high-SES schools, supporting the cultural reproduction model. These findings show that, in China, institutionalized negative selection and structural teacher favouritism are two important pathways linking cultural capital and educational achievement. This reveals how cultural capital can both promote educational mobility and perpetuate educational inequality, depending on how educational institutions value cultural capital.

Is parents’ employment uncertainty linked to children's development? Analysing patterns and mechanisms in France

Marta Facchini, Lidia Panico, Carlo Barone

Full text
Early childhood is a critical period for the reproduction of social inequality. While extant literature focuses on socio-economic background, less is known about the role of parents’ employment uncertainty, notwithstanding its significant presence in post-Fordist societies. We investigate the relationship between household employment uncertainty (HEU)—including temporary work and unemployment—and children's cognitive and socio-emotional development, as well as their physical health. We leverage four waves of the nationally representative French Longitudinal Study of Children (ELFE). First, using ordinary least squares and linear probability models, we find that the accumulation within the household and the persistence of HEU over time are both associated with lower cognitive development (up to −0.2 SD) at ages 2 and 3.5. Results for socio-emotional development are less robust, and physical health appears not to be correlated with HEU, possibly due to comprehensive healthcare. Second, we examine potential mechanisms using counterfactual mediation analysis. In line with the family investment and the family stress models, income and parental investment in cognitively-enhancing activities mediate the link with cognitive development, whereas parental stress mediates the association with socio-emotional development. Robustness tests include regression-with-residuals to account for other mediators and sibling birth as post-treatment confounders of the mediator–outcome link. Overall, HEU emerges as an important pathway in early inequality, calling for targeted policy.

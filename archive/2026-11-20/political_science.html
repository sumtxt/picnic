American Political Science Review

The Limit of Nature: Thucydides and the Politics of Theory

MARK D. FISHER

Full text
This article reconsiders Thucydides’s role as a foundational theorist of human nature. Contrary to conventional readings, I argue that Thucydides approaches “nature” ( physis ) less as a universal principle of behavior than as a site of political and theoretical investigation. By distinguishing his authorial voice from those of his speakers, I demonstrate that his analysis consistently approaches human nature as a limit concept—a baseline and boundary of human capacity that emerges in moments of existential precarity—while introducing a more nuanced set of terms to describe the habitual behaviors of political groups. By nevertheless foregrounding the more robust and determinative concept of nature in moments of contentious deliberation, Thucydides encourages reflection on the political dynamics that generate essentialist theories in public discourse. His account thus serves as a helpful point of reference for thinking about how competitive, polarizing speech affects the substantive categories of political debate and analysis, and through them political action.

How Sociotropic Aesthetic Judgments Drive Opposition to Dense Housing Development

DAVID E. BROOCKMAN, CHRISTOPHER S. ELMENDORF, JOSHUA L. KALLA

Full text
Voter opposition to dense housing development contributes to America’s housing shortage. Prevailing explanations emphasize homeowner self-interest and “NIMBYism.” We develop a theory of how sociotropic aesthetic judgments shape housing policy preferences and demonstrate its unique implications with a series of descriptive studies and experiments. First, people who choose to live in dense neighborhoods have an aesthetic taste for density, leading homeowners in already-dense areas to be more supportive of dense development both in their neighborhoods and elsewhere. Second, voters are more supportive of dense development when it is proposed in already-dense neighborhoods where it “fits”—even voters who live in those same dense neighborhoods. Finally, genuine aesthetic distaste for tall buildings is widespread and causally affects support for development. Our findings offer a new lens for understanding housing policy preferences and suggest novel solutions for addressing America’s housing shortage.

Comparative Political Studies

From Wealth to Welfare: How Economic Elites Made Inequality Work

Nils Blossey

Full text
Why do advanced economies engage in substantial income transfers but scarcely redistribute wealth? This paper argues that wealth inequality historically led economic elites to expand income transfers to propertyless households to stabilize the unequal property order. I study this proposition in the context of landholding inequality in Prussia. Estate owners, I argue, used income transfers to offset the economic insecurities of landless workers who could not rely on landownership as insurance against income loss. I digitize surveys of local poor relief and exploit Prussia’s administrative structure, where estates functioned as independent local authorities. The results show that landholding inequality caused higher income transfers to the poor, driven by the redistributive actions of estate owners. Estate owners allocated relief as social protection over the life cycle for households outside of the active workforce. This commitment to insurance was associated with greater voter support for the conservative parties representing estate owners’ interests.

Critical Intersectionality: Toward Transformative Policy Analysis, Not Just Another Form of “Academic Bullshit”

Ashlee Christoffersen, Serena D’Agostino

Full text
Contemporary invocations of intersectionality in political and policy analysis—particularly within the European context—frequently engage with the concept in an acritical manner. This tendency toward a race-blind, gender-first, or otherwise depoliticized approach to intersectionality risks undermining intersectionality’s transformative potential, reducing it to what Patricia Hill Collins calls “just another form of … ‘academic bullshit.’” While these arguments are now well rehearsed, in this article we discuss these tendencies that endure even in recent “intersectional” policy analysis, and even when the authors note these arguments themselves. We make suggestions for a more robust and transformative analytical approach to advance a rather stagnant field.

The Erosion of Academic Freedom in the United States in the Early Twenty-First Century: A State- and Movements-Based Analysis

Steven Brint

Full text
Academic freedom protections for US professors have never been secure, but they experienced an unprecedented erosion in the early twenty-first century. The objective of this article is to analyze why this sea change occurred—and with what consequences. It discusses the policy interventions in the 50 states and the federal government that brought the issue of higher education’s autonomy to a head in 2025. The analysis focuses on the growing distance between the ideological orientations of university faculties and Republican legislators; the development of organizational strength and coordination on both the right and the left; the use of state power by both Democrats and Republicans for partisan purposes related to higher education; and precipitating events that provided a springboard for extensive policy interventions. It also discusses deeper contextual factors, including demographic change, increasing income inequality, the rise of new media, and their links to political polarization.

What Role Did Immigration Attitudes Play in Latinos’ 2016–2024 Swing Toward the GOP?

Daniel J Hopkins, William Marble

Full text
After the 2012 and 2016 presidential elections, scholars and observers frequently characterized Latino voters as unlikely to support anti-immigration candidates, especially when immigration is salient. However, between 2016 and 2024, Latinos were among the groups that swung the most toward the GOP. Given the mismatch between Donald Trump’s strident opposition to unauthorized immigration and Latinos’ historically pro-immigration views, understanding the role of Latinos’ immigration attitudes in vote choice over this period is theoretically important. Acknowledging that Latinos are not single-issue voters, we investigate this question using multiple high-quality, population-based panels of Latinos (2016–2024). We document a sharp restrictionist turn in Latinos’ attitudes. We also find that Latinos perceive Democrats to have moderated slightly on immigration, though not enough to keep pace with the change in public opinion. Finally, we demonstrate that Latinos placed just as much weight on immigration policy in their 2024 vote choice as in 2016. Our results suggest that while immigration has not waned in importance for Latinos, changes in attitudes from 2016 to 2024 diminished Democrats’ advantage on this issue.

Political Science Research and Methods

A sentiment-based approach to measuring multidimensional party positions using transformer

Hung H. V. Nguyen

Full text
Existing automated position scaling models from texts rely on the conflict-driven assumption, forcing models to search the linguistic space for differentiating features between political subjects. This leads models to conflate rhetorical styles with political sentiments when subjects are not inherently conflictual. This article introduces ContextScale, a sentiment-based position scaling framework leveraging pre-trained Transformer models to predict policy topics and political sentiments and measure topic-specific position scores across multiple policy dimensions at the sequence level. ContextScale offers an alternative way to gauge party positions, focusing on how political texts express attitudes toward policy issues rather than on contrast between actors. Validity tests demonstrate its ability to capture nuances in party positioning and its adaptability across languages, text domains, and theoretical models.

Philosophy & Public Affairs

Ignorance and Indifference of the Deaths of (Select) Others

Lidal Dror

Full text
This paper offers an explanation of the disparate concern Westerners have for civilians who die in different wars abroad. I consider two attempts to explain the disparate concern in terms of racism: first a more interpersonal understanding of racism as racist animus, and then a more structural explanation in terms of “white ignorance.” I argue that trying to understand the disparate concern evinced for different civilian casualties primarily in terms of racism is mistaken, and that a Marxist account of imperialism offers a plausible framework for explaining Western attitudes. The paper also argues for the interplay between imperialism and race, and for the philosophical importance of considering the role of imperialism and capitalism when considering race, racism, and white ignorance.

Research & Politics

Claiming to protect women, persuading men: Femonationalism and the destigmatisation of radical right parties

Juliette Corbi

Full text
The electoral success of radical right parties is often constrained by the stigma surrounding them. These parties therefore actively employ destigmatisation strategies. One such strategy is the use of femonationalist framings: the justification of anti-immigration policies by the need to protect women. This article investigates the extent to which such framings succeed in destigmatising radical right parties by reducing their association with prejudice. To analyse the effect of this strategy, I conducted an online survey experiment in France in 2024–2025 with approximately 7,000 respondents, focusing on the Rassemblement National (RN). The results show that femonationalism, when it is used by the RN as a gender-equality framing, destigmatises the party among men and increases their perception that the RN supports women’s rights. These effects, however, are not statistically significant among women, whose security and rights are used in such femonationalist framings. Overall, these findings show that strategies employed by radical right parties to become normalised are effective, but their effect is concentrated in one part of the population.

European Political Science Review

Us, them, and the public broadcaster: group identification and bias perceptions toward public service media

Emily Gravesteijn

Full text
Prior research demonstrates that media bias perceptions are often rooted in social and political identification. However, most work focuses on partisan outlets. In Europe, public service media (PSM) are subject to bias accusations, despite their mandate to ensure pluralism and impartiality. This raises questions on whether frameworks explaining bias perceptions toward politically affiliated media apply to PSM. Building on insights from social identity theory and affective polarization, this study examines to what extent partisan in-group identification strength and strength of ideological and politicized societal out-group(s) antipathy are associated with bias perceptions toward PSM. Using original survey data ( N = 1863) from the Netherlands, I show that partisan in-group identification strength is not associated with bias perceptions toward PSM. Instead, the strength of ideological out-group antipathy is consistently associated with bias perceptions toward PSM and extends to politicized societal groups. Furthermore, the effects for the strength of ideological out-group antipathy are conditional on ideological self-placement and party support: it’s effect is primarily associated with bias perceptions among center- and right-leaning individuals and respondents who support right-leaning parties. Additionally, right-leaning and conservative respondents were overall more likely to perceive bias toward PSM. The findings of this paper advance hostile media and affective polarization research by demonstrating that ideological out-group antipathy and ideological predispositions are fundamental to understanding bias perceptions toward neutrality-bound institutions such as PSM. Together, bias perceptions toward PSM must not only be understood through hostility toward political opponents but also through broader ideological orientations through which audiences evaluate PSM’s impartiality.

Can you feel the group appeal tonight?: MPs’ conveyed emotions in disability representation in Western Europe

Vivien Fabry

Full text
Parties use group appeals to connect or distance themselves from certain social groups. Existing research focuses mostly on well-established groups, like social class, often neglecting marginalized groups, such as persons with disabilities. This study investigates disability group appeals and their symbolic dimension in parliamentary speeches from 1997 to 2019 across Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, and Ireland, employing transformer-based models to identify group appeals and analyze emotional context. Findings reveal country and party ideology differences in group appeal usage and a gender effect, indicating women are more likely to appeal to persons with disabilities. This research offers a methodological framework applicable to other marginalized groups and proposes adding a substantive dimension to the study of group appeals.

Comparative Politics

Challenging the Secular State: Faith-Based Drug Rehabilitation on the Rise in Brazil

Wendy Hunter, Natasha Borges Sugiyama

Full text
A significant achievement of Brazil’s evangelical Christian movement has been to secure public financing for religious drug rehabilitation. “Therapeutic communities” challenge the state’s longstanding commitment to a secular and scientifically-based public health system, which offers a credible alternative for drug treatment. That faith-based centers expanded under Workers’ Party presidents, which had historically defended Brazil’s estado laico and public healthcare system, is puzzling. The policy process literature helps explain this seeming paradox: rising illegal drug use opened a window of opportunity for religiously motivated actors to frame the problem in moral terms, gain initial traction through participatory venues, and set off policy feedback effects. These processes unfolded in a modernizing society marked by inequality and insecurity, creating fertile ground for the sacred to disrupt the secular.

Legislative Studies Quarterly

Strategic Moralization in Parliamentary Debates

Minahil Malik, Hanna Bäck, Emma Baker, Royce Carroll, Robert Klemmensen

Full text
Moralization, the framing of political conflict in terms of right and wrong rather than as pragmatic trade‐offs, is thought to both mobilize and persuade audiences, but may also make compromise less acceptable. This article examines when and why representatives employ moralizing rhetoric. We argue that moralizing rhetoric varies with the circumstances that shape the incentives representatives face. We examine whether such rhetoric is more common among opposition and outsider or niche parties and intensifies as elections approach. Because moralization in parliamentary rhetoric often operates through implicit framing rather than explicit moral vocabulary, we apply a generative large language model (LLM) with chain‐of‐thought prompting to Swedish parliamentary debates from 1991 to 2023. We find that moralization is substantially higher among nongoverning parties and increases before elections. Examining whether representatives from niche or outsider parties tend to moralize more, we find some evidence of higher moralization among the Left and Green parties, but not among the right‐wing populists when government status is accounted for.

The Rise of Amateur Candidate Pipelines and Declining Value of Candidate Experience in U.S. Senate Primaries, 1980–2024

Carlos Algara, Byengseon Bae, Isaac Hale, Michael Heseltine, Evan Lieber

Full text
Given the increasing nationalization of elections, research on U.S. electoral competition has shifted to emphasize same‐party competition rather than general elections. Much of this work, especially arguments that electoral amateurs are increasingly likely to win party nominations, focuses almost exclusively on U.S. House elections, leaving the more competitive U.S. Senate largely outside systematic inquiry. In this manuscript, we address this gap by introducing new data covering all candidates who filed to run for the U.S. Senate from 1980 to 2024, including primary contestants and candidates who later withdrew. We provide three descriptive findings. First, inexperienced candidates increasingly enter Senate primaries, especially Republican ones, while the number of experienced candidates remains stable. Second, congruent with the House, candidate quality declines as a predictor of primary success and is now statistically insignificant in both parties. Third, we rule out fundraising convergence and call for further research on mechanisms shaping the declining value of candidate experience.

Gender and Presiding Roles in Legislatures: Discrepancies in Mass and Elite Perceptions From Parallel Survey Experiments

Yuya Endo, Hirofumi Miwa, Hikaru Nukui, Yoshikuni Ono

Full text
Women are far less likely than men to serve as chairs of legislatures—a disparity often attributed to gender norms that associate leadership with men and assign women to subordinate roles. We test these assumptions about the gender gap in legislative leadership using vignette experiments with over 4000 voters and 7000 elected municipal council members in Japan. We find a significant gap between voters' perceptions of legislators' preferences and council members' evaluations of women's suitability for leadership. Voters believe council members prefer men as chairs and women as vice‐chairs, whereas council members view female colleagues as equally or more suitable for both roles. These results suggest that legislators' expressed gender bias alone may not fully explain women's underrepresentation as presiding officers and that institutional or supply‐side constraints may also play a role. By distinguishing voters' perceptions of elite preferences from elites' own evaluations, our micro‐level evidence contributes to debates over women's underrepresentation in political leadership.

When Oversight Stops Producing Answers: Parliamentary Questions and Executive Accountability in Turkey

Serdar Gülener

Full text
Under what conditions does an oversight instrument remain formally available while ceasing to produce answers, and how does that loss unfold when measured directly rather than inferred from the rules? Where comparative research reads legislative constraint from the instruments a parliament possesses, this article reads it from what those instruments yield, using every question submitted to the Turkish Grand National Assembly between 1999 and 2026 and its record of when ministries answered. Under a durable single‐party majority, the share of written questions answered within the statutory period eroded gradually, then settled at a low floor; no political milestone produces a step in that trend. The later abolition of oral questions and the motion of censure aligned the rules with an amended constitution and left no detectable change in the surviving instrument. The decline reached the governing party's own questions and fell hardest on the DEM Party lineage. Oversight weakened in a particular order, yield first and gradually, formal availability last and abruptly; measures built from formal availability date that loss to the wrong moment.

Partisan Delay: Gubernatorial Discretion and Representation in Special Elections

Dillon Laaker, Cassidy Reller

Full text
How do governors work as partisan agents to influence legislative control and representation? This article examines a unique power given to governors to select the timing of congressional and state legislative special elections. We theorize that governors are active partisan agents who expedite and delay special election timing based on whether the vacancy will favor or disfavor their party. We test this using two novel datasets on special elections in the U.S. House from 1959 to 2024 and state legislatures from 2009 to 2024. We find that governors systematically delay special elections when the opposing party is expected to win, with effects more pronounced in the modern era. These findings have significant implications for representation, demonstrating that even safe districts can be deprived of representation due to partisan demands. We contribute to the burgeoning literature on election timing, demonstrating the role partisan actors play in shaping the election calendar.

European Union Politics

Disaggregating European Union issue salience: Party leaders’ selective emphasis on Twitter in six countries

Jan Kovář

Full text
This article examines the internal composition of the salience dimension of European Union politicisation and asks how attention across its sub-issues relates to party-political characteristics, politicising moments and policy-field characteristics. Using 181,615 tweets by party leaders in six countries, it identifies 14,528 tweets related to the European Union, maps them across 21 issue categories, and analyses covariates of thematic emphasis. The results show concentrated and differentiated salience: governance dominates, macroeconomics, international affairs, and immigration form a second tier, and many categories remain episodic or marginal. Variation is systematically associated with party characteristics, crisis-related moments, and policy-field characteristics. Aggregate European Union salience therefore conceals substantial heterogeneity in the policy and polity objects that parties make visible across issues and over time.

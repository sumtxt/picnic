Contesting temporariness, claiming belonging: Ukrainians under differing policies of collective protection in Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden

Marthe Handå Myhre, Oleksandra Deineko, Olga Filippova

Full text
This paper studies how Ukrainian refugees in four Nordic countries perceive of and navigate their temporary protection status amid an uncertain future. Over time, the temporariness embedded in temporary collective protection becomes protracted and contested, as it conflicts with refugees’ aspirations to become full-fledged members of the host society. Refugees contest this embedded temporariness as well as integration policies in the host state, or the lack thereof, which they see as limiting their possibilities for being included. The paper has two main takeaways. Temporary collective protection, as a status of residency granted rapidly after arrival and allowing people to work right away, encourages and even demands a great deal of agency from refugees to take part in the host society. Although the embedded temporariness and the uncertainty it entails are experienced as difficult, refugees deploy strategies to cope with it, such as “living here and now”, “maintaining ties with Ukraine in case of return”, “changing to another status of residency”, or “moving to another country”. While differing policies in the host states, most notably requirements for transitioning to a more permanent status of residency, are decisive for what strategies are at hand, refugees claim belonging through employment and hopes of full inclusion.

International Migration Review

(No) Longer Dependent: The Link between Reception, Housing, and Civic Integration Policies and Social Assistance Dependency among Refugees in the Netherlands

Vardan Barsegyan, Samir Mustafa Negash

Full text
We analyzed how key aspects of refugees’ reception and civic integration experiences relate to their probability of exiting social assistance for employment in the Netherlands. We focused on the duration of stay in reception centers, relocations between centers, completion of the civic integration program, and the language level of the integration diploma. We used administrative data on approximately 95,000 refugees who received refugee residence permits between 2014 and 2022. We conducted robustness analyses using a cohort of approximately 21,000 Syrian refugees who received residence permits between 2014 and 2016 and representative survey data on 2,553 members of this cohort, which allowed us to control for pre-migration education and employment. Multilevel linear probability discrete-time survival models showed distinct gender patterns. Among men, the monthly hazard of exiting social assistance increased until approximately 3 years after housing and subsequently declined, whereas among women it increased gradually throughout the observation period. Prolonged stays in reception centers were associated with a lower hazard of employment among men, particularly among those who stayed for more than 2 years, and this disadvantage persisted over time. Among women, the differences by reception duration were small and inconsistent. Relocations had a small and temporary association with employment. Completing civic integration was strongly associated with labor market entry for men and women, and these differences remained substantial 5 to 6 years after housing. B1- and B2-level integration diplomas were generally associated with a higher probability of exiting social assistance than A2-level diplomas.

Uneven Return and Spatial Diffusion in Post‐Conflict Syria: An Agent‐Based Model of Refugee Return

Sefa Secen, Haci Duru

Full text
Refugee return after civil war rarely unfolds evenly across space. Even within the same national transition, some regions experience rapid demographic return while others remain depopulated despite comparable improvements in security and reconstruction. Why does return cluster geographically rather than diffuse uniformly? This article conceptualizes refugee return as a spatial diffusion process shaped by heterogeneous activation thresholds, place‐based governance legitimacy, and network density. Drawing on an agent‐based model calibrated with survey data from Syrian refugees in Lebanon, we simulate post‐conflict return dynamics following regime change in Syria in December 2024. The model embeds households within geographically structured social networks, allowing perceptions of risk and opportunity to evolve through peer influence. Building on the conflict model of migration and the human‐insecurity approach, the model treats insecurity as sticky rather than completely informational. In other words, households with direct experience of violence in Syria are less swayed by peers' reassurance and slower to revise their own perceived risk. Social proof diffuses weakly where insecurity is embedded. The results reveal pronounced regional divergence: returns emerge in nonlinear, wave‐like cascades in some governorates, while remaining slow in others. These patterns mirror observed post‐2024 return data through September 2026 and show how localized legitimacy environments, network clustering, and the memory of violence shape return patterns in fragile, post‐conflict contexts.

Home as a Factor in Old‐Age Emigration From Switzerland

Verena Baltes‐Kallas

Full text
This paper uses “home,” in its physical, emotional and origin dimensions, as an integrative lens for understanding late‐life emigration from Switzerland. Its life‐course perspective draws on sociology, economics, and psychology. The paper is based on 50 interviews with Swiss and foreign nationals aged 55–74 who have left Switzerland or are considering doing so. Results are put into context with descriptive statistics and analysis of matched longitudinal administrative data. Findings confirm the role of home as an anchor, indicate a regional mobility pattern, and reveal the significance of nationality for both the risk of emigration and the destination country. The paper discusses the outlook for Swiss housing, population and migration trends and derives policy recommendations. Its exploration of emigrant profiles, motivations, and circumstances highlights the multiplicity of factors and the complexity of emigration decision‐making. It also shows the benefits of analyzing diverse nationalities and destinations jointly and offers insight into the tension between mobility aspirations and feasibility.

Well‐Being and Sociocultural Adaptation in Relation to Return Intentions: Evidence From Older Italian Migrants in Ticino, Switzerland

Giulia Laporta Parsaei, Claudio Bolzman, Camilla Matera, Elisa Barbiano di Belgiojoso

Full text
The aim of this article is to examine the relationship between subjective well‐being, sociocultural adaptation, and return intentions in later‐life migration. Although many older migrants express a desire to return to their country of origin, they often remain in the host country upon retirement. Decision‐making is shaped by affective, identity‐related, and contextual factors, whose interplay produces trajectories that do not always correspond to stated intentions. The article addresses two research questions: (1) What is the relationship between subjective well‐being and return intentions? (2) How is sociocultural adaptation associated with return intentions? This contribution integrates psychological and sociocultural perspectives to offer a more multidimensional understanding of later‐life return migration. The analysis draws on a survey of 193 Italian immigrants aged 60‐80 living in the Canton of Ticino, Switzerland. The findings show that very high subjective well‐being reduces the propensity to return, while sociocultural adaptation emerges as the strongest predictor.

Spatial Convertibility of Transnational Human Capital: How Domestic and Overseas Education Shape Chinese Overseas Returnees' Post‐Return Labor‐Market Outcomes

Yuanyuan Xie, Can Cui

Full text
As growing numbers of international students return to China after completing overseas degrees, their post‐return labor‐market outcomes have attracted increasing scholarly attention. Existing research has focused primarily on their earnings and employment status, paying limited attention to the spatial dimension of these labor‐market outcomes. In China, employment opportunities are strongly stratified across cities. To foreground the spatial embeddedness of the labor market, this study conceptualizes post‐return labor‐market outcomes as comprising both occupational attainment and employment location within the urban hierarchy. Using résumé data from a leading professional networking platform and multinomial logit models, we focus on returnees who obtained a bachelor's degree in China and a master's degree abroad, and analyze how university quality and university location across both stages are associated with their post‐return labor‐market outcomes. The results show that university quality is primarily associated with access to high‐skilled jobs in first‐tier cities, whereas university location is linked to a wider set of labor‐market outcomes. Particularly, undergraduate study in first‐tier cities and master's study in North America are associated with more favorable outcomes. These findings suggest that the conversion of transnational human capital is shaped by both educational quality and the spatial contexts in which education is acquired and evaluated. By incorporating city hierarchy, this study moves beyond an occupation‐centered view of returnee outcomes and shows how educational backgrounds are evaluated and rewarded within spatially uneven labor markets.

Migration Governance, Gender and the Identification of Trafficked Women: Evidence From Greece

Francesca Cimino

Full text
The early identification of trafficking victims is widely recognised as a key component of protection policies, yet little is known about how identification procedures operate in contexts shaped by restrictive migration governance. This article analyses the identification of women victims of trafficking in Greece between 2015 and 2019 through a gender‐sensitive and intersectional lens. Drawing on seventeen interviews with policy implementers and policy experts, together with field observations, it examines whether identification procedures reflected a victim‐centred approach and how EU and national migration policies shaped frontline practices. Findings show that identification often occurred within overcrowded and unsafe settings where the interaction between gender, nationality, migration status, socio‐economic resources and migration trajectories was insufficiently considered. Procedures relied heavily on victims' self‐reporting, while the hotspot framework, geographical restrictions and fragmented responsibilities hindered proactive detection. The article argues that these dynamics revealed a tension between formal commitments to early identification and the operational priorities of migration governance, limiting the implementation of gender‐sensitive identification mechanisms and contributing to forms of institutional invisibility for trafficked women within asylum and reception systems. These findings also provide a basis for assessing the subsequent development of formal identification mechanisms in Greece. In light of the new Pact on Migration and Asylum, the operational pressures identified in this study may remain relevant unless accelerated screening and border procedures are accompanied by effective vulnerability detection, referral mechanisms and protection safeguards.

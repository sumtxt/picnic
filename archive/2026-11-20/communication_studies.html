Softening the spring: Sensitization of transient media effects through repeated exposure over time

Yuqian Ni, Joshua Baldwin, Allan D Tate, Michael D Schmidt, Kyle Johnsen, Sun Joo (Grace) Ahn

Full text
Existing longitudinal media effects research often emphasizes overall trends in behavioral or attitudinal changes, while overlooking fluctuations that occur before and after each exposure instance. This oversight can obscure the mechanisms underlying longitudinal media effects, particularly how they accumulate from successive short-term effects. We hypothesized a sensitization process where a single media exposure induces a transient change in behavior followed by a rapid decay, while repeated exposure gradually strengthen the magnitude of transient effects and make them more sustained. We tested this hypothesis by analyzing continuously recorded behavioral data collected via wearable sensors over 6 months. During this period, children repeatedly interacted with an integrated system of media technology designed to promote physical activity (PA). Data partially supported the hypothesized sensitization process. Children’s PA showed a transient increase following single exposures. Moreover, although the transient effect did not become more sustained over time, it grew in magnitude with repeated exposure.

Contextualizing Platform Power: A Comparative Study of Newspaper–Portal Dependency in South Korea and Japan

Joo-Young Jung, Misook Lee, Mikko Villi, Akira Tanaka, Yong-Chan Kim, Kaori Hayashi, Nando Malmelin

Full text
This article examines how dominant news portals, Naver in South Korea and Yahoo Japan, shape power and dependency relations with mainstream newspapers under different national resource environments. Drawing on media system dependency theory, structuration theory, and normalization process theory, the study analyzes how senior newsroom leaders understand portal dependency, respond to portal control over key resources, and reflexively monitor the organizational consequences of those responses. Based on fifteen in-depth interviews with senior editors and executives across ten major news organizations, the analysis reveals variation within a shared pattern of portal centrality. In both countries, portals control important resources for audience access, visibility, and monetization, and newspapers continue many portal-related practices despite recognizing their costs. However, dependency differs in degree and form. In South Korea, limited alternatives make continued accommodation to Naver organizationally reasonable, while repeated portal-oriented practices further weaken direct audience relationships and intensify asymmetric dependency. In Japan, newspapers retain more diversified resources, including subscription revenues, distribution networks, advertiser relationships, and institutional access, enabling greater negotiation and partial reconfiguration of relations with Yahoo without eliminating dependency. The findings show that portal dependency is reproduced through the interaction of unequal resource configurations, reflexive monitoring, and repeated organizational practices. The study thus offers a context-sensitive and recursive account of platform power by showing how critical awareness of dependency may coexist with practices that sustain it, and how the availability of alternative resources shapes organizations’ capacity to modify those relations.

Disenchanting the Epistemic Authority and Inevitability of AI: An Analysis of Factcheckers’ Perspectives and Professional Guidelines

Regina Cazzamatta, Augusto Santos

Full text
As a sociotechnical practice, fact-checking integrates artificial intelligence (AI) while resisting “enchanted determinism” to preserve professional agency and epistemic authority. This study examines how fact-checkers in Europe and Latin America perceive AI affordances within their professional practices and how organizations articulate and govern AI use. Methodologically, the study is based on 50 qualitative interviews with practitioners and a thematic analysis of 18 publicly available AI guidelines and institutional documents. The findings suggest a distinction between instrumental optimism and epistemic disenchantment . While fact-checkers embrace AI’s functional affordances to enhance efficiency in tasks such as monitoring and data processing, they resist delegating the authority to determine truth to autonomous systems. At the organizational level, human-in-the-loop requirements formalize this distinction and operate as a form of boundary work that defines the limits of automation. We also identify a shared rationale for AI use across countries, which may reflect some degree of institutional convergence. However, this consensus coexists with a persistent “AI divide” shaped by contextual forces. Overall, the study shows how fact-checkers act as sociotechnical agents who “strip the magic” from AI, reaffirming human judgment as the field’s core epistemic foundation.

When Elections End, Does Incivility? Examining Online Incivility Perceptions Across Four National Elections

Eliza Mitova, Laia Castro, Pamina Syed Ali, Michael Amsler, Yuan Zhang, Frank Esser

Full text
Political incivility violates democratic, deliberative, and politeness norms, with repercussions for societal health. These repercussions are amplified in today’s digital environment, where social media platforms facilitate the expression and spread of incivility at an unprecedented global scale. Because incivility is subjective and context-dependent, its perception can only be fully understood through a joint consideration of individual factors and contextual conditions. To this end, we advance a three-level framework for examining incivility perceptions which conceptually maps the interdependence between micro-level individual factors and macro-national and meso-electoral specifics. We examine how well this framework plays out for perceptions of political incivility on social media across four nations with different media and political systems (Brazil, Spain, Switzerland, and the United States), drawing on a two-wave panel survey in the weeks leading up to and following their national elections (2022–2024). Contrary to our expectations, perceptions were highest in Brazil and the United States, the latter being the context whose permissive opportunity structures we expected to be most desensitizing. Change over the electoral period followed three distinct pathways: perceptions increased in Brazil, remained stable in Spain, and decreased in the United States and Switzerland, a pattern consistent with how decisively each electoral contest was resolved. Individual dispositions showed no uniform effects. Instead, their influence varied with national contexts. The findings suggest that online incivility perceptions may be primarily shaped by macro- and meso-level conditions, specifically the national political context and its electoral landscape. We conclude by discussing what the results imply for democratic resilience in increasingly uncivil information environments.

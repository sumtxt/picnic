American Sociological Review

Rising Wages and the Decline of Low-Wage Work

Clem Aeppli, Soohyun Roh, Nathan Wilmers

Full text
From 2014 to 2024, the share of U.S. hourly workers paid below $15 in real terms fell by two-thirds, from 38 percent to 13 percent. Why? We first distinguish the entry and exit of employers—wherein high-paying employers have supplanted low-paying ones—from organizational changes among incumbent employers. Organizational changes can be imposed on employers by external constraints, like labor market tightness, or emerge from the pursuit of new opportunities. Using panel data drawn from administrative records and job reviews, we bring this framework to bear on the recent wage gains at the bottom of the labor market. Contrary to predictions of creative destruction, we can trace only a small amount of these wage gains to the disappearance of low-wage organizations. Instead, the main driver of rising pay was low- and middle-paying incumbent employers of low-wage occupations increasing pay. These gains in low-wage occupations were associated with tightening labor markets and increased minimum wages. But over and above these external constraints, employers also pursued new opportunities for reorganizing work in low-skill jobs. Notwithstanding organizational inertia, major gains for workers’ pay can come from changes in existing workplaces.

Off the Record? Effects of Interview Audio Recordings on Interviewer Behavior

Lukas Olbrich, Jonas Beste, Joseph W Sakshaug, Silvia Schwanhäuser

Full text
Survey interviewers are usually instructed to follow standardized interviewing procedures when conducting interviews. The introduction of computer-assisted interviewing facilitated the collection of interview audio recordings to assess if interviewers indeed follow these standardized procedures. However, in most cases, audio recordings require respondent consent, and thus interviewers know whether an interview is recorded or not. In this paper, we analyze the extent to which interviewers change their behavior when interviews are recorded. Using automatically collected timestamp data and both cross-sectional and longitudinal analysis approaches to account for selection effects, we find that interviewers substantially change their behavior when recorded. Our results show that audio recordings lead to substantially fewer very short and very long questionnaire module durations and increase the average time spent on questionnaire modules, which is in line with a deterrence effect. Concerning substantive outcomes, we find larger interviewer effects for estimates derived from non-recorded than for recorded interviews.

Population and Development Review

Disrupted Timing, Enduring Norms: Return Migration and Fertility in Argentina, Romania, Senegal, and Ukraine

Inmaculada Serrano, Héctor Cebolla‐Boado, Teresa Castro‐Martín

Full text
Return migration remains understudied in the migration–fertility literature, despite returnees accounting for up to 25 percent of all international movements. This article examines whether the fertility patterns of return migrants—a highly mobile population experiencing at least two moves: migration and return—resemble or diverge from those of nonmigrants in their countries of origin. Using a unique cross‐country dataset encompassing four diverse socioeconomic and migration contexts (Argentina, Romania, Senegal, and Ukraine), we examine three key fertility outcomes: timing of first birth, completed fertility, and childlessness. Our findings reveal a general pattern of similarity between returnees and nonmigrants across nearly all contexts. A singular exception is that the transition to the first child is significantly delayed during the migration period. However, our evidence demonstrates that this disruption is largely a temporal effect, as a robust recuperation effect is observed upon return. Furthermore, although large and statistically significant delays occur during migration among women in Ukraine and men in Argentina and Senegal, these do not necessarily translate into a permanent reduction in family size. Specifically, return migrants in most groups register completed fertility similar to those of nonmigrants.

Commonalities and Differences in Lowest‐Low Fertility Patterns

Gianpiero Dalla‐Zuanna, Tiziana Leone

Full text
Lowest‐low fertility has been extensively studied, particularly in the Global North. Much less is known about whether these patterns will repeat across other regions of the world. As demographic theories evolve to explain recent trends, there is a growing need to understand how emerging patterns of fertility decline in the Global South might be (dis)similar to past trends observed in the Global North. Using an empirical approach, we examine 34 countries that experienced a period total fertility rate (TFRp) ≤ 1.3 for at least one year between 1950 and 2023, with the aim of identifying common patterns and possible trajectories. The findings reveal heterogeneous yet comparable dynamics: after initially spreading at the end of the 20th century, the diffusion of lowest‐low fertility appeared to stall. However, since the early 2010s, the number of countries with TFRp ≤ 1.3 has increased, affecting both more and less developed countries. Our cohort analysis suggests that additional countries may soon join this group. We argue that lowest‐low fertility results from the combination of declining early fertility and the absence of compensatory increases in late fertility. These processes should be examined starting from different theoretical explanations, with attention to differences between more and less developed countries.

DNA, Self-reported Ancestry, and Social Scientific Inquiry

Luyin Zhang, Sam Trejo

Full text
Highlights All humans share one vast family tree, and our place in it leaves a partial imprint on our DNA. Genetic similarity proportions estimate how closely one’s DNA resembles that of various reference groups. Our framework estimates genetic similarity while limiting bias from researchers’ cultural schemas. Within ancestry groups, genetic similarity varies widely and indexes racialized physical traits. Genetic data offer researchers a lens for studying, not disputing, the social construction of race.

New Latinx destinations, adolescent friendship, and substance use: how migrant expansion inspires network revitalization

Cassie McMillan

Full text
Contrary to political rhetoric and stereotypes perpetuated by the media, Latinx migrants report lower rates of substance use than their native-born counterparts. These unanticipated benefits are not limited to migrants themselves but also strengthen the neighborhoods where they settle. Although social networks are frequently cited as a mechanism that could explain these patterns, it is unclear whether Latinx migrant expansion restructures our social networks. The current study addresses these gaps by offering the concept of network revitalization to describe how influxes of Latinx residents can reshape and reorient patterns of interaction. I evaluate the extent to which this social process occurs by applying stochastic actor-oriented models to fifty-three longitudinal adolescent friendship networks from rural Midwestern communities. Focusing on the coevolution of youth friendship and substance use, I compare the structural dynamics of networks embedded in new Latinx destinations to those in communities without growing Latinx populations. Results indicate that adolescents who use substances are less popular, less social, and less likely to form homogenous friendships when they attend school in new Latinx destinations versus non-destinations, though variations emerge across different substances. These findings suggest that network revitalization is an overlooked meso-level process that can help explain why migrant expansion enhances community well-being.

Class diplomacy: cross-class friendship, social mobility, and the promise of symbolic exchange

Sam Friedman, Rose Butler

Full text
Economists in the United States and UK have recently suggested that cross-class friendships are widespread and provide an important source of social capital for people from working-class backgrounds. However, these findings sit in tension with a broader sociological literature indicating that friendships rest on principles of reciprocity and equality and that cross-class friendships are rarely successful. In this paper, we interrogate this tension by drawing on forty-two in-depth interviews with people in cross-class friendships in the UK. Our findings indicate that while friendships formed across class can produce social capital for those from less advantaged backgrounds, such one-way transfers often generate feelings of inequality or indebtedness. In some instances, this is a source of ongoing tension or downward spirals in friendships. However, in successful cross-class friendships, we find that class difference is negotiated via a process we term “class diplomacy.” We outline three modes of class diplomacy: (1) joking—using humor to recognize and defuse class tensions; (2) avoiding—prioritizing emotional intimacy over discussions of class to protect the friendship; and (3) confronting—openly discussing class differences to build empathy and shared understanding. Our findings suggest that, when enacted successfully, class diplomacy can pave the way for forms of symbolic exchange to develop between friends, which both has a mutual socializing effect and can lead to a heightened political sensitivity to class inequality.

European Sociological Review

Scarcity in crises: access disparities to COVID-19 vaccines among natives, migrants, and refugees in Germany

Laura Goßner

Full text
This paper investigates disparities in the timing of COVID-19 vaccination uptake among natives, migrants, and refugees in Germany. Using data from the representative, nationwide RKI-SOEP-2 survey and employing Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, Cox proportional hazard models, and an Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition, the study reveals a significant temporal gap and associated factors. Natives received their vaccination earlier than migrants, and those in turn earlier than refugees. While accounting for differences in eligibility and individual vaccination intentions, the paper examines the role of potential access barriers. In line with fundamental cause theory, key flexible resources such as income, language skills, social networks, and access to transportation are found to be significantly associated with vaccination timing. Differences in these flexible resources account for a substantial share of the observed gap in vaccination timing between natives, migrants, and refugees. The paper highlights how structural disadvantages can have direct public health implications. The results stress that—beyond the health crisis of the COVID-19 pandemic—ensuring fair access to scarce resources is essential to prevent existing social inequalities from translating into health disparities during crises.

My house, my rules: Chinese aid, leader birth regions, and the encroachment of protected areas

Stefano Jud, Anthony Luongo

Full text
Foreign-funded development projects have long come into conflict with environmental protection efforts, including by encroaching on protected areas (PAs). We investigate the conditions under which countries are more likely to allow foreign aid projects to infringe on PAs. Drawing on the selective enforcement literature, we hypothesize that leaders are more likely to permit incursions into PAs when projects are located in their home region, as local beneficiaries can translate the resulting rents into political support for the incumbent, while the risk of substantial public backlash over environmental violations is comparatively muted. Using geocoded data on 3,675 Chinese infrastructure projects, we find no overall increase in PA encroachments associated with leaders’ birth regions. However, the aggregate null masks a positive association in Africa, where projects placed in a leader’s birth region are roughly 10 percentage points more likely to encroach on PAs than projects outside the birth region. This association is concentrated in a few countries and in energy, mining, and industry projects. Exploratory analyses suggest that domestic public opinion toward China and the political salience of ethnicity moderate this effect. We find no analogous relationship for World Bank projects.

Whose priorities? Media reporting and the output legitimacy of International Organizations

Giorgio Malet, Tom Hunter, Johannes Scherzinger, Stefanie Walter

Full text
Traditional accounts of output legitimacy emphasize that support for multipurpose international organizations (IOs) depends on their ability to deliver effective policies. We argue that legitimacy beliefs also hinge on which policies IOs are perceived to prioritize, and that media reporting on the policy focus of international organizations plays an important role in this process. Such reporting can shape legitimacy beliefs by influencing the extent to which citizens perceive an IO’s policy output to align with their own ideological preferences. Using the European Union as a test case, we analyze over 400,000 news articles from Austria, France, Germany, and Spain with semi-supervised machine learning to identify issue emphasis. We link this to Eurobarometer survey data capturing individual-level assessments of the EU’s output legitimacy. We find that citizens tend to evaluate the EU more positively when media coverage highlights policy areas aligned with their ideology. These findings suggest that citizens judge IOs not only by how well they perform, but also by whose priorities they are perceived to serve.

Journal of Peace Research

Strategic communication under constraint: using nonstate actor media to study relational dynamics in civil war

Emily K Gade, Shahryar Minhas, Bree Bang-Jensen, Kai Ping Leung, Michael Gabbay, Mohammed Hafez, Kayla Salehian, Ava Sharifi, Emily Kate Thomas, Arica Schuett, ... (+3 authors)

Full text
Between 2012 and 2018, Syrian rebel groups produced tens of thousands of social media posts documenting conflict events, alliances, and organizational identities. Although this media is propagandistic and not held to journalistic standards, it captures micro-level dynamics that conventional news sources often overlook, especially among smaller and lesser-known actors. In this article, we evaluate whether rebel-produced content can serve as a useful foundation for relational and locational observational conflict data. Drawing on roughly 28,000 social media posts, we construct the SAGAA (Syrian Armed Groups, Alliances, and Actions) dataset and leverage a latent variable network framework to compare the relational signal captured in SAGAA to that captured in the widely used UCDP Syria event dataset. We find that while SAGAA contains fewer total events, it captures a denser and less aggregated network of inter-rebel relationships—including ties absent from news sources but critical to understanding fragmented civil wars. These results are consistent with a theory of rebel self-reporting as strategic communication under constraint: armed groups have incentives to distort outcomes or minimize civilian harm, but face stronger credibility constraints when reporting jointly observable relational and locational details. Thus, we demonstrate the value of rebel media for recovering the relational structure and localized interactions that shape conflict dynamics, while underscoring the need for conflict-specific, triangulated approaches that complement rather than replace traditional news-based datasets.

Political violence and waves of regime change

Mario Ferrero

Full text
A widely held view in conflict scholarship holds that nonviolent action is more successful than violent movements. This paper argues that a movement aiming at regime change that is part of a wave of similar movements is most likely to succeed if it relies on both violence and nonviolence. In a wave context, violence can remain a potential threat that affects subsequent movements, leading to domino effects that make outcomes in different countries interdependent. Observed levels of violence and nonviolence are thus a poor proxy for the real mechanisms of regime change. We develop a simple economic model of revolutionary overthrow of a regime and apply it to three waves of regime change: decolonization after World War II, the communist revolutions of 1917–1923, and the communist downfall of 1989–1991. Once the potential for violence is accounted for, coexistence of both techniques appears ubiquitous, often leading to the regime's disengagement. The failure of the communist revolution wave was due to the revolutionaries' misperception of past violence, driving them to insufficient resort to actual violence.

Introducing UNGAInsight (1990–2023): new data on the United Nations General Assembly’s agenda

Bridget L Coggins, Jungjae Hong, Julia C Morse, Annjulie Vester

Full text
Scholars use roll-call voting in the United Nations General Assembly to understand various political phenomena. Yet information on the politics of voting is limited because scholars lack details on the content and context of resolutions. This article fills this gap by providing new data: UNGAInsight (UNGA-I). UNGA-I complements existing datasets with new data on resolutions from 1990 to 2023, offering 32 mutually exclusive substantive topic categories that map onto the organization’s own committee-centered workflow. UNGA-I also includes information on which countries are targeted by specific resolutions and the nature of related demands. Finally, it provides more detailed information on the political dynamics that undergird absences. The data will allow researchers to develop more nuanced understandings of the UN and its political relevance across time. Additional insight into the UNGA’s agenda also raises questions about the common practice of using UN voting patterns as an indicator of alignment with the U.S.-led liberal international order.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

From Secular to Sectarian: How Political Secularism’s Decline Fuels Violence

Nilay Saiya, Stuti Manchanda

Full text
This article argues that contemporary religious violence arises largely from a global crisis of political secularism. Whereas much of the literature on the causes of religious violence emphasizes the conflict behavior of minority religious communities, we argue instead that religious violence stems centrally from empowered religious majorities , not from embattled religious minorities—a dynamic that has been exacerbated by the global secularism crisis. When states disproportionately discriminate against minority religious groups, they encourage extremists from favored groups to undertake violence. To test this theory, we employ a longitudinal statistical analysis of majoritarian religious violence across countries. The results reveal a robust positive association between government discrimination against religious minorities and majoritarian violence, consistent with our argument that the erosion of political secularism creates conditions conducive to religious violence.

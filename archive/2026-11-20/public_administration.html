Aim High: The Impacts of Marijuana Legalization on Air Force Recruitment, Drug Use, and Disciplinary Actions

Zachary S. Fone, Aaron Albert, Anne Portlock

Full text
This study examines the labor market impacts of marijuana legalization in a unique setting: a large employer, the Department of the Air Force (DAF), with strict drug use policy for its employees (military service members). US military recruiting has grown more difficult, partly due to declining eligibility among young adults, with drug use cited as a factor. The spread of recreational marijuana laws (RMLs) may affect recruitment, drug use, and discipline among service members. Using a difference‐in‐differences approach, we find that RMLs do not impede recruit quality, although there is some evidence of fewer Black people recruited in RML states. Among serving military members, positive marijuana tests increase for some groups (particularly White enlisted members), but overall illicit drug use shows little evidence of rising, as part of the increase reflects substitution across substances. We find no strong evidence of changes in overall discipline but do find increases in grade reductions (a form of discipline), possibly reflecting differential disciplinary treatment of marijuana relative to other substances or differential behavioral responses to its use.

Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory

“Unseen and unheard”: Recognition and Its Role in Shaping Administrative Burden

Noam Tarshish

Full text
Administrative burden scholarship has identified psychological costs alongside learning and compliance costs, yet the category remains homogeneous, grouping stigma, diminished autonomy, and stress, without a shared organizing principle. This article advances a mechanism-level account centered on recognition, drawing on a qualitative case study of claiming disability benefits in Israel, based on in-depth interviews with 33 individuals (16 young adults with diagnosed mental health conditions and 17 social and outreach workers). We argue that recognition, and its denial, are part of the mechanism through which psychological costs are produced, and that whether an encounter carries a message about a person's standing sorts the category into three analytically distinct types. Affective costs are emotional responses to procedural load and involve no recognition; primary symbolic costs are emotional responses to status positioning; and secondary symbolic costs arise when procedural load itself carries a message about standing. The latter two, in which recognition is at stake, are traced through Honneth's three spheres of rights, solidarity, and care, each producing its own configuration of costs. The types differ in their causes and remedies, with affective costs calling for efficiency reforms and symbolic costs for recognition-oriented ones. Social workers emerge as allies who counter misrecognition, and drawing on these findings we propose bureaucratic recognition, the building of voice, certainty, and validation into procedural design, as a remedy for status-related costs. Finally, misrecognition both constitutes symbolic costs and compounds others, positioning status, alongside distribution, as a critical axis of administrative burden.

Policy Design and Polarization: How Policy Characteristics Moderate the Relationship Between Issue and Social Polarization

Johnathan Noah Wolff, Deven E. Carlson, Adam Tyner

Full text
Political scientists distinguish between issue‐based polarization (i.e., partisan differences in policy preferences) and identity‐based polarization (i.e., social animus between individuals with different identities, political or otherwise) and have shown that the latter can drive increases in the former. We extend this work by exploring whether policy characteristics moderate the extent to which identity‐based polarization leads to issue polarization. We draw on prior research to identify seven policy characteristics across three broad categories (policy uncertainty, policy breadth, and policy familiarity) that, when combined, shape the likelihood of identity‐based polarization driving issue polarization around the policy. Our empirical assessment of this proposition draws on data from two separate surveys that polled respondents about their views on three different education policy topics: Social and Emotional Learning, Critical Race Theory, and affirmative action. Consistent with our expectations, results across each of the three policy areas show that identity‐based gaps in support for policies that exhibit these characteristics are substantially larger than support gaps around policies where these characteristics are absent.

Does policy evaluation depend on the quality of democracy?

Julián David Salazar, Johanna Hornung

Full text
Policy evaluation contributes to the quality of democracy, but does the quality of democracy, in turn, contribute to the production and use of policy evaluation? Existing research provides evidence for the former claim but not for the latter. Addressing this research gap, this article examines the pertinence of democratic characteristics alongside or in combination with economic, bureaucratic and educational capacities and analyses the extent to which they are necessary or sufficient for the production and use of policy evaluation. Drawing on Bertelsmann’s Sustainable Governance Indicators (SGI), the analysis applies fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA) to all 41 OECD and EU countries. The results suggest that a strong balance of powers and access to information are necessary, but not sufficient conditions for the production and use of policy evaluation. Instead, democratic settings provide an enabling context that must be matched by strong bureaucratic and educational capacities for policy evaluation to be produced and used. Conversely, pathways involving weak democratic accountability, insufficient educational and economic capacities, or insufficient bureaucratic capacity, result in the absence of the production and use of policy evaluation. By shedding light on the tenuous relationship between policy evaluation and democracy, this article carries important implications for policy evaluation and evidence use in countries facing democratic decline and the political erosion of state capacities.

The politics of policy transfer: political dynamics in OECD accession

Vicente Silva, Ivar Vargas

Full text
While recent scholarship has called for greater attention to the political dynamics of policy transfer, this dimension remains under-theorised in policy studies. Existing work has convincingly shown that transfer is politically motivated yet often lacks analytical tools capable of systematically unpacking these dynamics. This article examines the politics of policy transfer empirically. Drawing on political sociology, it conceptualises policy transfer as a process of mobilisation by interested actors occupying positions within organisational fields. These actors use policy transfer as an opportunity to mobilise resources and advance their positions, while seeking to legitimate their choices among national and transnational audiences. Building on three analytical dimensions – fields, opportunities, and legitimacy – the article examines policy transfer associated with the accession of Chile and Colombia to the OECD during the 2010s and 2020s. The analysis shows that transfer was driven, at the transnational level, by the OECD’s efforts to reinforce its relevance within the multilateral field, particularly in the Latin American region, while domestically it was shaped by governing elites seeking economic capital in the form of foreign investment and symbolic capital through international recognition. The article concludes by showing how attention to fields, opportunities, and legitimacy can advance understanding of the political dynamics that shape policy transfer.

Explaining policy stasis on same-sex unions in Peru

Carlos Alza-Barco

Full text
This article advances understanding of how policy monopolies sustain policy stasis in morality policy, where scholarship has explained policy change more fully than its absence. It develops the concept of a moral policy monopoly to examine how interlocking mechanisms can sustain policy stasis in contexts of high religiosity. Using abductive, mechanistic and analyticist process tracing, and drawing on documents and interviews, the study examines three decades of legislative inertia on same-sex unions in Peru, where 17 Bills were submitted to Congress between 1993 and 2024 and none advanced to plenary approval. The analysis identifies five interlocking mechanisms through which ultraconservative religious actors sustain stasis under conditions of high religiosity, substantial church capacity and a religiously embedded party system. Forging alliances, building moralised policy images and capturing politics operate continuously, while joint opposition campaigns and competing Bills operate episodically in response to triggering events. Each mechanism presupposes or reinforces the others, so stasis emerges from interlocking and deliberate activity rather than dispersed opposition. By showing how these mechanisms work together to prevent policy change, the article demonstrates how policy monopolies can actively sustain stasis in morality policy. These mechanisms also reveal religiosity not as a background condition but as a resource that actors convert into political capacity.

Why policy actors choose not to coordinate in policy conflicts

Fredrik Johansson, Örjan Bodin, Daniel Nohrstedt

Full text
Research on advocacy coalitions in public policy highlights the importance of coordination among policy actors seeking to influence policy making. Less attention has been devoted to why actors sometimes choose not to coordinate, even when they share similar beliefs or interests. In this study, we explore the rationales behind actors’ decisions to refrain from publicly visible coordination in wind power development projects in two Swedish municipalities over a 16-year period. Our findings show that, while most actors engage in coordination at some point, they also refrain from coordinating with others for several reasons. First, they perceive alternative means of exerting influence as more cost-effective; second, they have concerns about the trustworthiness of like-minded actors; third, they view the long-term consequences of coordination as uncertain; and fourth, they are less willing to coordinate when conflict is high, unless they represent political parties. These findings show that shared beliefs or interests do not necessarily translate into coordination and identify conditions that can constrain collective action among policy actors. The study contributes to research on advocacy coalitions by bringing decisions not to coordinate more explicitly into explanations of collective action in contested policy subsystems.

Negotiating Competing Expectations—Chiefs and Mineral Governance in Ghana

Joel Agbesinyale

Full text
This paper examines what shapes chiefs' discretionary decision‐making and how they exercise authority within hybrid governance arrangements, focusing on Ghana's artisanal and small‐scale mining (ASM) sector. Existing scholarship recognizes that traditional authorities can strengthen or undermine state governance but offers limited explanations for variation in their practices. Drawing on interviews with chiefs, miners, and state officials, the paper develops a discretionary governance framework that integrates hybrid governance, access theory, and discretion. It argues that overlapping customary and statutory authority creates a discretionary space for chiefs to navigate competing expectations. Chiefs exercise this discretion through diverse governance practices, including restricting access to mining sites, mobilizing communities, and collaborating with state institutions, as well as facilitating access to land, negotiating informal payments, and shielding unauthorised miners from enforcement. These practices can reinforce, negotiate, circumvent, or undermine formal mining regulation. The paper advances debates on traditional authority and hybrid governance while challenging assumptions that constitutionally empowering chiefs necessarily strengthens mineral governance.

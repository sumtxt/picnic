American Political Science Review

On Liberalism’s Global Career: Dadabhai Naoroji and Representative Government

TEJAS PARASHER

Full text
This article reconsiders the relationship between the globalization of political liberalism and the globalization of political equality. Departing from interpretations of liberal theories of rights and representation as egalitarian or democratic, the article reconstructs an elitist paradigm of liberal representative government from the colonial world. It does so by offering a new reading of the thought of Indian political leader Dadabhai Naoroji. It recovers Naoroji’s overlooked commentary on political representation and imperial reform. I show that Naoroji drew upon analyses made by the first generation of Indian liberal thinkers about imperial political economy and mass-elite relations and used these arguments to formulate a distinctive model of electoral politics as a form of hierarchical tutelage. The recovery of Naoroji’s theory illustrates the deep tensions between representation and mass politics that marked liberalism’s global circulation.

The Ambiguities of Ownership and Class: A Reply to Nicholas Vrousalis

CHIARA CORDELLI

Full text
The article replies to Nicholas Vrousalis’s attempt to rescue radical republicanism from my critique that it fails on three counts: its non-contingency, domination, and structural injustice theses. Central to my response are the claims that (i) capitalist power cannot be reduced to legal ownership of the means of production, as the process of capitalization is constitutive of capital; (ii) neither neo-republican nor Kantian notions of domination adequately capture what is inherently wrong with capitalism, and (iii) the concept of “class domination” remains theoretically puzzling. I then defend an alienation-centered critique: capitalism’s mode of investment subjects citizens to an impersonal and anarchical form of governance, which, by being necessarily alienating, contradicts democratic self-determination. Overall, this critique forms the core of a “political, political theory of capitalism” and grounds the case for a specific kind of democratic socialism—socialism as reconciliation—which is more demanding than both republican and liberal socialisms.

The Symbolic Politics of Housing

DAVID E. BROOCKMAN, CHRISTOPHER S. ELMENDORF, JOSHUA L. KALLA

Full text
Voter opposition to development contributes to America’s housing shortage. Prevailing explanations emphasize NIMBYism (opposition to housing nearby) and homeowners’ self-interest. However, homeownership often fails to predict housing policy views, while NIMBYism offers little insight into support for policies that do not concern specific proposed housing projects—the focus of much recent policymaking. Drawing on symbolic politics theory, we argue that voters’ affect toward groups that housing policies make salient powerfully shapes their judgments about housing policies. We present evidence supporting this theory. Experiments show that affect toward developers and investors who build housing, government actors who approve it, and residents who move into it explain anti-development preferences when policies make these groups salient. Providing further causal evidence, short-form videos that manipulate affect toward developers have meaningful downstream effects on support for new development and permitting reforms. Ironically, voter dislike of the very actors who could ameliorate the housing shortage further exacerbates it.

American Journal of Political Science

Structure and agency in emancipatory reform: A blueprint from a Black union

Benjamin T. Lynerd

Full text
The National Labor Convention of 1869, the first of its kind for African American workers, yielded a grand strategy for achieving full emancipation, in which those long subjected to domination might attain durable independence. Drawing upon decades of Black republican thought, this agenda combined macro‐level reforms targeting agrarian and industrial monopolies with micro‐level interventions into the habits of the working class. While the project fell short, its multivalent approach nevertheless offers republican theorists today a framework for better understanding the structural dynamics of domination and how to overcome them.

Comparative Political Studies

Perpetrator Identity and Post-Conflict Ethnic Attitudes: The Legacies of Intragroup and Intergroup Violence

Joan Barceló, Keshana Ratnasingham

Full text
Researchers have extensively examined how political violence shapes social and political behavior, yet most studies focus on interethnic violence and assume a binary conflict structure. This study challenges that assumption by examining how perpetrator identity, ingroup versus outgroup, shapes postwar intergroup relations. Using original survey data from Tamil civilians in Northern Sri Lanka, we analyze exposure to intragroup and intergroup violence during Sri Lanka’s 26-year civil war, which involved substantial violence both across and within ethnic groups. We find that exposure to intragroup violence is associated with lower trust toward coethnics, reduced ingroup favoritism in attitudes and behavior, and weaker reliance on ethnic cues in political choice. In contrast, exposure to intergroup violence increases interethnic prejudice and social distance, heightens discrimination in behavioral interactions, and strengthens ethnically motivated political behavior. These findings indicate that perpetrator identity shapes the social legacies of wartime violence and can generate opposing postwar dynamics.

Queenpins: Women Leaders and Violence in Organized Crime

Fernanda Quintanilla Domínguez, Marco Alcocer

Full text
How does women’s leadership affect the behavior of criminal organizations? Organized criminal groups are male-dominated patriarchal organizations that use violence to govern, compete, and control territory. Qualitative research suggests that women leaders—though rare—reduce their organization’s violent behavior. We examine this expectation through Mexico’s Tijuana Cartel, leveraging the sudden 2008 ascension of a woman to leadership. Although violence increased under her tenure, synthetic control results show greater reliance on hidden violence and less on visible violence and territorial expansion. Original data on male cartel leaders reveals that systematic gender differences in trajectories help explain these patterns. Our results indicate that women leaders are not necessarily less violent than men but instead strategically employ different violent repertoires. Case studies of 44 women leaders across 18 countries since 1850 assess the broader applicability of these findings and identify important scope conditions. Our findings underscore the importance of incorporating gender into organized crime research.

Perspectives on Politics

Agenda over Authority: Why Right-Wing Populists Contest the Liberal International Order

Anna Wojciuk, Maciej A. Górecki, Maciej Kisilowski

Full text
Right-wing populist (RWP) governments are widely regarded as major challengers of the liberal international order (LIO), yet existing scholarship has not systematically established why they contest some international organizations more strongly than others. Is such contestation driven by populist opposition to supranational authority, or by substantive conflicts between right-wing ideology and particular international organizations’ agendas? We distinguish these mechanisms and derive expectations across the domains of human rights and the rule of law, trade, and security. To test them, we draw on an original cross-national expert survey measuring RWP governments’ approval of individual international organizations in 13 countries. The results show that an organization’s agenda matters more consistently than its authority. RWP governments are least supportive of organizations promoting human rights and the rule of law, display mixed attitudes toward trade-related bodies, and are more approving of security organizations. In the security domain, greater international organization authority is associated with higher rather than lower approval. These findings challenge explanations that attribute RWP contestation primarily to populist anti-elitism or generalized resistance to supranational authority. Instead, such contestation is selective and reflects a deeper conflict between right-wing ideological commitments and the egalitarian norms advanced by parts of the LIO.

The Politics of Black Classification: Sociopolitical Cues and Racial Perception

Lauren D. Davenport, Hakeem Jefferson, Hunter E. Rendleman

Full text
What makes someone Black in American society today? From Donald Trump questioning Kamala Harris’s racial identity to Joe Biden’s claim that hesitant Black voters “ain’t Black,” American politics frequently brings questions of racial authenticity and belonging to the surface. Yet political science often approaches race as a fixed attribute, rather than a social construction. Here, we seek to understand how Americans define blackness in social and political life. Using a conjoint experiment with a racially diverse sample that includes Black, White, and mixed-race Black-White respondents, we evaluate how ascribed and acquired traits influence perceptions of blackness. The results show that inherited characteristics—particularly parentage and skin tone, which are the strongest determinants of racial classification—play a central role, yet sociopolitical cues such as partisanship, neighborhood context, and spousal race also influence racial classification. Using a continuous measure, we also show that respondents make graded assessments of blackness rather than purely binary classifications, with some individuals perceived as more Black than others. Black respondents are more likely than White respondents to classify a broader set of profiles as Black, consistent with a more inclusive understanding of racial membership; however, they also place greater emphasis on shared political identity. These findings clarify how racial categories are socially constructed and why that construction carries real political and social consequences.

Downward Competition: How Perceived Economic Proximity Reduces Support for Redistribution to the Poor

Matias Engdal Christensen

Full text
How does inducing comparisons to both the poor and the rich shape support for redistribution toward the poor? I argue that when individuals who identify as middle-income perceive economic proximity to the poor alongside distance from the rich, one of two opposing mechanisms is activated: a social affinity mechanism, which increases support for redistribution, or a status and economic competition mechanism, which reduces it. Drawing on two survey experiments in the United Kingdom ( n = 3,470) that induce bidirectional comparisons, as well as observational data from 14 democracies, I find that support for redistribution to the poor is lowest when perceived downward proximity coincides with upward distance. Suggestive evidence shows that economic and status-based competition drive this relationship. Together, these findings advance our understanding of how social comparison processes shape attitudes toward redistribution and offer insights for policy makers when designing redistributive policies.

Opinionrot: Toward a Theory of Collective Reasoning Decay in the Age of Synthetic Consensus

Maud Reveilhac

Full text
This article introduces the concept of opinionrot to describe the structural erosion of collective reasoning within contemporary information systems. Rather than treating post-truth politics as a failure of individual rationality or as a decline in information quality alone, the article argues that public reasoning is increasingly shaped by infrastructures that capture attention, monetize cognitive bias, and automate the conditions under which claims become visible and credible. Methodologically, the article proceeds through a conceptual-historical reconstruction, tracing how persuasion became institutionalized, platformized, and increasingly predictive. It identifies three mechanisms through which opinionrot intensifies today: cognitive capture, infrastructural decay, and discursive saturation. The framework is illustrated through debates over equality and affirmative action, showing how communicative infrastructures can transform contested political concepts into predictable and polarized semantic routines. By shifting the analysis from discrete falsehoods to the conditions under which public judgment is formed, contested, and revised, the article offers a diagnostic vocabulary for studying how information infrastructures increasingly convert deliberation into statistical efficiency within contemporary economies of attention.

Beyond Sovereignty or Exile: The Limits of Exilic and Diasporic Jewish Politics and the Egalitarian Potential of Jewish Nationality

Miko Zeldes-Roth

Full text
This article argues that Jewish accounts of exile or diaspora predicated on disaggregating Jewishness from Zionism cannot sustain the nonsovereign Jewish politics they endorse. Although these accounts provide new grounding for Jewish critiques of Zionism and the State of Israel, they prioritize a Jewishness that is not implicated in Jewish political responsibility for Palestinian subordination. As a result, they have a more contingent relationship to binational political equality between Jews and Palestinians. To overcome these issues, I offer a normative alternative: a creolized reading of Jewish nationality. I argue that such an account, which accepts the Zionization of Jewishness, can better support a Jewish communal turn toward binational political equality. This is a reading of Jewish nationality defined as the enactment of a localized, binational coalition with Palestinians based on a recognition of present inequality and shared opposition to Palestinian subordination. Such a politics could frame the State of Israel as a threat to Jewish politics not because it abrogates an idealized notion of Jewish identity or ethics but because it is hostile to the egalitarian, binational partnership on which any such politics must rest.

From the Authoritarian Playbook: Academic Freedom and the Politics of Self-Censorship in Florida

Janika Spannagel

Full text
Between 2021 and 2023, the State of Florida enacted numerous bills and took administrative actions interfering with university autonomy and academic freedom amid a combative political discourse vilifying universities as too “woke.” Defenders of these interventions have argued that academic teaching and research content have not been censored. However, research on self-censorship under authoritarianism suggests that the effects of forceful state intervention may be more complex and long-lasting than they appear. This article addresses this issue by expanding theories on “chilling effects” in democracies, conceptualizing them as a potential instrument of soft repression that illiberal political actors may use to suppress content while circumventing judicial scrutiny. Based on 60 qualitative interviews conducted in early 2024 at six higher education institutions in Florida, the study shows how pervasive uncertainty and informal intimidation led scholars to self-censor in their teaching and research in ways not prescribed by law. Although these behaviors vary across individuals and are partly mediated by institutional responses, they are not isolated cases and are likely to have a significant impact on Florida higher education.

Digitally Networked Territorial Control: Digital Communication and Hybrid Order in the Urban Periphery

Daniel Rincón Machón

Full text
How do armed actors leverage digital communication for territorial control? In violently contested urban environments (often known as “hybrid orders”), territorial control involves regulating who belongs in a specific geographical area. Enforcing these rules, in turn, requires gathering fine-grained information to evaluate threats and selectively project violence. This study illustrates how the affordances of digital communication platforms help armed actors to do so. Using a comparative ethnographic approach and open-source research methods, the article analyzes three digital intelligence-gathering practices used by criminal groups and militarized police in Fortaleza’s urban periphery (Brazil): smartphone inspections, digital information-sharing networks, and social media monitoring. The findings reveal how the structural features of digital communication platforms facilitate accessing and disseminating strategic information, which in turn introduces effectiveness, flexibility, and efficiency gains in territorial control. This suggests that digital media affordances play an important role in contemporary forms of territorial competition by armed groups.

Do Appeals to the Diaspora Affect their Attachment to the Homeland? Findings from Jewish-Americans before and during the Israel-Hamas War

Yehonatan Abramson, Anil Menon, Alon Yakter

Full text
Due to the growing importance of diasporas in contemporary geopolitics, many states seek to cultivate diasporic attachment and encourage diaspora mobilization using strategic narratives. Are such appeals effective? We report results from a pre-registered two-wave survey experiment conducted among Jewish Americans before and during the Israel-Hamas war. Our intervention assesses the impact of three common appeals identified by prior work: threats to the homeland, shared historical memory, and discrimination in the diaspora’s country of residence. We find that none of these appeals influenced Jewish-Americans’ attachment to, support for, or intent to mobilize for Israel, even during a war in the “homeland.” Instead, baseline differences in these outcomes reflect deep-seated generational, denominational, and partisan cleavages within the Jewish American community. These findings suggest that intra-diaspora fragmentation and long-standing views of the “homeland” might dominate short-term rhetorical interventions. Consequently, strategic frames deployed by kin states and political animators may be less effective on diaspora audiences than intended.

Immigration and Nationalistic Attitudes: Panel Evidence from Chile

Catalina Vega-Méndez, Giancarlo Visconti

Full text
Does rapid and large-scale migration fuel nationalism? Using administrative and panel survey data from Chile, we show that sudden, large-scale migration inflows increase identity-based nationalistic attitudes, such as national pride, among native-born citizens. These “immigration shocks” appear to activate perceptions of identity threat, leading to stronger expressions of national pride and belonging. Supplementary evidence from surveys, media reports, social media, and crime records suggests that this nationalism is predominantly exclusionary: areas most affected by migration also experienced spikes in anti-migrant rhetoric, hate crimes, and support for a newly formed far-right party. These findings underscore how rapid demographic changes can reshape political attitudes and contribute to the rise of exclusionary forms of national identity.

The Labour Market Link: An explanation of Brexit support

Katy Morris

Full text
Where can we expect support for populist parties and initiatives to be weakest, and why? We argue support will be weakest in places that have experienced strong growth of employment in high-quality jobs, because such growth has positive spillover effects that shape wider perceptions of the economic status quo and the prevailing ideological climate. We test this proposition in relation to Brexit support at the time of the 2016 EU referendum and in the present day, using Census data to proxy the rate at which highly paid jobs replaced low- and mid-paid jobs in English and Welsh Local Authority Districts 1991–2016, and substantiate it at both the area and the individual-level. High-quality employment growth is a more powerful predictor of Brexit support than alternative local economic indicators and individual-level effects are sizeable. All else being equal, analysis of Understanding Society data shows the probability of Brexit support is only 30% among respondents in places that experienced very strong high-quality employment growth, compared to 55% among those in places with negative growth. These findings have important implications for how left behind places should be defined and for strategies that seek to address political discontent.

Public Opinion Quarterly

How the Public Makes Sense of Gender Politics: Textual, Emotional, and Symbolic Dimensions

Marta Fraile, Nerea Gándara-Guerra, Paula Zuluaga-Borrero

Full text
How do ordinary citizens make sense of gender politics? While research has examined public support for feminism and gender equality either separately or as interchangeable concepts, far less is known about the meanings citizens attach to these labels and whether they evoke distinct political meanings. This article compares the textual, emotional, and symbolic associations of three related but distinct framings of gender politics: feminism, gender equality, and equality between men and women. Drawing on an original pre-registered survey experiment in Spain, respondents were randomly assigned to define and evaluate one of the three labels, allowing us to analyze their meanings across textual, emotional, and visual dimensions. We find that while most respondents express sympathy toward all three labels, feminism stands as the most contested. Feminism also has the sharpest sociopolitical profile, eliciting greater support from young, highly educated, progressive women. Text analyses reveal that the public frames feminism in terms of collective action, conflict, and women’s identity, drawing heavily on political language, while the other two labels emphasize policy and material concerns. Emotionally, gender politics elicits more positive than negative responses across all three labels, though men report slightly higher negative emotions toward feminism. Feminism is also the only label consistently associated with images of women’s mobilization and protest, reinforcing its distinctively political resonance. These findings show that feminism and gender equality tap related but meaningfully distinct constructs in the public mind, with implications for how scholars measure attitudes toward gender politics and interpret public support for equality-related policies.

Attitudes Toward LGB and TGD Rights in Latin America

Oscar Castorena, Eli G Rau, Valerie Schweizer-Robinson, Elizabeth J Zechmeister

Full text
Research on attitudes toward transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) individuals lags behind research on opinion on the lesbian, gay, and bisexual (LGB) population, leaving open questions about public opinion on these two LGBTQ+ subgroups, in general and in particular in the Global South. While a sizeable gap exists in US public opinion toward TGD and LGB rights, we do not know whether that finding is generalizable to other contexts. With a focus on Latin America, we draw on a range of scholarship to identify factors that could result in a similar divergence of attitudes toward these subgroups or, instead, relative convergence. Our analyses focus primarily on novel survey data from representative surveys across thirteen Latin American countries. Our results demonstrate a fair amount of convergence in mean levels, item nonresponse rates, and predictors of views on LGB versus TGD rights. We present some notable differences across countries and issues, with results for the former (countries) varying by socio-political context and the latter (issues) varying across political and nonpolitical domains. We identify these patterns as constituting a future agenda for researchers of opinion on LGBTQ+ topics. We conclude that, in the contemporary period, the Latin American public holds fairly similar mental schemas toward LGB and TGD groups.

Political Science Research and Methods

Private-sector support for programmatic candidates: evidence from a Senegalese election

Abhit Bhandari, Lucas Borba, Jessica Gottlieb

Full text
Demand for clientelism in low-income countries distorts political incentives and constrains growth. Informal workers are seen as a strong clientelist constituency because they rely on forbearance and targeted benefits, yet they also constitute an electoral bloc that could coordinate in support of beneficial programmatic policies. We examine whether—and how—such demand for clientelism can be attenuated. We implement an information experiment with firm owners ahead of Senegal’s 2022 local elections, during which an upstart political faction campaigned on programmatic reform. Reminding informal workers of their coordination capacity reduced engagement with dominant clientelistic networks. This shift did not translate into support for programmatic alternatives but into political disengagement. Demand for clientelism can be mitigated, but programmatic mobilization remains a challenge.

Between reconciliation and peace making and brutalization: The Israeli case

Daniel Bar‐Tal

Full text
This article examines the relationship between peacemaking, reconciliation, and brutalization in violent conflicts. It argues that these processes are deeply interconnected: effective peacemaking and reconciliation can interrupt or reverse brutalization, whereas unsuccessful or incomplete peacemaking may allow brutalization to persist, deepen, and become normalized, even alongside formal reconciliation initiatives. This article is organized into two parts. The first one reviews the scholarly literature on peacemaking, reconciliation, and brutalization. It examines each process separately. Then proposes an analytical model that explains the emergence, escalation, and persistence of brutalization. Finally, it conceptualizes its relationship with peacemaking and reconciliation. The second part applies this model to the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, focusing on Israeli Jewish society in its confrontation with Palestinians in the Gaza Strip. This case study serves to illustrate the model and assess its explanatory value. Beyond examining peacemaking and reconciliation, the article's primary objective is to develop a comprehensive conceptualization of brutalization by defining its nature, identifying its underlying psychological and social mechanisms, and analyzing its antecedents and consequences for collective cognition, emotions, behavior, and norms.

Solidarity between U.S. people of color: Synthesizing evidence, highlighting challenges, and proposing actionable solutions

Efrén Pérez

Full text
Mounting research establishes that solidarity between U.S. people of color (PoC) is triggered when a racially stigmatized ingroup believes they are discriminated similarly to another outgroup based on their alleged inferiority or foreignness . This heightened solidarity then produces political opinions and behaviors that benefit stigmatized outgroups who are not one's own—for example, Black adults become more pro‐Latino, Asian adults become more pro‐Black, and Latino adults become more pro‐Asian. This essay organizes, discusses, and synthesizes the overflowing evidence on this solidarity mechanism, paying special attention to its generality across stigmatized groups and political settings. The essay then highlights major pending challenges and opportunities to learn more about U.S. inter‐minority solidarity, emphasizing rival explanations, complementary pathways, and methodological pluralism. In parallel, this latter discussion illustrates the advantages of creating greater conceptual and theoretical synergy with comparativist research on ethnic identities and political conflict in nations across sub‐Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Western Europe. Finally, the article outlines new research agendas to deepen our understanding about U.S. inter‐minority politics, paying special attention to intra‐ group processes that regulate solidarity's vitality in an uncertain, complex, and threatening polity.

A secondary bond: Why economic threats boost compensatory preference for government control in China

Ronghua Xu, Yi Ding, Yongyu Guo, Jan‐Willem van Prooijen

Full text
Compensatory control theory suggests that threats to personal control increase the desire for external control. Yet, despite the growing salience of global economic instability—amplified by trade conflicts, protectionist policies, and heightened geopolitical tensions—empirical research has rarely examined this dynamic in the context of economic threats. Moreover, the direct psychological consequences of endorsing external control—whether it ultimately enhances or diminishes personal control—remain largely unexplored. Addressing both gaps, the present research focuses on the socio‐political context of China to investigate whether individuals facing economic threats and diminished personal control show increased preference for government control, a particularly powerful and salient external source of control in Chinese society. Across studies, the results showed a positive relationship between perceived economic threats and preference for government control, mediated by a reduction in personal control (Studies 1–3). Furthermore, we found that endorsement of government control helped enhance, rather than undermine, personal control (Studies 4 and 5). These findings suggest that government control serves as a compensatory source of control in response to economic threats.

A blind spot for dominance? Why liberals underestimate dominant leaders more than conservatives

Danbee Chon, Hemant Kakkar

Full text
The success of dominance‐oriented leaders often evokes surprise and dismay. We offer a psychological explanation beyond partisan reasoning, positing a systematic tendency among liberals to underestimate the appeal and efficacy of such leaders compared with conservatives. Drawing on moral foundations theory, we propose that liberals' heightened emphasis on harm avoidance and fairness explains their reduced liking for dominance‐oriented leaders, and that this reduced likability helps explain why they underestimate these leaders' prospects of success more than conservatives do. We test our predictions across multiple studies, including both political and organizational contexts, demonstrating that political liberals consistently perceive dominance‐oriented leaders as less likely to succeed than conservatives do—even when the leader's political affiliation is irrelevant, unknown, or aligned with their own. We conclude our investigation by suggesting that these diminished expectations from liberals might paradoxically enhance the success trajectories of dominant leaders.

Climate change and conflict: The vicious cycles undermining climate action, and what can be done to break them

Boryana Todorova, Matthew J. Hornsey, Winnifred R. Louis, Kelly S. Fielding

Full text
In this review, we synthesize evidence to argue that climate change and conflict are mutually reinforcing. We first examine conflict as a barrier to mitigation, focusing on four faultlines that slow climate action: (a) ideological polarization, (b) “elite versus ordinary people” narratives, (c) intranational, place‐based conflicts, and (d) international disputes. The consequence of these conflicts is worsening climate change, which in turn increases the prevalence of factors known to be conflict amplifiers: heat stress, economic stress, widening inequality, and forced displacement. Our analysis points to the potential for recursive “climate–conflict loops”, fuelled in part by erosion of institutional trust, shrinking moral circles, political polarization, and de‐prioritization of long‐term climate goals. In a spirit of optimism, however, we identify three buffers capable of breaking these vicious cycles: maintenance of procedural and distributive justice, enhancing information integrity, and scaffolding institutional cooperation. We conclude by proposing a research agenda that integrates psychological mechanisms into dynamic, multi‐level accounts of how climate impacts and political conflict coevolve.

Political psychology in an era of personalization: Misinformation research, generative AI, and the promise of tailored experiments

Yamil Ricardo Velez

Full text
Voters increasingly inhabit personalized information environments, where content, algorithms, and social cues vary across people. Yet popular approaches to studying and addressing features of information environments such as misinformation exposure often flatten this heterogeneity: Measures and interventions are developed for populations rather than individuals. Using misinformation as an illustrative case, I argue that population‐based methods for measuring and intervening on beliefs can obscure why some beliefs matter politically while others do not. I advocate for tailored approaches that adapt research designs to groups and individuals. Tailoring can increase relevance, facilitate persuasion, and create fruitful conditions for testing influential theories of opinion formation and change. I conclude by discussing the promise of a personalized political psychology that aligns measures and interventions with the idiosyncratic ways voters form and express opinions.

Rejecting authoritarianism: When threats to democracy shape values

Amy Erica Smith, Mollie J. Cohen, Mason W. Moseley, Matthew L. Layton

Full text
As politicians with authoritarian tendencies win elections globally, it becomes urgent to understand the social psychological mechanisms underlying support for such candidates. Prior research identifies authoritarianism as a likely predictor of vote choice. Contrary to views of authoritarian parenting as a stable, exogenous disposition, however, we argue that such attitudes can also evolve during campaigns, as voters take cues from elites. Analyzing AmericasBarometer data and a five‐wave panel study, this paper assesses authoritarian parenting values over Brazil's 2018 presidential campaign. Consistent with prior literature, authoritarian parenting attitudes early in the campaign predict who supported and opposed the authoritarian candidate Bolsonaro. However, these attitudes change over the campaign. This polarization is asymmetric, limited to Bolsonaro's opponents, who become increasingly anti‐authoritarian. The results illuminate how polities can resist authoritarian leaders and call for a reconsideration of authoritarianism in the public.

European Political Science Review

The effect of mass–elite congruence in representational style on citizens’ democratic satisfaction

Philippe Mongrain, Nino Junius

Full text
Satisfaction with democracy and confidence in political institutions are often linked to policy or ideological alignment between voters and elected officials. While policy congruence has been widely studied, less attention has been paid to process congruence—the gap between citizens’ preferences for representation and politicians’ perceptions of their duties. This paper uses data from three Portuguese election cycles (2005, 2011, and 2015) to examine how process congruence affects satisfaction with democracy. It also assesses whether political interest and educational attainment moderate this relationship, shaping the extent to which citizens respond to congruence between their own representational preferences and those of political elites. The results indicate that policy congruence is by far the strongest correlate of satisfaction with democracy. By comparison, the independent association of process congruence is small and not statistically robust: once policy and ideological congruence are accounted for, moving across the observed range of process congruence is not reliably related to satisfaction, and the estimates are inconsistent in direction across representative targets (i.e., whether congruence is measured in relation to all MPs, government MPs, or one’s preferred-party MPs). Citizens’ underlying representational preferences are nonetheless related to their satisfaction—those who favour a delegate style tend to be less satisfied than those who favour a trustee style. Contrary to expectations, this relationship is not stronger among the politically interested or the more educated; the limited moderation we detect—by education—runs opposite to expectations. Taken together, these findings caution against treating process congruence as a determinant of democratic satisfaction comparable to policy congruence, while suggesting that how citizens think representation should work is not irrelevant to how they evaluate democracy.

Electoral reforms rejected: Partisan cues in an anti-party context

Todd Donovan, Caroline Tolbert, Olive Kuester

Full text
Proposals to change how parties functioned in elections appeared on several American states’ ballots in 2024, often bundling nonpartisan primaries (NPPs) with ranked choice voting (RCV). Given the complexity of the proposals, voters faced a difficult task and may have relied on cues from campaigns when voting. We conducted a post-election survey to measure recalled exposure to campaigns and cues and model the role of cues in voting on these proposals. We find voters made choices consistent with partisan cues they report being exposed to. Democrats recalling Democratic elites were in favor were more supportive. Republicans who recalled a co-partisan elite being opposed were more likely to be opposed. We also used a survey experiment that manipulated exposure to partisan cues to test for effects of cues on support for NPPs and found results that corroborate what we observed with our self-reported survey data. Thus, even in an environment of mass anti-party sentiment, we find rules changes affecting parties may be difficult to pass via ballot measures when there is insufficient support among dominant party elites.

Caught in the crossfire: electoral spillover effects of accommodating the radical right

Jasmin Rath, Lukas Hetzer, Jan Berz, L. Constantin Wurthmann, Sarah Wagner

Full text
Mainstream parties face mounting pressure from radical right challengers. While existing research highlights the electoral costs of accommodating the radical right, we know less about how this decision affects potential coalition partners. When mainstream parties accommodate, this generates spillover effects for potential coalition parties that may be perceived as complicit in legitimising the radical right. We test this mechanism in Germany, exploiting an Unexpected Event during Survey Design: the CDU/CSU’s announcement of tougher migration policy and signaled openness to AfD support. We examine how initial supporters of the CDU/CSU’s potential coalition partners changed their vote intentions in response. We find that the event increased switching from the SPD to The Left by approximately three percentage points, with a similarly directed but statistically insignificant effect for the Greens. Supporting Violence in a Violent Land: Condonation of Political Violence in South Africa

James L. Gibson, Amanda Gouws

Full text
Research on public support for political violence has focused on three types of support: violence in the abstract, in the context of specific, contextualized events, and behavioral support. Most research uses abstract measures to indicate support, more limited research focuses on attitudes toward actual violent events, and little research addresses behavioral support. In this analysis, we examine the interrelationships of these three types of measures, based on a 2024 survey in a South African province with a long and recent history of widespread political violence. We draw three specific conclusions from our analyses: abstract attitudes are not entirely irrelevant, but some indicators are more useful than others. Abstract attitudes have some value for predicting how people judge realistic acts of political violence, just as these attitudes are to some degree connected to actual violent behavior. Our most important overall conclusion is that much more attention must be paid to measurement issues if scholars want valid and reliable evidence of popular support for political violence.

The Role of Identity-Related Issues in Partisan Shifts Among White Americans, 2016–2020

Regina Branton, Meredith Walsh Niezgoda

Full text
During the 2020 US presidential election, identity-related issues—immigration, racial justice, and white identity—were central to public discourse. This study examines how these issues shaped shifts in partisanship among white Americans who changed their affiliation from 2016 to 2020. Using American National Election Study panel data, we analyze how attitudes toward immigration, the Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement, and white identity relate to Democratic Party, Republican Party, and Independent affiliations. We find that stronger white identity and restrictive immigration views increased the likelihood of switching to the Republican Party, whereas support for BLM increased the likelihood of identifying as a Democrat. These findings suggest identity-related issues played a key role in reshaping partisan alignments, especially in strengthening the Republican Party’s identity-based appeal.

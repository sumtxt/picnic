Political affiliation impacts climate attention and choice beyond extreme weather exposure

Annika M. Wyss, Emmanuel T. Guizar Rosales, Michael Schulte-Mecklenbeck, Julie Buchs, Ulf J. J. Hahnel, Daria Knoch

Full text
Political polarization and increasing exposure to extreme weather can shape public engagement with climate change, yet the underlying psychological mechanisms remain unclear. In this Registered Report, we examine how political affiliation, extreme weather exposure and subjective attribution influence climate-relevant information search and decision-making in a US sample ( N = 1,451). We combined a process-tracing paradigm within an incentivized decision task involving real carbon emissions with objective data on extreme weather exposure. Democrats attended more to carbon emission information and made more pro-climate choices than Republicans, with no evidence of moderation by objective extreme weather exposure. The subjective attribution of extreme weather to climate change was associated with greater attention to carbon information, more pro-climate choices and moderated the effects of exposure on both outcomes. These findings highlight the importance of political affiliation in climate-relevant decision-making and suggest that extreme weather effects depend less on exposure itself than on how these experiences are interpreted.

Wiley Interdisciplinary Reviews: Climate Change

Exploring Climate Knowledge Making, Usability, and Use

Lisa Maillard, Maria Carmen Lemos

Full text
Scholars in the social sciences have long been inspired by the question of why and how scientific knowledge is used (or not). In the case of scientific climate knowledge (SCK), two prevalent arguments to explain lack of use posit that: (1) Although SCK is inherently useful, there is a “deficit” regarding climate knowledge production and accessibility and (2) SCK lacks usability or actionability because it does not fit what users want. To address the issue of usefulness/deficit, scholars have argued that we need to (a) make more and better knowledge; (b) improve how climate scientists communicate knowledge; or (c) increase public scientific literacy. In turn, addressing lack of actionability requires scientists interacting and iterating with potential users to better understand and take into consideration user needs and constraints. More recently, a third body of literature has emerged seeking to understand SCK use by focusing on how users make decisions. Yet, while there is growing empirical literature, especially related to climate adaptation, addressing SCK usefulness and actionability, relatively less evidence is available about its use. In this review, we argue that to better explain how and why SKC is used or not, these three bodies of literature need to reconcile across their theoretical assumptions and analytical frameworks, methodological and research design approaches, and findings.

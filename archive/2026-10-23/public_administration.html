Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory

Routinized Deference: Predictive Screening and Bureaucratic Capacity in Child Protective Services

Jiaen Wu

Full text
Predictive algorithms are increasingly used to support frontline decision-making in public administration, but evidence remains limited on how they affect bureaucratic capacity. This study examines county-level adoption of predictive screening tools in U.S. Child Protective Services using NCANDS data (2010–2023) and a stacked difference-in-differences design. Leveraging staggered adoption, I find no change in the total number of investigations opened and a sizable decline (about 15%) in the share of investigations that are substantiated. These results are inconsistent with operational capacity gains and more consistent with misclassification that erodes analytical capacity. These findings are consistent with a mechanism of routinized deference: under workload and accountability pressures, algorithmic recommendations can become procedurally defensible defaults. Overall, this study argues that predictive screening is not capacity-enhancing by default, as routinized deference can undermine the professional judgment central to bureaucratic work.

Balancing risk communication and accountability concerns in risk management: A study of major public project investments

Sof Thrane, Ulrik Christiansen, Torben Juul Andersen, Giulio Zichella

Full text
Effective administration of public organizations requires timely responses to emerging risks, yet communicating those risks up the hierarchy can expose operational managers to blame in politicized accountability environments. This study examines how public managers navigated that tension in an analysis of 130 risk reports filed over a seven-year period associated with two major procurement and maintenance projects in the Danish Defence. Using an abductive qualitative approach, we develop a dynamic model of risk management with four phases: (1) risk as an operational concern, (2) turning risk into an organizational problem, (3) assigning blame and accountability, and (4) initiating mitigation and response. We find that risk and accountability communication are interdependent processes rather than independent sequential processes. Operational managers attempt to raise awareness of emerging risks while simultaneously deflecting potential blame and catering to higher-level concerns about public scrutiny. This tension generates pressure to only report when it is absolutely necessary, while higher-level managers often postpone action to avoid political exposure. These practices contribute to delays, projects not meeting specifications, and significant budget overruns. The study advances a dynamic theory of public sector risk management and explains how hierarchical communication practices influence whether, when, and how emerging risks are addressed.

Public Administration Review

Institutional Amnesia and the Loss of “The Why Rationale” in Central Government Agencies

Alastair Stark, Hridesh Gajurel, Jack Corbett, Dennis C. Grube, Heather Lovell, Rodney J. Scott

Full text
Data from 115 interviews conducted with officials in the Westminster systems of Australia, New Zealand, and the United Kingdom is used to substantiate a corrosive relationship between institutional amnesia and the ability of public servants to make sense of their organizational context. A process in which public servants question why the policies, procedures, and organizational frameworks that surround them is proposed as one medium through which this relationship operates. Four problematic effects produced by this relationship and process are subsequently analyzed: (1) public servants following but not understanding procedures, (2) inexplicable organizational aspects getting abandoned, (3) the layering of new organizational features on top of old ones, creating complexity, and (4) public service inefficiencies and associated public servant frustration. The article concludes with the implications of this important relationship for public administration scholars and practitioners.

Public Administration and the Crisis of American Democracy

Donald F. Kettl

Full text
The field of public administration stands amid genuine crisis. Americans don't trust government or its administrators. Elected officials have attacked the career service as agents of the “deep state” and, though it might be hard to admit, many of public administration's critics have a point. Government's performance too often falls short of what people expect, practitioners too often seem too unresponsive to elected officials, and researchers too often seem to have only fuzzy guidance for practitioners. However, public administration undoubtedly is more important now than ever in translating public goals into effective action, and in bringing to life the democratic aspirations Madison and his colleagues wished for the country at its founding. The field urgently needs new strategies and tactics for solving the new generation of puzzles the country faces. In coming together, practitioners and researchers build on these puzzles to chart an exciting road forward.

Regulatory Agencies Under Mutating Populism in Office: Insights From Italy (1990–2025)

Fabrizio Di Mascio, Giulio Francisci, Martino Maggetti, Alessandro Natalini

Full text
Our paper builds a bridge between the emerging strand of research on public administration under populist rule and the established literature on delegation to independent regulatory authorities (IRAs). We focus on Italy, a critical case in Western Europe, which between 2001 and 2025 experienced six coalition governments dominated or strongly influenced by populist parties of different ideological orientations. Findings highlight that delegation to IRAs is remarkably resilient once institutionalized, even under sustained political contestation. Despite repeated populist participation in government since the early 2000s, Italian IRAs have not been subject to systematic de‐delegation or statutory retrenchment. However, the preservation of formal independence has not implied the absence of means for political control. Our analysis shows that populist governments have systematically relied on political appointments. Interestingly, political appointments have not been driven by partisan loyalty or overt politicization. Instead, the intervention of populist parties has operated along a different dimension of political control that has received less attention in delegation theory: the specialization of appointees. Populist governments have displayed a preference for appointing IRAs' board members with lower levels of domain‐specific specialization, privileging generalist profiles over specialists.

Social Safety Nets and Corporate Tax Avoidance: Evidence From Paid Family Leave Programs

Yifei Chen, Dan Palmon

Full text
Social safety nets are essential to a resilient and adaptive economic system. This study examines their influence on corporate tax avoidance using the staggered implementation of U.S. state‐level paid family leave (PFL) programs, social safety net policies designed to enhance employees' work‐life balance, as a quasi‐natural experiment. We find a statistically and economically significant decline in corporate tax avoidance following PFL adoption. The results hold across various robustness tests. A structural equation model shows that the effect is mediated by decreases in employee turnover and improvements in employee productivity. The effect is stronger among firms facing greater financing frictions and among labor‐intensive firms. Overall, this study identifies a reduction in corporate tax avoidance as an unintended consequence of social safety nets.

Impact of Anti‐Money Laundering Enforcement on Suspicious Activity Reporting—Empirical Evidence From Real Estate Agencies in Norway

Anne Marthe Bjønness

Full text
This paper examines the impact of anti‐money laundering (AML) oversight on suspicious activity reports (SARs) filed by real estate agencies in Norway. Using unique data on inspections, sanctions, and confidential SARs, the study estimates the effects of inspections on reporting through a difference‐in‐differences approach. The results show an increase in reporting post‐inspection driven by entities with a low, positive number of prior filings, while report quality remains unchanged. There is no evidence of spillover effects; inspections do not influence the reporting of uninspected entities either geographically close to or affiliated with inspected ones. The findings suggest that AML oversight may create a learning effect for inspected entities but does not lead to sector‐wide improvements beyond local effects. This paper contributes to the modest empirical literature on AML oversight effects and the general literature on regulatory enforcement and provides recommendations for improvements to the AML system.

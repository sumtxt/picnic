Quarterly Journal of Economics

How New Products Enter Markets: Social Networks, Consumer Imports, and Retailer Learning

David Argente, Esteban Méndez, Diana Van Patten

Full text
This paper studies how new varieties enter markets and become locally available. We provide causal evidence of demand externalities that operate in two steps. First, information about new varieties diffuses directly through real-world social ties among consumers. Second, early purchases generate an indirect spillover to firms: local retailers learn from “pioneer” consumers which new varieties are most likely to succeed and adjust their product offerings accordingly. We study this process in the context of direct-to-consumer imports. Using customs records on individuals’ purchases matched to population-wide social networks, international migrant links, and retailer catchment areas, we document economically meaningful demand externalities. Product-specific expenditure shocks abroad transmit through migrant networks and shift which varieties consumers purchase. Leveraging these shocks as a plausibly exogenous source of local demand variation, we show strong peer effects: prior purchases by close neighbors, coworkers, or friends increase an individual’s likelihood of purchasing the same variety, especially for premium and visible goods. We leverage this result to identify an indirect spillover from consumers to firms: retailers are more likely to add a variety to their assortment when it becomes popular among consumers in their catchment area. Combining the instrument with linked consumer–retailer data and a self-conducted retailer survey, we show that this response reflects learning about latent demand for varieties not yet stocked locally. Together, social diffusion and retailer learning generate demand multipliers that reshape local product availability and expand access to global variety.

Optimal Flood Insurance in a Second-Best World: Fiscal Spillovers, Reclassification Risk, and Moral Hazard

Jonathan Gruber, Adam Solomon

Full text
Intensifying climate change makes protection against natural disaster risk – either through ex ante insurance or ex post aid – a first-order public policy issue. Flood risk protection in the U.S. traditionally featured ex post aid through FEMA and heavily subsidized insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). In an effort to correct subsidy-induced overbuilding and under-mitigation in flood-prone areas, the NFIP recently moved to actuarially fair insurance premiums. But this change has two unintended consequences: a fiscal spillover onto FEMA disaster aid as insurance coverage declines in flood-prone areas, and household exposure to uninsurable reclassification risk from uncertain climate projections. We develop a model of optimal flood insurance that incorporates the static and dynamic tradeoffs of subsidizing premiums. We then use a variety of identification strategies and data to estimate the five key parameters needed to implement this model. We find that the fiscal spillover and reclassification risk protection benefits significantly outweigh the moral hazard costs, and that the optimal subsidy to flood insurance is 51%, comparable to the level of subsidization before the recent reform.

Review of Economic Studies

When Should We (Not) Interpret Linear IV Estimands as LATE?

Tymon Słoczyńki

Full text
In this paper I revisit the interpretation of the linear instrumental variables (IV) estimand as a weighted average of conditional local average treatment effects (LATEs). I focus on a situation in which additional covariates are required for identification while the reduced-form and first-stage regressions may be misspecified due to an implicit homogeneity restriction on the effects of the instrument. I show that the weights on some conditional LATEs are negative and the IV estimand is no longer interpretable as a causal effect under a weaker version of monotonicity, i.e. when there are compliers but no defiers at some covariate values and defiers but no compliers elsewhere. The problem of negative weights disappears in the interacted specification of Angrist and Imbens (I1995), which avoids misspecification and seems to be underused in applied work. I illustrate my findings in an application to the causal effects of pretrial detention on case outcomes. In this setting, I reject the stronger version of monotonicity, demonstrate that the interacted instruments are sufficiently strong for consistent estimation using the jackknife methodology, and present several estimates that are economically and statistically different, depending on whether the interacted instruments are used.

Robust Aggregation of Correlated Information

Henrique de Oliveira, Yuhta Ishii, Xiao Lin

Full text
Decisions are typically made by drawing on multiple sources of information, yet the correlation among these sources is often hard to assess. We study robustly optimal strategies—those that maximize the worst-case payoff guarantee across all correlation structures. With two states and two actions, the robustly optimal strategy is strikingly simple: use only the best information source and ignore the rest. With more actions, a robustly optimal strategy decomposes the problem into local comparisons and assigns each comparison to the source that is best at that comparison. With more states, the same specialization logic survives through a concavification characterization: the decision problem is decomposed into payoff-relevant components, and each component is assigned to its best source. These results offer a new rationale for why ignoring information can be optimal and highlight the benefit of using specialized information sources when correlations are ambiguous.

Review of Economics and Statistics

Parental Response to Fetal Shocks and Macroeconomic Volatility

Rakesh Banerjee, Farhan Majid

Full text
We examine parental response to a fetal shock using data from 150 Demographic and Health Surveys in 50 developing countries. Focusing on the impact of Ramadan fasting, a common shock experienced by more than a billion Muslims worldwide, we find that children exposed to Ramadan fasting during pregnancy have lower rates of full vaccination for DPT and polio compared to their non-exposed siblings. Further, parents tend to compensate for the fetal shock during booms and reinforce during economic downturns. Our estimations suggest that this has resulted in approximately half a million excess infant deaths over the past three decades.

The Female Sensitivity Hypothesis: Evidence From Experimental Economics

Felipe A. Araujo, Neeraja Gupta, Lise Vesterlund

Full text
We assess the empirical evidence for the hypothesis that women more than men respond to changes in treatment. First, we examine whether the results of over two hundred experimental economics papers support the female sensitivity hypothesis. Second, using data from two studies (DellaVigna and Pope, 2022; Exley et al., 2025), we conduct over two hundred pairwise tests of the hypothesis. Both analyses show that gender is not predictive of responsiveness to treatment. We further examine how the hypothesis has been disseminated in the literature and find that it is referenced predominantly by papers that support it.

The Equilibrium Effect of Information in Consumer Credit Markets: Public Records and Credit Redistribution

Scott L. Fulford, Éva Nagypál

Full text
Information is central to consumer credit, but little is known about its equilibrium effects. We study a 2017 natural experiment in which non-bankruptcy public records were purged from U.S. credit reports. The large information-removal differences across states allow us to identify equilibrium impacts. Available credit increased for consumers who lost negative information directly. Consistent with our model, available credit declined indirectly for consumers who did not lose the negative information because they could no longer distinguish themselves. The direct and indirect effects nearly canceled, so the net equilibrium effect after a year was nearly zero but with substantial redistribution.

Hostility, Population Sorting, and Backwardness: Quasi-Experimental Evidence from the Red Army after WWII

Christian Ochsner

Full text
How can short episodes of localized hostility shape regional economic trajectories that even amplify over time? I exploit economic differences across the idiosyncratic and short-lived line of contact between the Red Army and the Western Allies in South Austria at the end of WWII. The 74-day hostile presence of the Red Army shaped the age- and occupation-structure via refugee flows, causing amplifying demographic effects over time and backwardness in sector development and tax revenues. My findings also point to the isolated role of the Red Army’s hostile actions after WWII in understanding the European economic East-West divide.

Structuring Cash Transfers: Cash Flow Preferences, Seasonality, and Financial Decisions in Rural Kenya

Carolina Kansikas, Anandi Mani, Paul Niehaus

Full text
We examine the preferences of low-income households in Kenya over the structure of unconditional cash transfers. Most preferred lumpy transfers, and some preferred deferred receipt—in contrast to the typical structures of safety-net programs, but consistent with evidence on the financial challenges of poverty. Turning to consequences, receiving transfers later in the year raised income 1.5 years later—but willingness to defer receipt was sensitive to small changes in cash flow around the time of decision-making. Taken together, these results illustrate how adapting cash transfer design to the decision-making environment of those in poverty could improve financial choices and outcomes.

Blended Finance and Female Entrepreneurship

Halil İbrahim Aydın, Çağatay Bircan, Ralph De Haas

Full text
We study the real and allocative consequences of relaxing gender-specific credit constraints by analyzing a blended finance program designed to expand bank lending to female entrepreneurs in Turkey. Merging credit registry, tax, and matched employer-employee data, we find participating banks raised their credit share to women by over 18%, spanning repeat, poached, and first-time borrowers. Beneficiary firms invest more, grow employment, sales, and profits, diversify their networks, and exit less. Treated banks also expand lending to male entrepreneurs, though the increase is smaller and disproportionately directed toward higher-productivity firms, suggesting reallocation rather than crowding out. District-level effects are absent, reecting the program’s modest scale.

How Credible Are the Findings from Online Experiments?

Abel Brodeur, Nikolai Cook, Anthony Heyes, Lamis Kattan

Full text
Online experiments have surged in popularity, but how credible are their published results? We examine the universe of hypotheses tested on Amazon Mechanical Turk and reported in leading journals between 2010 and 2021. We find evidence of widespread p-hacking, publication bias, and under-powered studies. The extent of the problems varies substantially across fields, with marketing particularly badly afflicted and little evidence of distortion in economics. We explore correlates of increased credibility and compare the online results to laboratory results from the same outlets, finding similar distortions in both, with bunching just above the significance threshold more pronounced on MTurk.

Tax Incentives and Return Migration

Jacopo Bassetto, Giuseppe Ippedico

Full text
We study how tax incentives affect the return migration of high-skilled expatriates to their home country, exploiting a generous income tax break for returnees in Italy. Using administrative data and a Triple-Difference design, we estimate a migration elasticity to the average net-of-tax rate just below one. Responses are sizable across the upper half of the earnings distribution, indicating that tax-induced migration is not limited to top earners. A cost-benefit analysis reveals that, while costly in the short term, the scheme pays for itself in present value if a sufficiently large fraction of returnees remains after the scheme elapses.

Contracting Structures in Public Procurement: Evidence from Donor-Funded Electrification in Kenya

Catherine D. Wolfram, Edward Miguel, Eric Hsu, Susanna B. Berkouwer

Full text
We study how contracting and monitoring structures in public procurement affect large-scale infrastructure construction. Starting in 2016, Kenya’s public electric utility ran competitive auctions and then awarded and administered dozens of procurement contracts to connect millions to electricity. The causal analyses leverage natural and experimental variation and granular, independentlycollected construction quality data. Kenya Power contracts funded by the World Bank featured unbundled contract components known as ‘design-bid-build’ and enhanced monitoring compared to African Development Bank-funded contracts. Unbundling contracts delayed construction progress but improved quality. Randomized inspections improved quality without large delays. Bundled contracting with additional independent monitoring could improve outcomes.

Is American Soft Power a Casualty of the Trade War? Evidence from U.S. Movie Viewership in China

Haichao Fan, Yichuan Hu, Lixin Tang, Shang-Jin Wei

Full text
The US trade war against China can either enhance or diminish American soft power in China, depending on whether Chinese citizens view it as legitimate. We study how US movie viewership, a measure of American soft power, responds to the trade war. We find a significantly greater reduction in US movie viewership in regions more exposed to the Trump tariffs, but no corresponding decline in non-US movies. The decline is stronger for action and drama movies, which tend to embody greater perceived “Americanness.” Survey data also show a sharper decline in trust in Americans in more tariff-exposed regions.

How Competition Shapes Peer Effects: Evidence from a University in China

Siyu Chen, Zihan Hu

Full text
Competition is widely used to enhance effort and performance. However, in many domains, like education, competition could backfire, as performance is not solely reliant on individual efforts but also on collaboration endeavors among peers. Utilizing university administrative data, we examine how competition changes peer effects and peer interactions. Exploiting randomly assigned roommates, we first demonstrate that high-ability roommates have detrimental effects on the academic performance of high-ability students. More importantly, such negative peer effects significantly increase along various dimensions of competition intensity within dorm rooms. Follow-up survey findings reveal that competition hinders mutual assistance and fosters unfriendly behaviors among roommates.

Estimating Substitution Patterns and Demand Curvature in Discrete-Choice Models of Product Differentiation

Cameron Birchall, Debashrita Mohapatra, Frank Verboven

Full text
We extend BLP’s aggregate discrete-choice model of product differentiation to create more flexibility in the price functional form. We apply a Box-Cox specification, which relaxes the typical unit demand assumption and creates flexibility on demand curvature. The model provides a unifying framework for mixed logit and mixed CES models, while remaining computationally tractable. We provide an illustrative application to the ready-to-eat cereals market. This shows that the cross-sectional relation between price elasticities and average prices per product is more in line with descriptive elasticity patterns, and that substitution between product pairs may be affected to some extent.

Democracy Doesn’t Always Happen Over Night: Regime Change in Stages and Economic Growth

Vanessa Boese-Schlosser, Markus Eberhardt

Full text
How substantial are the economic benefits from democratic regime change? We argue that democratization is often not a discrete event but a two-stage process: autocracies enter into “episodes” of political liberalization, which eventually culminate in regime change or not. To account for this chronology and the implicit counterfactual groups, we introduce a repeated-treatment difference-in-difference implementation capturing nonparallel trends and selection into treatment. We find that modeling regime change in two stages rather than a single event yields stronger long-run growth effects. Among democratizers, experiencing repeated episodes without regime change reduces growth in democracy, whereas length of episode does not.

National Wage Contracts, Monopsony, and Welfare in a North-South Economy,

Emanuele Ciani, Alberto Dalmazzo, Guido de Blasio

Full text
In countries characterized by significant geographical disparities in productivity, nationwide labour contracts may cause local unemployment by preventing the adjustments that would occur in a competitive labour market. Here we argue, however, that when there are mobility frictions and monopsony power, nationwide contracts may still be preferable to local contracts. Using a spatial general equilibrium model calibrated on the Italian North-South divide, we suggest that nationwide wage contracts may still lead to narrower regional welfare gaps if monopsony power is sufficiently strong in low-productivity areas.

Econometrics Journal

Estimation and Inference for Cointegrated Systems of Multi-Factor Production Functions: Modelling the Joint Behavior of GDP and Emissions

Sebastian Veldhuis, Martin Wagner

Full text
This paper develops integrated modified ordinary and generalized least squares estimation for systems of cointegrating multivariate polynomial regressions, i. e., systems of equations that include deterministic variables, integrated processes and (cross-)products of non-negative integer powers of these variables as regressors. The stationary errors are allowed to be correlated across equations, over time and with the regressors. The necessity to consider integrated modified generalized least squares estimation arises in the case of estimation under restrictions, which in general implies that ordinary and generalized least squares estimators cease to be identical. We discuss in detail hypothesis testing for the unrestricted and restricted estimators. Furthermore, we develop asymptotically pivotal fixed-b inference, shown to be available only in special cases. A brief illustration to modelling output and emissions as a Cobb-Douglas-Translog cointegrated production system with TFP, capital and labor as inputs indicates the potential usefulness of the developed methods for climate econometrics.

Economic Journal

The Immigration Surplus Revisited

Michael Amior, Alan Manning

Full text
We extend the immigration surplus results of Borjas (1995). In a closed competitive constant-returns economy, we show that an influx of migrants (holding their skill mix fixed) must increase the total real income of natives, for any number of factor inputs, any pattern of substitutability or complementarity between them, and any number of intermediate or final goods – with larger gains if natives and migrants differ more in skill mix. If natives supply labour elastically across skill types, their welfare gains are diminished, even if they respond by moving to higher-paying jobs. We illustrate our results using a US calibration.

Networks of Dissent: Social Leaders and Protest in an Autocracy

Johannes Buggle, Max Deter, Martin Lange

Full text
This paper examines how network ties between local social leaders influenced the diffusion of mass protests in an autocracy. We focus on the Protestant Church and the Peaceful Revolution in East Germany. To quantify the role of leader networks in protest diffusion, we compile biographical records of over 1,600 Protestant pastors and construct network ties based on shared employment histories. Our findings reveal that network connections led to an increase in protest diffusion by 7.1 percentage points in a given week. Evidence from quasi-random connections, placebo networks, and an IV strategy supports a causal interpretation of these results.

Cognitive ability, risk preferences and errors

Michalis Drouvelis, Graeme Pearce

Full text
In this paper we examine the link between cognitive ability, risk preferences and the errors that people make. We build a unique dataset that contains almost 260,000 decisions made under risk in a variety of tasks by approximately 13,000 individuals, with each person having completed a test designed explicitly to measure their cognitive ability. Our analysis suggests that intelligence has (1) no impact on risk preferences and (2) a robust link to errors. Our results have implications for the development of theories that seek to explain the impact of cognitive ability on decision making.

Professional Networks and the Labour Market Assimilation of Immigrants

Olof Åslund, Mattias Engdahl, Sébastien Willis

Full text
We examine how professional networks shape immigrants’ job search and labour market assimilation. Analysing displaced workers in Sweden, we show that connections to firms through conationals are especially important for immigrants’ job finding. As networks expand to include more natives, the value of such connections rises towards that seen for displaced natives. Origin homophily also predicts whether a connection provides a referral—increasing earnings and lowering turnover—rather than vacancy information. Networks account for 20 per cent of the growth in immigrants’ re-employment over their first decade after arrival; immigrants’ networks are as productive as natives’ after fifteen years.

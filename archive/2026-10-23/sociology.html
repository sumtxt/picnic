Population and Development Review

Two Decades of Change in Mexico–U.S. Migration, 2005–2025

Andrés Villarreal

Full text
Mexican migration to the United States has undergone dramatic changes since reaching its peak two decades ago when it was considered the largest sustained population flow in the world. Previous research has documented a sharp decline in Mexico–U.S. migration in the late 2000s, but less is known about later trends. Studies also suggest that return migration to Mexico may have risen following an increase in removals from the United States. This study provides an update on the bidirectional flow of migrants between the two countries disaggregated by demographic subgroups using data from a quarterly employment survey in Mexico. Following the dramatic decline in the late 2000s, Mexican migration to the United States remained low for over a decade with only a modest increase in recent years. The rate of return migration to Mexico also declined sharply until the mid‐2010s and has remained low. Because return migration declined for longer than emigration, net emigration from Mexico has increased since 2010. The observed changes in Mexican migration in recent years are inconsistent with explanations that attribute the earlier decline to lower demand for immigrant labor in the United States and to the direct compositional effects of population aging in Mexico.

To Work or Study? The Development of Adolescent Migration Expectations

Jennifer E. Glick, Melissa Alcaraz, Erick Axxe, Dirgha Ghimire

Full text
Migration features regularly in young adult life course transitions in many low‐ and middle‐income countries. The aspirations–capabilities framework points to the importance of life goals and enabling resources for forming expectations of future moves. For some young people, moving away represents an opportunity to reach their aspirations. Others hold aspirations that are more easily fulfilled in the local setting. To understand how migration is connected to prior experiences and aspirations in adolescence, we focus on the formation of migration expectations, disaggregating expectations to include labor migration, education migration, and the expectation to avoid migration altogether. Relying on longitudinal data from adolescents in three diverse settings, Jalisco, Mexico ( n = 1593), Gaza Province, Mozambique ( n = 1350), and Chitwan, Nepal ( n = 1884), the analyses consider the mechanisms through which contemporary young cohorts form expectations for these different migration pathways. The results show that aspirations motivate distinct migration pathways with strikingly consistent patterns across settings. Educational aspirations are associated with subsequent expectations to migrate for further education, portending diversification of migration flows from low‐ and middle‐income settings where labor migration has predominated but access to formal education is expanding and youth engagement in education is growing.

U.S. State Policy Contexts, County Economic Contexts, and Working-Age Mortality

Iliya Gutin, Jennifer Karas Montez, Shannon Monnat, Emily Wiemers, Douglas Wolf

Full text
Highlights Jointly modeling state and county contexts reveals synergistic, not independent, mortality associations. Lowest mortality is observed in economically secure counties within liberal-policy states. For males, extreme county economic insecurity yields high mortality regardless of state policy. For males, high labor force participation raises mortality in conservative state contexts. For females, liberal state policies buffer both county economic and labor disadvantages.

Structuralizing the stress process: states as contextual determinants of stress and mental health

Rachel Donnelly, Zhe Zhang, Mateo P Farina, Blake W Powell

Full text
Despite growing evidence that policy environments influence mental health, we have limited knowledge of how policy environments influence the stress processes that shape mental health. This study develops and tests a theoretical framework that structuralizes the stress process model by explicating how the state policy context (1) directly influences mental health, (2) indirectly influences mental health via stress exposure, and (3) mitigates the impact of stressors on mental health. This study uses nationally representative data from the Household Pulse Survey (2020–2023; n = 3,043,961) and the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS; 2015–2019; n = 1,989,932) merged with state policy liberalism data to consider how state policy liberalism—the ideological orientation of state policies—shapes mental health. Results show that more liberal state policies were associated with lower odds of probable depression, probable anxiety, and frequent mental distress and weakened associations between three focal economic stressors—income loss, financial hardship, employment status—and mental health outcomes. Moreover, the association between state policy liberalism and mental health was partially mediated by financial hardship. Findings differed slightly by age: mediation via financial hardship was more evident among older adults (65+), whereas the buffering of economic-related stressors was mostly among adults under sixty-five. Overall, this study demonstrates the importance of a structuralized stress process model for mental health research.

Leaving together: social influence and church decline

Elis Carlberg Larsson, Peter Hedström

Full text
Church membership has declined across much of the Western world in recent decades. Existing research has identified individual characteristics that affect individuals’ likelihood of leaving their churches, but far less is known about how social interactions influence church decline. We examine (1) how exposure to others leaving the Church of Sweden affects individuals’ likelihood of leaving and (2) the aggregate membership dynamics generated by these micro-level processes. Using full-population register data from 2002 to 2023 and a dynamic matched-sample design, we estimate social-influence effects and assess how susceptibility varies with religious embeddedness, defined as the extent to which individuals are socially connected to other church members. We use these estimates to calibrate an agent-based simulation model linking individual behavior to aggregate membership decline. The results show that exposure to leavers increases individuals’ likelihood of leaving the Church, particularly among those with weak religious embeddedness. The simulations indicate that social influence contributed appreciably to the numerical decline of the Church of Sweden, while religious embeddedness moderated this effect. Social influence can thus amplify ongoing religious decline, while religious embeddedness attenuates this amplification.

Nonstandard work, nonstandard lives? Temporary employment, unemployment, and family transitions

Hande Inanc

Full text
It is well documented that unemployment is closely associated with family outcomes, affecting the occurrence, timing, and sequencing of key family transitions. Individuals with unemployment experience are more likely to postpone marriage, enter cohabiting unions, transition into parenthood outside marriage, and experience partnership dissolution. Temporary employment is often regarded as a better alternative because it provides income, opportunities for skill accumulation, and continued attachment to the labor market. In reality, however, temporary jobs are characterized by substantial insecurity and strain, which may have important consequences for family lives. Applying discrete-time event history analysis to nationally representative longitudinal data from the British household panel study, I show that temporary employment constitutes a meaningful source of uncertainty with substantial consequences for family formation and stability. The consequences of employment insecurity vary by educational attainment, gender, and union type. Educational differences strongly condition men’s family responses to employment insecurity, while for women education primarily structures exposure rather than buffering its consequences. Cohabitation functions as an adaptive response to unemployment but not to temporary employment, which is instead associated with greater instability of cohabiting unions. Together, these findings highlight the importance not only of employment, but of stable employment, for family formation and stability.

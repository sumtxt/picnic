Framing Democracy: How Elites' Discourse Shapes Citizens' Understandings of Democracy and Enables Backsliding

Selim Erdem Aytaç

Full text
abstract: Citizens' divergent understandings of democracy have important implications for democratic resilience, yet the sources of this heterogeneity remain contested. The author argues that partisan elite discourse is a key driver of these divergent understandings: political leaders' framing of democracy systematically shapes their supporters' democratic priorities. Observational and experimental evidence from Turkey supports this argument. The author establishes that Turkish party leaders differ in how they conceptualize democracy in public discourse, and these differences map onto their supporters' understandings of democracy. A population-based survey experiment shows that when leaders emphasize particular dimensions of democracy, copartisans also assign greater importance to those same dimensions of democratic governance. These findings highlight how partisan elite framing can influence mass conceptions of democracy in ways that enable democratic backsliding: would-be autocrats can exploit this dynamic to subvert liberal norms without significant backlash, even where overall support for democracy remains high.

The Paradox of Imperial Taxation: How Diversity Shaped Fiscal Development in the Ottoman Empire

Yusuf Magiya

Full text
abstract: This article investigates the relationship between diversity and state building by examining how diversity affects the costs of investing in fiscal capacity. The author argues that diversity—ethnic or religious—makes populations less legible to the state, thereby increasing those costs. Higher costs deter the state from investing in fiscal capacity in more diverse areas, which hinders fiscal capacity building in those regions. Instead, the state diverts investments to less diverse areas, which are typically populated by the core/dominant group. As a result, the dominant group bears a higher tax burden compared to minority groups, which do not experience comparable tax increases. The author tests these arguments using original data on local-level fiscal revenues in the late Ottoman Empire along with qualitative and quantitative evidence from archival sources and contemporary cross-national and subnational data.

Political Socialization In Authoritarian Regimes: Sites and Everyday Experiences for Young People in Zimbabwe

Marjoke Oosterom, Simbarashe Gukurume, Tinotenda Chishiri

Full text
abstract: Scholarship on political socialization has largely overlooked how forms of violence and repression experienced by young people living in authoritarian regimes affect their political development. Bringing together the literatures on political socialization and violence, this study aims to advance debates on youth political socialization in authoritarian regimes. How and where do young people in such contexts develop political orientations and behaviors, and through which mechanisms? To address these questions, this study draws on qualitative research with urban youths in Zimbabwe between 2019 and 2022 to demonstrate the significance of everyday encounters with authoritarianism in contested urban spaces. The authors advance existing scholarship by conceptualizing and analyzing diffuse socialization experiences and sites of socialization, showing how ruling party agents operating at urban markets use diverse socialization practices to cultivate loyalty to the ruling party among young traders. The authors argue that political socialization in this context does not foster genuine loyalty but provides tacit knowledge about authoritarian politics and how to navigate it through performative compliance.

Renewing Democracy: How Exposure to Electoral Turnovers Reinforces Citizens' Democratic Support

Damien Bol

Full text
abstract: A prevailing narrative argues that citizens who live under democratic rule take it for granted, potentially leading to democratic backsliding. This article advances a more optimistic perspective, arguing that electoral turnovers—that is, elections that displace the head of government—reinforce democratic support. To test this argument, the author merges multiple international surveys that cover more than 350,000 respondents in seventy-nine countries, matches those surveys with a century's worth of electoral data, and identifies the national electoral turnovers to which each respondent was exposed throughout their life. Cohort analysis shows that exposure to turnovers, especially those that occur after prolonged periods of incumbency, has a positive and lasting effect on democratic support. Further analysis reveals that the effect is positive in a wide range of contexts, but is strongest among citizens who have always lived in a democracy. This finding thus suggests that frequent electoral turnovers are important to renew citizens' support for democracy.

When Does Civil Society Prevent Violence? Civic Networks, Demography, and Communal Conflict in Côte d'Ivoire

Philip A. Martin

Full text
abstract: This article explores whether local civic networks can reduce the risk of violence in communally divided societies. The author argues that dense associational networks can foster intercommunal peace near elections, but only when those networks are situated in communities diverse enough that civic networks foster intergroup contact and monitoring by local elites. Leveraging new village-level data and interviews from Côte d'Ivoire, the author examines the relationship between the country's civic associations and the political violence that occurred during national elections in 2020 and 2021. Contrary to the arguments of civil society skeptics, he shows that robust networks of voluntary associations had significant spillover benefits for peace, particularly in communally diverse areas. However, in areas with a dominant communal group, civic networks had little impact on the risk of violence. The findings inform debates about the constraints on intercommunal conflict and the significance of associationalism in divided societies.

Journal of Peace Research

Strike when they aren’t looking: great power crises and threats to rule

David B Carter, İpek Ece Şener

Full text
Although regime threats such as civil conflict and coup d’état are widely studied, their links to global crises receive little attention. We argue that episodes of instability among great powers ripple through the international system, encouraging violent threats to rule in less powerful states. We assess our arguments with both new and existing indicators of systemic instability and show that periods of economic and security instability among great powers are associated with a heightened risk of both civil conflict onset and regime change coup attempts in smaller states. We further demonstrate important heterogeneity in the risk faced by smaller states by showing that security instability which inflicts a given regime's great power allies drives this statistical association. Our work offers a novel explanation for the emergence of threats to rule, integrating the literatures on great power politics, civil conflict, and coup d’état, while also demonstrating why regime threats not only cluster geographically but also cluster in time. This study has important implications for contemporary international politics and for future conflict research given the widely observed increase in great power competition and systemic instability over the past decade or so.

The demobilizing effect of violent actions and radical-right involvement on public support for protest

Daniel Saldivia Gonzatti, Eylem Kanol, Sophia Hunger, Swen Hutter

Full text
How does radicality affect public support for protest? Despite a long-standing scholarly debate about the demobilizing power of radicality, empirical evidence beyond the use of violent action and left-libertarian social movements remains scarce. Therefore, this article advances the debate by examining the effects of two forms of radicality, violent forms of protest and radical protest actors, in the context of a right-authoritarian movement. We employ a novel and comprehensive multistep research design to study these theoretical expectations, integrating original survey and natural experimental evidence. Empirically, we draw on the case of the anti-containment protests in Germany during the COVID-19 pandemic, emblematic of the rise of right-authoritarian social movements in contemporary democracies. We examine the role of demonstrator-instigated violence and radical-right involvement in demobilizing sympathizers and likely participants. The empirical findings show that, in contrast to left-libertarian movements, individuals strongly committed to the cause are likely to continue to take to the streets regardless of the presence of violence and radical-right actors. The findings suggest adverse diminishing demobilization effects of radicality in contemporary democracies, which are less pronounced for individuals close to right-authoritarian movements.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Introducing the Peace Observatory: Peace Negotiations and Mediation in Armed Conflicts

Allard Duursma, Janek Bruker, Carina Diener, Samantha Gamez, Valentin Felix Geier, Arianna Guidolin

Full text
This article introduces the Peace Observatory, a new global dataset that captures over 10,000 rounds of peace negotiations in interstate, intrastate, and non-state conflicts between 1989 and 2023 with regular updates planned for additional years. The Peace Observatory disaggregates peacemaking into distinct negotiation rounds. To demonstrate the value of the dataset, the article examines how the involvement of autocratic states as mediators is associated with negotiation outcomes across different phases of the peace process. Unlike many existing datasets that operate at the conflict-year level and do not systematically distinguish between stages of peacemaking, the Peace Observatory records the specific phase of each negotiation round – prenegotiation, negotiation, or implementation. This enables analysis of whether mediator characteristics matter differently at distinct stages of the process. The analysis shows that autocratic involvement is positively associated with the conclusion of peace agreements during the negotiation phase, but not in the prenegotiation or implementation phase.

Stringent policies were associated with greater Covid-19 vaccine uptake including among those who did not trust the vaccine

Manu M. Savani, Richard Koenig, Brendan Nyhan, Sanchayan Banerjee, Andrew Hunter, Peter John, Blake Lee-Whiting, Peter John Loewen, John McAndrews

Full text
Are stringent vaccine promotion policies associated with higher vaccine uptake? We argue policy stringency, which changes the costs and benefits of immunisation choices, can affect individual vaccination decisions. In a survey of G-7 countries, 22% of respondents ( n = 42,417) said they did not trust the Covid-19 vaccine, but half of them reported having at least one vaccination. We investigate factors associated with take-up. Our data indicate a high-stringency policy environment (with vaccine mandates and travel restrictions) is associated with increased uptake of the initial vaccination protocol (7 percentage points) compared to a low-stringency policy environment. Among those who did not trust the Covid-19 vaccine, a high-stringency policy environment is associated with increased uptake of the initial vaccine (16 percentage points) and the booster vaccine (20 percentage points). Our findings suggest high-stringency policies can promote vaccine uptake but raise questions about how to strike the right balance between persuasive and coercive policies.

International Theory

Decolonial IR, Indo identity, and epistemic marginalisation in International Relations

Quintijn B. Kat

Full text
Recently, a major concern of International Relations (IR) has been the need to ‘decolonise’ the discipline as a remedy for ‘Eurocentrism.’ This article argues that Decolonial IR is at risk of developing and perpetuating notable blind spots and making mistakes similar to those that it rightly berates Eurocentric IR for. First, while pointing at problematic binaries that undergird Eurocentric IR, Decolonial IR fails to escape the use of identity-based binaries of its own when justifying its project. Second, the use of these binaries allows Decolonial IR to decouple its task from the actual historical events of decolonisation, allowing for the marginalisation of communities that are not easily categorised. This decoupling enables exclusion and exposes Decolonial IR to the criticisms that it levels at Eurocentric IR. The article supports this argument through a discussion of Decolonial IR’s rationale, objectives, and strategies that are inflected with binary thinking. It then presents the Indo community as an example of marginalisation that follows from this thinking, highlighting the many contributions that attention to communities like the Indos can bring to the discipline. Finally, the article suggests a four-step approach through which Decolonial IR can improve and avoid the pitfalls that the article observes.

There is no outside: on the impossibility of non-Eurocentrism

Ilan Kapoor

Full text
This article argues that there is no fully non-Eurocentric standpoint available after colonialism. The Western symbolic order – institutional, epistemic, juridical, and political-economic – has become globally hegemonic, structuring not only liberal international institutions but also the grammar of their critique. Postcolonial and decolonial scholarship has powerfully exposed the violence of Eurocentrism, yet the desire for an uncontaminated ‘outside’ risks reproducing the very logic it opposes. Drawing on Lacanian psychoanalysis, I argue that the fantasy of epistemic purity functions as an inverted mirror of colonial universality. The problem is not merely ideological error but also libidinal investment in wholeness, authenticity, and moral innocence. Engaging debates in International Relations theory on hegemony, sovereignty, and global order, the article contends that Eurocentrism persists not only discursively but through the political economy of global capitalism and international law. Decolonization, therefore, cannot mean epistemic exit; it must instead involve traversing ambivalence, contradiction, and complicity from within a shared symbolic terrain.

International Studies Quarterly

Social Capital, Platform Power, and the Private Authority of Big Tech Firms: Theorizing The Micro-Foundations of Platform Power Among Social Networking Sites

Mitushi Mukherjee

Full text
In the era of digital globalization, Big Tech firms have emerged as pivotal non-state actors in International Relations (IR). A growing body of research theorizes the political influence of these firms through the concept of platform power, which emphasizes the unique combination of economic scale and intimate relationships with their users. Despite the analytical power of the concept, past work on the platform power of Big Tech firms obscures important differences among these firms. To improve our understanding of the foundations of Big Tech’s platform power, I focus specifically on Social Networking Sites (SNSs). SNSs are platforms that provide web-based services to enable individuals to create profiles, connect with others, and share information, opinions, and media. I argue that the micro-foundation of SNSs’ platform power is their exceptional capacity to foster social capital accumulation among their users. In turn, this cultivates loyal consumer bases and ultimately enables SNSs to wield significant private authority in global politics. I demonstrate the implications of this argument for how SNSs gain, maintain, and potentially lose private authority. This conceptual framework enhances our comprehension of Big Tech’s authority and helps further investigation into the normative implications, identity dynamics, and security concerns within this evolving landscape.

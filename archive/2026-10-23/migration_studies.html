Democratic Drain: The Effect of Democratic Backsliding on Migration Intentions

Justin Gest, Lucas Núñez, Jeremy Ferwerda

Full text
To what extent does the perception of democratic backsliding motivate international migration? Drawing on global survey data, we first show that citizens dissatisfied with the state of democracy in their country are significantly more likely to express a desire to migrate. Leveraging variation in survey fielding dates relative to elections, we show that deconsolidating elections can act as catalysts, activating migration intentions particularly among those citizens most concerned with democratic freedoms and institutions. These findings suggest that elections can encourage the selective exit of democratically committed citizens, potentially contributing to a self-reinforcing cycle of democratic erosion in countries of origin.

Denied Departure: Mapping Emigration Bans in Asia and Africa 1960–2023

Flor Vandevelde, Polina Koriukina, Ilse Ruyssen

Full text
Origin-state restrictions on legal exit play a crucial role in shaping international mobility but remain largely undermeasured in migration governance research, which has focused primarily on destination-country immigration control. This paper introduces the Emigration Ban Indicator (EBI), the first time-varying, disaggregated measure of documented prohibitions on legal exit, covering 25 Asian and African countries from 1960 to 2023. The EBI records the presence and population coverage of six ban types—occupation bans, military conscription, women-specific, men-specific, general, and other—while its bilateral version captures destination-specific restrictions. Our analysis shows that, contrary to widespread belief, emigration bans have not become less common; rather, broad population-wide bans have given way to more targeted restrictions, especially on specific occupations. Patterns vary sharply by region: occupation bans, particularly those on domestic workers, concentrate in South and Southeast Asia, while military conscription bans dominate North Africa and the Horn of Africa. Different ban types are embedded in distinct political, gendered, labor market, and geopolitical contexts, and emigration bans should not be equated with immobility or the achievement of stated policy objectives. The EBI provides a new empirical basis for studying origin-state exit control and speaks to debates on migration governance, mobility inequality, and the right to leave.

Staying or Moving? Aspirations, Constraints and Residential Intentions in Italy's Major Urban Regions of Rome and Milan: Convergent Mechanisms and Divergent Motivations

Massimiliano Crisci, Stefania M. L. Rimoldi, Alessandro Coppola, Silvia Lucciarini

Full text
Residential mobility is a key mechanism of urban socio‐spatial change, yet less attention has been paid to the formation of mobility intention preceding actual moves, particularly in Southern European contexts. This paper investigates the socio‐economic, demographic, housing and neighbourhood factors associated with residential mobility intentions in Italy's two main urban regions, Rome and Milan, together with respondents' stated motivations for prospective mobility and immobility. Drawing on data from the 2025 MetroMosaic Survey, we use logistic regression models to assess whether established correlates of mobility intentions operate differently across two metropolitan contexts that share the same national housing system but have followed divergent socioeconomic and housing‐market trajectories. Results show substantial convergence between Rome and Milan: education, employment instability, housing tenure and adequacy, household composition and neighbourhood dissatisfaction are associated with mobility intentions in broadly similar ways. Greater differentiation emerges in stated motivations, with significant differences between the two urban regions concentrated in involuntary/forced and neighbourhood‐related reasons. Overall, the findings suggest that shared institutional conditions structure the opportunities and constraints underlying residential intentions, while contrasting metropolitan contexts emerge in some of the reasons associated with moving or staying. The study highlights the value of residential intentions for understanding how aspirations, resources and constraints interact before residential mobility is realised.

‘ If you had experienced what I experienced… ’: Un/Doing Refugeeness as Knowledge‐Making Practices of Interviewees From Authoritarian Regimes

Caterina Rohde‐Abuba

Full text
This paper explores the concept of refugeeness as a socially constructed identity shaped by both democratic and authoritarian migration management systems. Using qualitative interview data from studies with children and adults fleeing Syria, Iran, and Iraq, the article investigates what and how interviewees communicate about their refugeeness to non‐refugee researchers from Germany. Employing the theoretical concept of performativity, the paper demonstrates that the processes of un/doing refugeeness in research interviews are agentic, as interviewees actively produce knowledge about authoritarianism in relation to the positionality of researchers from democratic contexts. Key findings show that interviewees make refugeeness intelligible by simplifying complex experiences and referring to aspects of the authoritarian/democratic dichotomy that are familiar within German discourses and reflected in asylum law, such as religious coercion. Through VulnerAbility, interviewees performatively enact information about political repression and human rights violations by referring to emotions and bodily sensations, such as fear, sorrow, or pain, which convey to researchers what life under authoritarianism and on flight routes feels like. In this way, interviewees enable researchers to empathise with their experiences by imagining themselves in similar situations.

Income Support for Migrant Agricultural Workers: Differential Inclusion via Racialized Administrative Burdens Under Canada's Employment Insurance Program

Leah F. Vosko

Full text
Under Canada's Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program and Agricultural Stream, migrant agricultural workers (MAWs) arriving principally from Caribbean and Latin American countries are granted entry to Canada as essential workers. Yet they have limited access to social and statutory benefits and entitlements vis‐à‐vis citizens and permanent residents. Through an inquiry into rules governing access to benefits under Employment Insurance (EI) – a national contributions‐based unemployment insurance program providing income replacement in instances of job loss, sickness, maternity, parental, and caregiving responsibilities—this article analyzes administrative mechanisms contributing to MAWs' differential inclusion. Examining EI policy administration, an underexplored area of scholarly inquiry, it demonstrates how, given the structure of migrant work programs in agriculture, seemingly neutral rules governing the provision of income replacement contribute to disentitlement among MAWs otherwise fulfilling qualifying requirements. Bridging scholarship illustrating how policy design can shape differential access to EI benefits among social groups and research documenting racialized (im)migration policies opening exclusively temporary migration to MAWs historically and contemporaneously, the analysis highlights the role of racialized administrative burdens in impeding MAWs from accessing EI's full range of income supports.

Make It Easier, Not More Complex: Employee Views on Immigration Detention Decision‐Making in Canada

Bronwen Perley‐Robertson, Ralph C. Serin, Isabella R. L. Bossom, Sarah K. Watts, Shelley L. Brown

Full text
Rising public scrutiny has put immigration detention systems under pressure to defend their decision‐making practices. The Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) has drawn attention in this area, largely due to its partnerships with correctional agencies. Critics challenge CBSA's framework for placing noncitizens in these facilities, characterizing its risk assessment practices as opaque, arbitrary, and subjective. To address these concerns, CBSA commissioned us to develop an empirical tool for guiding immigration detention decisions—spanning conditional release to security classification level within detention. We surveyed 92 CBSA employees using mixed methods to gain their insights and ensure the operational utility of our tool. Top‐rated risk factors for decision‐making were criminal/compliance history, mental health, cooperation, identity concerns, and behaviour. We also identified misconceptions about standardized risk tools, the need for a streamlined assessment process, gaps in mental health guidance, operational decision‐making barriers, and opportunities for enhanced training. This study provides a foundation for developing an operationally useful tool that will improve the transparency and defensibility of CBSA's immigration detention decisions.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

¡Órale! : How Country-Specific Accents Limit the Effectiveness of Spanish-Language Campaign Appeals in the United States

Alejandro Frydman, Alejandro Flores

Full text
Spanish-language appeals are a common tool for mobilizing Latino voters, yet most research treats Spanish as a uniform cue of a panethnic Latino identity. This paper argues that Spanish-language communication operates through a dual-cue logic : it can signal shared panethnicity, while the accent of delivery simultaneously cues national origin attachments. Using a preregistered survey experiment of 953 Mexican-origin Latinos, we test how accent congruence—whether the accent in a Spanish-language appeal matches listeners’ national origin background—shapes evaluations of a Latino candidate. Respondents exposed to a Puerto Rican accent rated the candidate less favorably and perceived weaker commitment to the Mexican-American community than those exposed to a Mexican accent. These findings show that accent incongruence weakens the link between language and representation, highlighting how subtle linguistic variation can complicate identity-based outreach and efforts to mobilize the nation’s second-largest ethno-racial group.

Sisters, How Y’all Feel? Brothers, Y’all Alright?: Examining the Effects of Fear and Anger in the Politics of Diverse Men and Women

Nadia E. Brown, Camille Burge-Hicks, Christine M. Slaughter

Full text
Group identities and emotions shape how people experience politics. Groups provide cues about political participation and evaluations of policies and candidates. Although a burgeoning literature demonstrates that emotions matter for public opinion and decision-making, most research focuses on White Americans. When the emotions of people of color are examined, racial and ethnic groups are often studied separately rather than comparatively. We address these shortcomings by examining anger and fear among Black, Latino, Asian American, and White women and men. Examining these groups together reveals how emotions stimulate or constrain electoral and non-electoral political behavior. Drawing on the theory of racialized emotions, we argue that positionality within America’s race-gendered matrix of oppression shapes how fear and anger are channeled into political behavior. We find variation across diverse men and women in experiences of fear and anger and further, that these emotions have markedly different effects on electoral and non-electoral political participation.

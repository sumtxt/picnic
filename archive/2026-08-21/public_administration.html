Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory

Conditional Compliance: The Role of Sector and Policy Design in State Sexual Misconduct Policy

Jaeyeong Nam, Daniel L Fay, Emily Boykin, Elizabeth Bell

Full text
The role of principal-agent dynamics in performance and accountability reforms has been a recurring theme in public administration research. Yet, less attention has been dedicated to how the intersection of sector and policy design shapes principal-agent dynamics and subsequent policy outcomes. This study theoretically explores and empirically tests how sector and policy design shape the effects of state legislation that seeks to prevent shirking (mismanagement and underreporting) in the context of sexual misconduct outcomes in U.S. colleges and universities. We code every state policy designed to regulate higher education institutions’ management of sexual misconduct from 2007 to 2018 in the U.S. and leverage a Poisson Pseudo-Maximum Likelihood with a staggered difference-in-differences design to identify the effects. Our findings suggest that both sector and policy design matter: public institutions increase reporting in response to prevention policies that promote education and awareness, but prevention policies have no effect on private institutions. Other policies regulating enforcement and infrastructure produce null effects across both sectors. These findings have important implications for principal-agent theory and policy design research on effective methods for reducing shirking in social equity, accountability, and performance reporting.

Public Administration Review

Under Pressure: Analyzing LEO Turnover in America's Swing and Pivot Counties for 2016, 2020, 2024

Gary Johnson, Spencer Cragun, Jake Yoder

Full text
Post‐2024, the United States faces an acute labor crisis marked by accelerating Local Election Official (LEO) attrition. This study examines causal drivers of this exodus, focusing specifically on disproportionate turnover within volatile “swing” and “pivot” counties. Utilizing a mixed‐methods design, using longitudinal data from the Bipartisan Policy Center, US Census Bureau, Elections Voter Survey, MIT Election Data and Science Lab (MEDSL), and the Reed LEO survey, alongside an original dataset compiled from 74 competitive counties and 25 “super landslide” counties. Using Geographic Information Systems, we connect a detailed county profile to spatial mapping of the legal and political environment across the 2016, 2020, and 2024 elections. LEOs in highly competitive jurisdictions face an erosion of institutional trust, serving as a primary catalyst for departure. These volatile jurisdictions highlight where democratic machinery is vulnerable, offering evidence‐based insights for state‐level policy interventions designed to stabilize the public administrative workforce amidst unprecedented hyperpolarization.

Governing Before the State: Indigenous Javanese Administrative Theory

Dede Setiono

Full text
Public administration scholarship often treats the Weberian ideal type as a universal model of bureaucracy, making governance arrangements that differ from it appear deficient rather than different. A decolonizing approach instead asks whether non‐Western historical records can offer alternative ways of theorizing governance. This study explores that possibility through the Nagarakretagama, a 14th‐century Javanese court eulogy composed by Mpu Prapanca in 1365, which predates Machiavelli's The Prince by 148 years. Qualitative content analysis of all 98 cantos identifies five governance domains: bureaucratic hierarchy, mandala‐based territorial administration, conditional fiscal exemptions, a legal order combining written law and custom, and ceremonial practices integrated with administration. These dimensions show how the Javanese empire organized authority, territory, revenue, law, and legitimacy through institutional arrangements that differ from the Weberian model. Reading this governance system as theory reveals an alternative conceptualization of administration and advances a decolonizing, pluriversal approach to public administration.

Democratic Backsliding and Governance Outcomes: Measuring Administrative and Political Channels of Decline

David Ibañez, Ricardo A. Bello‐Gómez

Full text
Democratic backsliding has emerged as a defining political challenge of the contemporary era, yet systematic cross‐national evidence linking it to governance outcomes remains scarce. This article argues that backsliding affects governance through two analytically distinct channels: a political accountability channel, whereby weakened contestation and oversight reduce the external costs of poor performance; and an administrative channel, through which executive politicization erodes meritocratic recruitment and bureaucratic impartiality. Using a country‐year panel of 86 countries from 1996 to 2024 and generalized structural equation models, we decompose the direct, indirect, and total effects of backsliding on government effectiveness, regulatory quality, and control of corruption across stages of democratic decline. The findings show that governance deterioration is stage‐specific, channel‐specific, and outcome‐specific, following a nonlinear, U‐shaped pattern in which complete erosion—rather than autocratic reversion—produces the largest governance losses.

To Add Insult to Injury: Exploring the Impact of Policy Inequity on Administrative Burden

Yongzheng Yang, Qingqing Zhang, Peng Liu

Full text
The relationship between inequity and administrative burden constitutes a pivotal concern in the realm of public administration and policy, but how policy inequity (i.e., inequity in policy implementation) influences administrative burden has garnered scant attention. The present study employs data about public assistance programs in China spanning from 2015 to 2017 to examine the consequences of policy inequity on administrative burden. The empirical findings underscore that policy inequity augments psychological and compliance costs while concurrently diminishing citizens' participation in welfare programs, thereby indicating that policy inequity intensifies administrative burden. Notably, individual‐level social capital and contextual‐level information transparency in terms of public exposure related to welfare approval and receipt significantly moderate this relationship. Contrary to our theoretical expectations, social capital and public exposure amplify, rather than dampen, the impact of policy inequity on administrative burden. This study highlights the policy inequity lens and offers important implications for burden reduction.

Expansive Public Service Delivery: Advancing Public Value Governance

Mary K. Feeney, Julie Langer, Justin M. Stritch

Full text
Public administration has traditionally emphasized the normative ideal that public agencies have a responsibility to improve the lives of communities through public service delivery. We turn to public libraries to explore possibilities for what we call Expansive Public Service Delivery. Expansive Public Service Delivery is defined by a mission to serve all, professional and ethical responsibilities, and proactive responsiveness through outreach and (re)assessment. Public libraries have long aimed to improve communities and advance democracy by providing open, free information access to all. Today, they also proactively seek to expand their services to underserved groups ensuring their relevance and mission achievement. This research draws from 25 in‐depth qualitative interviews to explore librarian narratives to identify four core responsibilities that constitute Expansive Public Service Delivery. We conclude with a discussion of how service for all, professionalism, ethical responsibility, and reflexive decision‐making around community needs can result in more vibrant, sustainable public organizations.

Policy Studies Journal

Contested Rule Formation Under Institutional Mismatch: A Framework for Early‐Phase Governance of Disruptive Innovations

Sina Jangjoo

Full text
Disruptive innovations often diffuse faster than inherited policy institutions can classify, assign jurisdiction, and govern them. In these early phases, the central struggle is often not yet about the stringency of substantive standards, but about prior questions of what the innovation is (its legal and administrative classification), who is competent to decide (allocation of jurisdiction and decision authority), and where authoritative decisions should be made (designation of legitimate governance venues). This article develops Contested Rule Formation (CRF), a meso‐level, four‐stage framework for analyzing early‐phase governance under institutional mismatch in institutionally dense settings. CRF reconceptualizes institutional voids as localized misfit along five co‐evolving dimensions: recognition, authority, enforcement capacity, venue clarity, and interpretive frames. It then specifies a staged mechanism (incipient recognition, exploratory tactics, rule crystallization, and consolidation/backlash) in which classification and jurisdictional settlements function as rate‐limiting gates for durable rulemaking and for procedural learning, understood as a contingent trajectory rather than an automatic result of contestation. A proof‐of‐concept comparison of ride‐hailing governance in California and Austin/Texas shows how different category‐and‐venue pathways shaped early outcomes. The article closes with a diagnostic and comparative research agenda for studying upstream governance across AI, fintech, energy, and other domains of disruptive innovation and socio‐technical change.

Agile Digital Transformation and the Rule of Law: Perks and Perils in a Belgian and a Dutch Public Sector Organization

Shirley Kempeneer

Full text
This paper examines the interplay between agile digital transformation and the rule of law within the public sector, focusing on case studies from a semi‐large municipal government in the Netherlands and a department of the Flemish regional government in Belgium. Drawing on expert interviews, the paper explores how agile methods, championed for flexibility, interact with existing administrative routines and affect rule of law principles that prioritize consistency. Theoretically, the paper builds on public administration scholarship concerning administrative routines and the rule of law. Empirically, the paper shows that agile methods align well with administrative routines that prioritize decentralized leadership and collaboration. Moreover, it shows that agile approaches can foster, rather than threaten, the rule of law: many legal uncertainties arise not from agile practices themselves but from existing complexities in laws and policies, highlighting a need for stronger technical precision in lawmaking and administrative awareness in digital implementation.

From Social Equity Budgeting to Revenue Equity: Minority Leadership and Local Governments' Exploitative Revenues

Tingzhong Huang, Rui Wang, Can Chen, Herong Gao

Full text
Social equity budgeting has gained increasing attention, yet research has largely focused on expenditures while neglecting revenue equity. This study contributes to the literature by examining the relationships between minority population size and local governments' reliance on exploitative revenues, and whether minority political representation moderates this relationship. Drawing on racial threat and political representation theories, we analyze data from 66 Florida counties between 2011 and 2019. Results show that counties with larger minority populations rely more heavily on exploitative revenues, but this relationship diminishes when minority political representation increases. These findings highlight how minority leadership could moderate fiscal practices that disproportionately burden minority communities. More broadly, this study advances the framework of social equity budgeting by shifting the focus from expenditure allocation to revenue generation.

Organizational Capacity to Implement Social Equity in Emergency Management: A Local Government Perspective

Jason D. Rivera, Claire Connolly Knox, Clinton McNair, Joseph T. Ripberger

Full text
The emergency management profession and discipline is increasingly embracing social equity as a core value, yet limited attention has been paid to whether local emergency management organizations possess the capacity to implement equity‐focused practices. Drawing on the Capacity Building Framework, this exploratory study analyzes data from a national panel survey of US emergency managers to examine the organizational capacity to advance social equity through the lens of adaptive capacity. The findings reveal that while political, technical, financial, and managerial capacities matter, financial capacity is the most critical constraint. Respondents prioritized additional funding for personnel, community outreach and education, and the development of accessible public resources. The results highlight how resource scarcity shapes the pursuit of equity at the local level and underscores the need to align equity goals with investments in organizational capacity. This study contributes insight into the practical challenges emergency managers face in translating equity commitments into action.

Three Configurations of In‐House Government Consulting: Public‐Value Purpose and Organizational Viability

Sean Safford

Full text
Governments have created in‐house consulting organizations to reduce dependence on external firms and retain public capability and control. Yet organizations grouped under this label pursue different forms of public value that require different designs. This article distinguishes three reasons for a dedicated unit: retaining control over consequential work, capturing returns from problems that recur across government, and mobilizing expertise dispersed across public and adjacent institutions. It develops three corresponding configurations—Strategic Transformation, Scalable Solutions, and Expertise Platform—and shows how choices about work, staffing, knowledge, authority, client relationships, financing, and evaluation must fit together. Documentary comparison of eight units in five countries grounds the analysis. The cases show that viability depends on whether work renews the conditions needed for subsequent work and that evaluation must reflect configuration and stage. Internalization is therefore organization‐building, not the reversal of a procurement decision: a unit creates public value only when work reproduces the conditions on which that value depends.

Why Comply? Explaining Sectoral Patterns in GRECO 'S Anti‐Corruption Recommendations

Susana Coroado, Robert Gillanders

Full text
Why do states vary in their compliance with international anti‐corruption recommendations? Research often treats the state as a unitary actor and explains variation at country‐level. This article challenges that assumption by examining variation in compliance across institutional sectors within countries. Using original data from the Council of Europe's Group of States against Corruption (GRECO), the analysis shows a clear sectoral pattern. Recommendations addressed to prosecutors are implemented most frequently, followed by those targeting judges, while recommendations directed at members of parliament show the lowest levels of compliance. The article explains this variation through the political economy of target compliance. Reform outcomes depend on the interaction between the political costs imposed by reform and the degree of control that targeted actors exercise over its implementation. We find little evidence that country‐level factors shape reform outcomes, while sector‐specific incentives provide a stronger explanation for compliance patterns. By highlighting the importance of the target population, the article contributes to research on international compliance, anti‐corruption governance, and international monitoring mechanisms.

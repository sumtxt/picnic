International Journal of Press-Politics

How Recipients Compose Their News Sources Online: Utilizing Tracking Data to Investigate Digital News Media Repertoires

Robin Leuppert, Jule Scheper, Nico Spreen, Elena Link

Full text
Today’s high-choice media environment offers various opportunities for interest-driven news exposure, resulting in highly diverse and fragmented news usage patterns, especially in the digital media environment. Media repertoires provide a suitable theoretical framework for considering these circumstances and investigating the entirety and interrelatedness of individuals’ news sources, rather than focusing on singular news offerings. We explore media repertoires focusing on news by relying on behavioral tracking data from 805 German respondents over eleven weeks (November 2022–January 2023). Conducting a latent class analysis based on news usage frequencies across seventeen news categories, we identified eight digital news media repertoires with distinct usage patterns. Repertoire comparisons via analysis of variance (ANOVA) further revealed differences in the news topics used, users’ diversity of news outlets, news concentration, and news usage duration. Implications for future research on investigating media repertoire compositions are discussed.

The Policy Umwelt: How AI Problem Representations Render Governance Alternatives Unthinkable

Gleb Papyshev, Ninell Oldenburg

Full text
This paper investigates how AI narratives actively constrain the horizon of conceivable governance options. Moving beyond the established finding that narratives influence policy, we argue they fundamentally prefigure the scope of the politically thinkable by creating what we term “Policy Umwelten.” Adapting Jakob von Uexküll’s concept, a Policy Umwelt describes the perceptible and actionable landscape of interventions available within a given narrative, which systematically renders alternatives invisible. We analyze three competing narratives: Existential Risk, Accelerationism, and Critical AI. We do so through a “What’s the Problem Represented to Be?” analysis of their foundational manifestos. Each constructs a distinct Umwelt: Existential Risk makes global technocratic containment imperative; Accelerationism legitimizes only deregulation and market liberation; and Critical AI centers structural reform. Despite their overt conflict, these narratives converge in constructing Policy Umwelten that render a pragmatic approach to governance difficult to perceive: one that emphasizes iterative learning from empirical feedback and institutional flexibility over grand visions or predetermined certainties. The paper concludes that the most significant constraint on AI policy is not a lack of options but a collective failure of political perception. The Policy Umwelt concept thus provides a diagnostic tool for mapping ideological deadlock and identifying pathways toward more adaptive and less deterministic technological futures.

Social Media + Society

From Fringe to Power in the TikTok Age: Libertarian Ideology and Transgressive Performances in Milei’s ‘Populism Without the People’

Ana Ines Langer, Lluis de Nadal, Eugenia Mitchelstein

Full text
Democracies around the world are under strain from radical right populist leaders and parties, raising urgent questions about their appeal. One way to approach this is by examining what and how they communicate on social media. TikTok warrants close attention, not only as the fastest-growing platform for news and information, but also because of the wider ‘Tiktokification’ of social media and the growing importance of visual communication. Yet populist communication on TikTok has received little attention, reflecting a broader text-centric bias in the literature. Here, we trace Javier Milei’s journey from the political margins to the Argentine presidency through his communication on TikTok, a platform considered key to his electoral success. We combine content analysis of text, visual and performative dimensions and show that Milei rarely appeals to ‘the people’—striking given ‘people-centrism’ is typically considered populism’s defining feature. This is, however, consistent with his right-wing libertarian ideology, which elevates individual freedom over collective solidarity, and is fitting with TikTok’s individualised mode of consumption. Milei’s populism without ‘the people,’ as we call it, combines contempt for the State, anti-elitism and transgressive performances that, as our regression analysis reveals, generate higher engagement. These findings advance our understanding of populism beyond the Global North and for a leader celebrated by the populist radical right worldwide, with broader implications for the relationship between populism, digital media and liberal democracy.

The governance of snarking: Anti-fandom as tiny publics

Jessica Martin, Claudia Harris Coveney

Full text
This article examines online celebrity ‘snarking’ communities, otherwise known as anti-fandom spaces or even hate forums. Specifically, we provide an analysis of the governance of these groups and how they develop a collective morality through both articulations with the celebrities’ mediation and with the logics of social justice. Using data collected from a digital ethnography of two popular celebrity snark spaces, we employ Critical Discourse Analysis to track the particular discursive repertoires and vernaculars which characterise snark across multiple platforms. We argue that despite the perceived ‘incivility’ of these online spaces, discourses of care, justice and solidarity often emerge which complicate existing narratives of these groups. Though participation in snarking collectives is often explained in pathologising or individualising narratives of anti-fandom, we demonstrate some of the reflexive work that happens in these spaces which sometimes ignites sociological discussions and frustrations at inequalities. We therefore conceptualise snark through the lens of a ‘tiny public’ that identifies and explores broader race and class injustice in contemporary society. Here, the prevailing discourses about celebrity subjects ‘failing upwards’ betrays an anger at elites who do not suffer the same material consequences at times of crisis; though many of these frustrations are misdirected back at the celebrity subject and manifest in a collective desire to see repercussions for the individual celebrity, rather than collective action aimed at the structures that make this failure possible, visible and even lucrative.

Communication Research

What Do We Mean by Transparency? Conceptualizing and Operationalizing Transparency in Communication

Wenjia Yan, Li Crystal Jiang, Yiqian Gao, Pingping Jia

Full text
By analyzing 164 articles from communication journals published between 2002 and 2024, this scoping review systematically investigates temporal trends, conceptualizations, and operationalizations of transparency research within communication studies. The analysis identifies three primary conceptual dimensions of transparency: (1) information, encompassing both visibility and interpretation; (2) relations, functioning as an intermediary between dialogue and power; (3) values and norms, reflecting the tensions between ethical ideals and institutional constraints. Operationalization of transparency has predominantly relied on informational measures, whereas relational and value-based dimensions remain comparatively underdeveloped. Increasing attention has been directed toward subjective stakeholder perceptions, emphasizing transparency as a dynamic and context-dependent construct. Overall, the findings indicate that transparency extends beyond a stable informational attribute to incorporate relational and normative functions, while its enactment remains fluid, contextually shaped, and susceptible to unintended consequences.

Policy and Internet

Whose Digital Future? Civil Society and Alternative Imaginaries in Transnational Digital Governance

Outi Puukko

Full text
This article investigates different visions of digital futures, examining how desired futures are discursively constructed within civil society in transnational digital governance. The analysis builds on discourse theory and a growing body of research on imaginaries in digital governance. Through a discourse‐theoretical analysis of submission papers to the United Nations' Global Digital Compact, the article examines how different collective actors within civil society construct and mobilise desired futures in the institutional context of the UN. The study finds discursive elements that articulate three distinct digital futures: technological progress, societal balance and transformative change. While their objectives, obstacles and enablers are constructed in diverse ways, in all three, digital technologies are considered necessary. By illuminating different normative values and the affective potential through which individuals and collectives are positioned in relation to desired futures, these findings show a variety of articulations within civil society and offer insights into its normative and political roles. In doing so, this study contributes to an understanding of the possibilities and limitations of alternatives in digital governance.

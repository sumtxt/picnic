American Journal of Political Science

A theory of political divergence and gradual institutional change

Clair Yang

Full text
Why do some societies succeed in breaking away from historical traditions while others remain trapped? This paper proposes a formal theory to investigate the interaction between short‐run and long‐run political dynamics, highlighting a co‐evolution of political institutions and social structures. A society consists of various social groups, such as the rulers, the elites, and the commoners. Short‐run strategic considerations give rise to different coalitions among these groups, which determine not only political regimes but also redistribution policies. In the long run, these policies shape the accumulation of wealth and power, where small initial differences can lead to divergent paths of development. Finally, the paper examines three historical case studies on medieval Europe, imperial China, and the medieval Muslim world to illustrate potential pathways of long‐run political development.

Captured participatory institutions: Theory and evidence from Venezuela's Consejos Comunales

Jared Abbott, Jose Morales‐Arilla

Full text
Can participatory institutions (PIs) facilitate development when captured by the government? We propose an original theoretical framework to investigate how changing incumbent priorities can shape the developmental and political effects of captured PIs. While patrons concerned with survival can force PIs to specialize in political activities, those pursuing regime consolidation may incentivize PIs to multitask, delivering developmental gains and political rewards. We deploy our framework to study Venezuela's Communal Councils. Leveraging difference‐in‐differences analyses, an original survey of Council participants, and 51 interviews with government officials and grassroots Communal Council members, we find that Councils yielded both economic and political returns during an early period marked by political stability and regime consolidation. However, as Venezuela's economic collapse pushed the government into an authoritarian survival mode, Councils became politically specialized. While showing that captured PIs can foster development under certain circumstances, our findings cast doubt on PIs’ development‐enhancing potential under autocracy.

Public Opinion Quarterly

An Experimental Comparison of AI-Enabled Semi-Structured Interviews and Fixed Surveys: Respondent Satisfaction, Response Patterns, Quality, and Representation

Amanda Austin, Edward Hohe, Ryan Kennedy, Leib Litman, William Minozzi, Laura Moses

Full text
Artificial intelligence (AI) interviewing offers the potential to enhance the depth and quality of survey research, yet it may also introduce respondent fatigue and unintended consequences for the measurement of political attitudes. From the standpoint of respondents, more extensive interviews impose greater cognitive and temporal burdens, which may reduce engagement or increase satisficing. From the perspective of political behavior researchers, deeper probing may shift the subset of beliefs or arguments that respondents access when forming opinions, thereby altering the distribution and apparent stability of measured attitudes. To investigate these dynamics, we conduct a large-scale, fully randomized experiment. We find that AI-mediated interviewing significantly increases both the volume and specificity of respondents’ articulated rationales, but any decline in participant satisfaction is minuscule and we detect no increase in participant rolloff. However, AI interviews appear to shape subsequent responses: After offering justifications through AI interaction, respondents exhibit greater polarization in follow-up items. This result suggests that AI interviewing may alter the cognitive architecture of opinion formation. These findings both highlight the potential for AI interviewing and underscore the need for studies of how AI interviewing may become a source of attitudinal change.

Attitudes Toward Energy Across European Union Member States (2006–2024)

Piotr Jabkowski, Mariusz Baranowski, Piotr Cichocki

Full text
Our study describes European attitudes toward energy using a harmonized dataset comprising 46 waves of the Eurobarometer (2006–2024). This tracking survey covers all European Union member states; hence, our data accumulates 1,286 individual country surveys. We focus on attitudes relevant to energy transition through three complementary lenses: energy issue salience, policy priorities, and opinions on renewable energy sources. This study documents cross-national and temporal variation in attitudes without estimating the causal effects of specific energy-related European policies or contextual events. We record a marked increase in the salience of energy during crisis periods, especially Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, which rapidly elevated energy’s importance in the hierarchy of public concerns. We also show that support for renewables, in the context of energy transition, coexists with affordability and security concerns in some regions of the EU, particularly in Central-Eastern and Southern Europe. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically harmonize such a large collection of Eurobarometer waves into a single comparable dataset, enabling long-term tracking of energy attitudes across all EU member states. In addition to the substantive results, our study also points to several difficulties the Eurobarometer project faces in providing long-term monitoring of public opinion across the highly diverse member states of the European Union.

AI and the party: The three faces of party organization and artificial intelligence

Şebnem Yardımcı-Geyikçi, Patricia Correa, Giulia Sandri, Philipp Darius, Jasmin Fitzpatrick, Anne Goldmann, Fabio Lupato, Erdem Yörük, Kristina Weissenbach

Full text
This Research Note explores the emerging intersection of artificial intelligence (AI) and party politics by applying Katz and Mair’s “three faces” framework to analyze the role of AI in reshaping party organization. While AI is not yet uniformly transforming parties, its growing use in campaigning, policymaking, membership engagement, and internal management introduces new dynamics of centralisation, personalisation, and automation. The note highlights both the opportunities AI offers for efficiency and responsiveness, and the risks it poses to transparency, accountability, and democratic representation. It argues that the incorporation of AI into party life is uneven, contested, and mediated by broader political and institutional configurations, and require closer empirical and conceptual scrutiny. In doing so, the paper outlines a new research agenda for understanding how technological change intersects with party transformation in contemporary democracies.

Ballots and battlefields: Assessing the impact of the war in Ukraine on voting in the 2022 Italian general election

Claudio Cozzi Fucile, Luciana Fazio, Pierangelo Isernia, Sergio Martini, Paolo Bellucci

Full text
Research on the electoral impact of foreign policy has largely focused on the United States. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine offers an opportunity to reassess this question in a European context. This article examines the role the war in Ukraine played in shaping electoral behaviour in Italy during the September 2022 general elections. Relying on the ITANES pre- and post-electoral survey, we assess whether attitudes toward supporting Ukraine contributed to explaining Italian voter choices. The results indicate that orientations toward the Ukraine war, operationalised as support for both economic sanctions against Russia and military assistance to Ukraine, were associated with voter choice, even though domestic issues, such as immigration, remained more influential in structuring electoral preferences. We argue that this pattern reflects the strategic behaviour of parties in addressing a politically sensitive and potentially divisive issue. The article contributes to the ongoing debate on the electoral relevance of foreign policy by providing new evidence from a critical moment for the European continent.

Public support for cordons sanitaires against the radical left and radical right

Alexander Davenport, Jørgen Eikvar Axelsen, Aleš Kudrnáč

Full text
Though radical parties have often been excluded from government by mainstream elites (the ‘cordon sanitaire’), little is known about how voters evaluate such practices. Using data from the Czech Republic, where radical left and radical right parties have both been excluded, we examine voters’ support for the cordon sanitaire, and whether they differentiate between parties in this regard. We find considerable popular support for the cordon sanitaire, with less than a quarter of respondents rejecting this idea outright. Notably, left-wing voters are more likely to oppose cordons sanitaires against both the radical left and right, while right-wing voters are highly supportive of excluding both parties from government, with limited support among the electorate for excluding only one side of the ideological spectrum. The results indicate that where the cordon sanitaire has been established at the elite level, it may garner substantial popular support for excluding a range of radical parties.

Political Research Quarterly

Governing Party Issue Ownership and How Citizens Assess Conditions

Seth B. Warner

Full text
It is well-documented that Americans perceive conditions more favorably when their preferred party is in power. This study considers a second, more subtle, but potentially impactful partisan bias: that the governing party’s policy reputations lead voters to perceive better conditions on the issues it “owns.” In US politics, voters trust Democrats more on environmental, healthcare, education, and safety net issues while Republicans are favored on immigration, crime, defense, and keeping taxes low. In two studies, I observe whether these reputations also impact perceptions when each party is in power. First, I analyze hundreds of survey toplines from Gallup Trends, dating from 1956 to the present, about public perceptions on a variety of party-owned policy areas. Second, to leverage greater variation in control of subnational government, I analyze seven surveys that ask about state-level conditions as well. While results from the former analysis are only suggestive, both approaches point toward an association between the governing party and how citizens assess conditions on the issues it owns.

How Election Fundamentals and Racialized Considerations Shape Asian American Vote Choice Through the Trump Era

Joyce H. Nguy, Nathan K. Chan, Jessica HyunJeong Lee

Full text
Racial minorities voted for Trump at higher rates in 2024 than in previous elections, prompting renewed interest in the factors driving minority vote choice. Here, we focus on Asian Americans, the fastest growing single-race group in the United States. Bridging theories of economic voting and race-centered explanations, we present a comprehensive, up-to-date account of Asian American vote choice through the Trump era. We analyze the 2024, 2020, and 2016 Collaborative Multiracial Post-Election Surveys to assess indicators of Trump support over time among Asian Americans, with comparative analysis of Black, Latino, and White voters ( N = 24,061). We find that economic considerations shaped vote choice in 2024 among Asian Americans, and all other racial groups. Linked fate remained associated with lower Trump support among Asian Americans in 2024, indicating that race continued to matter even years after the peak of anti-Asian hate. In comparison, linked fate also shaped opposition to Trump among Black voters but not Latinos in 2024. Throughout the Trump era, racial resentment among non-Black racial groups and anti-immigrant sentiment among all racial groups consistently predicted greater Trump support, including Asian Americans, despite their predominantly immigrant-origin backgrounds. This research contributes to growing scholarship that addresses conservatism among minoritized communities.

Political Theory

Benjamin Franklin and Democratic Education Beyond Schooling

Rita Koganzon

Full text
Political theorists have long focused their understanding of democratic education on the institution of the school, particularly the public school, to the exclusion of broader visions of education beyond schooling. But as criticisms of schooling from outside the academy mount, it becomes imperative to examine the origins of American educational thought to determine whether opposition to schooling has an older historical source. This essay locates the roots of school skepticism in an alternative American educational tradition beginning with Benjamin Franklin that drew on Locke’s pedagogical arguments to devalue institutional schooling in favor of self-education. Franklin’s educational vision harnessed the independence of the young in early America by incentivizing learning through friendship and self-interest, and resorted to schools only as a guardrail against the abuse of freedom, incorporating them but minimizing their role in education. In this, Franklin initiated the Americanization of Lockean pedagogy, or the school-skeptical tradition in American thought.

Comparative Politics

Covering the Big Tigers in China: Patronage Ties and Political Signaling within Elite Circles

Yixuan Zhang

Full text
Official media in authoritarian regimes typically supports pro-government narratives, yet it also reports on high-ranking officials after corruption investigations into them, despite risking public criticism and offering little new information to central leaders. Why does this occur? I argue that such reporting functions as intra-elite signaling. The central media’s selective focus on certain disgraced officials helps autocrats signal power consolidation, while local media imitation allows local elites to display loyalty. Using an original dataset of news reports on high-ranking officials investigated during China’s anti-corruption campaign (2012–2017), I find that central media disproportionately targeted “big tigers” linked to the top leader’s political rivals. Local media largely mirrored this pattern, highlighting the strategic role of official media in managing relations between autocrats and other elites.

Who belongs to the demos? Undemocratic penalties to immigrant minority representation

Beyza E. Buyuker, Alexandra Filindra, Mia K. Gandenberger, Eva T. Green, Anita Manatschal

Full text
Opposition to political candidates who represent ethnic minority interests in Western societies has included calls for undemocratic penalties. Majority groups in democratic societies value democracy in principle, but such support is malleable. We argue that diverse societies lack consensus on who constitutes the “demos”, the imagined political community. When there is a mismatch between a citizen’s understanding of the demos and an officeholder’s background and/or agenda, citizens may be open to imposing undemocratic penalties by reclassifying the offender as a demos-outsider and/or the punishing action as democratic. We tested our expectations with three survey experiments in the US and Switzerland that varied the candidate’s background (majority v. immigrant minority) and agenda (advocating for an age quota v. ethnic diversity quota). Results confirm that citizens do not behave as principled democrats but rather use their understanding of the demos to support or reject undemocratic penalties against minority politicians and policies. Their vision of the demos also determines whether they perceive the candidate as a demos-outsider (not “true” American/Swiss) and his banning as democratic. Interestingly, support for undemocratic penalties is on average higher in Switzerland than in the US, and slight nuances in the empirical results point to certain contextual contingencies of our results. Overall, our cross-country results suggest that people’s understanding of democracy is not based on firm institutional and procedural criteria, but on distinct visions about who belongs, and who does not.

International Migration Review

Climate-Related South–South Migration in the Pacific Islands Region: Assessing Regional, Subregional, and Bilateral Governance Frameworks

Sargam Goundar

Full text
Climate change is reshaping population movements within and between Pacific Island Countries and Territories (PICTs), exposing critical gaps in how international migration is understood and governed in the Pacific. This study assesses key regional, subregional, and bilateral agreements in the Pacific to understand how they engage with climate mobility and to identify areas of neglect. It highlights a novel finding: while population movements between Pacific islands are a longstanding reality in the region, existing governance frameworks have so far rarely addressed climate-related South–South migration between PICTs. As a result, the needs of vulnerable receiving communities within PICTs have been largely overlooked in both research and policy. The paper further argues that this oversight reflects broader Global North biases in migration scholarship and governance that continue to shape orthodox understandings of climate-related mobility commonly framed through South to North migration paradigms. By centering South–South dynamics in the Pacific, a region often marginalized in global migration research, this study contributes to efforts to decenter dominant migration frameworks and calls for more inclusive approaches to climate-related migration scholarship and governance that reflect Pacific realities and the perspectives of affected communities.

Age at First Marriage or Cohabitation Among First-, Second-, and 2.5-Generation Immigrant Women, Finland 1987–2020

Citlali Trigos-Raczkowski, Kelsey Q. Wright, Joonas Pitkänen, Silvia Loi, Pekka Martikainen, Heta Moustgaard, Mikko Myrskylä

Full text
Immigrants and their descendants are increasingly forming families across Europe, yet little is known about how immigration background shapes first union entry in emerging immigration contexts. Here, we examine whether immigrant generation, age at arrival, and sending region are associated with entry into opposite-sex first unions for women in Finland from 1987 to 2020. Treating cohabitation and direct marriage as competing events, we estimate cause-specific hazards in separate accelerated failure time models. We find a systematic bifurcation in union entry: while nearly all immigrant groups forgo the modal Finnish path of first entering a nonmarital cohabitation, specific subgroups—including the second generation, first generation arriving during adolescence, and women from the Former Soviet Union, Eastern Europe, North America, South Asia, and the Middle East and North Africa—exhibit accelerated transitions to direct marriage. While these findings demonstrate heterogeneity in the pathways by which immigrant women enter unions, with no single narrative of delay or convergence capturing the full picture, the overall pattern of later and less cohabitation and higher levels of direct marriage suggest that immigrant women face substantively different cultural, institutional, and social capital conditions than nonimmigrant women.

Journal of Refugee Studies

The first 4 years: evolution of community attitudes towards refugees in a new rural resettlement location in Australia

Susan E Watt, Stefania Paolini, Tadgh McMahon

Full text
This study examined the evolution of host community attitudes towards refugees in the first 4 years of a new rural resettlement location. We used threat, contact, and attribution theories to obtain a dynamic picture of responses to refugee settlement. Six telephone surveys with host community adults (N = 1,132) gathered cross-sectional and longitudinal data. Attitudes towards refugees became increasingly positive, perceived threats declined, intergroup contact initially grew and then decreased (during the pandemic), and the host community became more positive regarding the refugee intake numbers. Neighbours were perceived as more likely to help one another, and multicultural neighbourhoods were perceived as getting along increasingly well, although with a drop from 2020 to 2021. Interestingly, contact outcomes were attributed to refugees rather than self or circumstances. National survey data confirmed the changes did not reflect broader trends. These results demonstrate that even in less diverse rural areas, positive intergroup dynamics can develop, challenging stereotypes of closed-mindedness and resistance to newcomers.

In Pursuit of Spatial, Life‐Course and Social Mobilities: Aspiring Filipino Seafarers Acquiring Migration Ability Through Servitude

Roderick Galam

Full text
This article examines aspiring Filipino seafarers' pursuit of the triple mobilities : spatial (migration), life course (education to work, youth to adulthood) and social. It focuses on those working as ‘utility cadets’ for manning agencies that supply seafarers to ship operators in exchange for a ship placement. Its aims are twofold. Based on long‐term research and drawing on interviews with utility cadets, successful seafarers who were once utility cadets, and parents or older siblings of utility cadets, it first conceptualises as ability acquisition how aspiring Filipino seafarers turn servitude into a strategy to accumulate the ability to migrate. Second, it shows how this spatial mobility enables their life‐course and social mobilities, and accounts for how these three mobilities are interconnected. The pace of life‐course mobility shaped and was shaped by the degree of the seafarers' contribution to their family's social mobility , and both constituted and were constituted by the seafarers' rank in the seafaring labour hierarchy, which determined their salary. The article further conceptualises the intertwinement of the triple mobilities by explaining their negotiation through processes of convergence and divergence .

Reconceptualising Social Integration: Experiences of Internal Youth Migrants in Ghana

Francis Diawuo Darko, Susanne Schech, Udoy Saikia

Full text
Research on migrant social integration has focused mainly on international migration, leaving internal migration, especially in the Global South, underexplored as a site of theory‑building. This paper reconceptualises social integration through an empirical study of young internal migrants in Sunyani, Ghana, a secondary city characterised by informality and decentralised governance. Drawing on a mixed‑methods design of survey data and qualitative narrative interviews, the analysis shows that integration is not a linear or place‑bound outcome but a negotiated process shaped by material precarity, social relations, institutional mediation, and sustained connections to places of origin. While economic participation and co‑ethnic bonding networks enable survival in the informal economy, limited cross‑group trust and exclusionary encounters with local authorities constrain broader inclusion. Importantly, the paper advances translocality as a constitutive dimension of social integration, showing that ongoing obligations to origin communities function as adaptive strategies for managing risk and belonging in contexts of weak urban safety nets, while simultaneously reshaping local embedding. By foregrounding internal migration in a secondary African city, the study challenges state‑centred and settlement‑based integration models and contributes to population geography by reframing social integration as a spatially distributed, relational process within national borders.

Deceptive Citizenship as an Instrument of New War: Authoritarian Practices in a Post‐Colonial Context

Irina Kuznetsova

Full text
The article demonstrates how, under authoritarian practices and post‐colonialism, a state employs deceptive citizenship as an instrument of a ‘new war.’ The deceptive citizenship operates within informational autocracy for a selected category of migrantised people, which make them unaware about the legal, political and mobility consequences of this status. By obscuring the legal, political, and mobility consequences of this status, it enables authorities to symbolically project power beyond territorial boundaries and consolidate their geopolitical post‐colonial standing among citizens. Using Russia's citizenship policies towards displaced Ukrainians between 2014 and 2022 as a case study, this article traces the complex genealogy of these policies and shows how, prior to the full‐scale invasion, they served geopolitical ambitions and postcolonial hegemony. At the same time, it demonstrates that displaced Ukrainians' citizenship practices were driven primarily by the need to cope with uncertainty and insecurity.

Neighbourhood Ethnic Diversification in Urban and Rural England and Wales

Richard Wright, Gemma Catney, Mark Ellis, Momoko Nishikido

Full text
The minority ethnic share of the population of England and Wales rose from 8.7% in 2001 to over 18% in 2021. Using 2001, 2011 and 2021 Census data for England and Wales with consistent neighbourhood boundaries, this paper investigates how these aggregate changes translated into alterations in neighbourhood ethnic composition across the urban–rural continuum. We classify neighbourhoods into three diversity types (low, moderate, high) via an entropy‑based metric and track transitions over two decades. Ethnic and racial diversity grew in all neighbourhood types and geographic settings. Between 2001 and 2021, neighbourhoods with all‐White populations all but disappeared, neighbourhoods classified as low diversity with a large White majority declined in number, and counts of moderately diverse neighbourhoods increased. Additionally, the proportion of highly diverse (multiethnic) neighbourhoods tripled during this period. These changes follow a broadly hierarchical pattern, focused first, and most intensively, in London and subsequently occurring in secondary Metropolitan counties and relatively smaller urban settings. Finally, we detect some decade‐specific differences, notably a deceleration in neighbourhood transitions toward greater ethnic diversity between 2011 and 2021. A counterfactual analysis of the consolidation of ethnic diversity into moderately diverse neighbourhoods rather than further condensation into high diversity spaces suggests the rising importance of locational preferences and constraints relative to demographic forces in shaping these new neighbourhood geographies.

The Factors Driving Migration Intentions and Destination Preferences in Central, East, and Southeast European Countries

Antea Barišić, Mahdi Ghodsi, Alireza Sabouniha, Robert Stehrer

Full text
This paper studies the determinants of the intentions to emigrate and conditional on migration aspirations, the destination choice in a number of Central East and Southeast European economies (CESEE). Using data from the OeNB Euro Survey, we apply a two‐stage Heckman procedure to identify the drivers of migration intentions – specifically individuals' demographic and household attributes, financial conditions, past economic experiences, and future expectations. In the second stage, conditional on the stated intention to migrate, we examine the determinants of destination choice, distinguishing between EU15, EU‐CEE, and extra‐EU countries. This micro‐level evidence indicates that economic and financial conditions (past, present, and expected) play an important role in shaping migration intentions, while non‐economic factors, including individual demographic and family attributes, as well as network ties, also exert important influence. Destination preferences, conditional on an already existing intention to migrate are not simply a mechanical extension of intention determinants. The choice of the destination region is determined mostly by marital status, educational attainment, and GDP per capita variables in the origin and destination region. This reflects the conceptualisation of migration as a two‐stage decision process, distinguishing between entry into the pool of potential migrants and destination choice within that pool. This stage‐specific perspective thus allows to differentiate determinants of intentions and destination choice of prospective mobility, that empirical results based on actual migration decisions cannot deliver.

Household Assets and Internal Mobility in Laos: Agricultural Land, Home Ownership, and Multi‐Stream Population Redistribution

Phengsy Dalavong, Chang Gyu Choi

Full text
Internal migration in agrarian economies is usually framed as a rural‐to‐urban transition, but population redistribution follows multiple pathways. Household assets such as land and housing shape these moves, and how they do so is contested: some research finds that assets anchor households in place, while other work finds that they enable movement. This debate has centred on rural‐to‐urban flows in settings of uneven ownership, leaving open how assets relate to the full range of mobility, including non‐migration and rural‐bound moves, where ownership is near‐universal. This study examines that question in Lao PDR, a setting of gradual urbanisation and continued rural dominance. Drawing on the nationally representative Lao Social Indicator Survey, it models three outcomes: migration versus non‐migration, rural versus urban residence among non‐migrants, and the direction of movement among migrants. Two findings emerge. First, mobility in Laos is multidirectional rather than a single rural‐to‐urban flow: measured against rural–urban migration, rural–rural movement is about three times as common, and urban–urban and urban–rural flows each about twice as common. Second, ownership and renting point in opposite directions: agricultural land and home ownership accompany staying and rural‐bound movement, while renting and internet access accompany urban‐oriented mobility. Notably, urban residence does not preclude landholding: most urban residents still own farmland. These findings extend the population geography of assets and mobility beyond the rural‐to‐urban paradigm, revealing a household‐level dual attachment in which the same assets anchor and enable mobility at once.

Rethinking Mobile Ethnography: Spatial and Social Experiences of Older Migrants

Tuğçe Tezel, Basak Bilecen, Nardi Steverink

Full text
Europe's ageing population includes a growing number of older migrants whose experiences of ageing in place require methods that capture mobility, place, and social participation. Mobile ethnography, particularly walking/go‐along interviews, offers valuable opportunities to examine how older migrants engage with everyday environments. Yet existing mobile research with older migrants has often focused on participants able to take part in walking interviews, leaving less attention to how mobile ethnography can be applied with migrants in advanced age and with different mobility conditions. Drawing on fieldwork with migrants from Turkey in Germany, nearly all aged 70 and above, this paper develops a more flexible understanding of mobility within mobile ethnography. It shows how engagement with place can be explored through walking, but also through familiar community settings, shorter movements, narrated routes, remembered places, supported mobility, and repeated everyday routines. The paper offers a more inclusive methodological approach for research with ageing migrant populations.

A Gamified Strategy to Resist Migration Disinformation in Adolescents

Dana Ondrušková

Full text
Migration‐related disinformation has become increasingly prominent in online environments, particularly in the context of war‐related migration and polarized public discourse. Adolescents may be especially vulnerable to manipulative online narratives due to ongoing cognitive and social development. This study examined whether the gamified inoculation intervention Bad News could strengthen adolescents' resistance to migration‐related disinformation. A quasi‐experimental pre–post design was conducted among Czech secondary school students aged 16–18 ( N = 235). Participants evaluated simulated social media posts before and after gameplay using measures of perceived credibility and confidence. Truth discernment was defined as the difference between credibility ratings of accurate and misleading content. Results showed reduced perceived credibility of disinformation, increased confidence in evaluation, and improved truth discernment following gameplay. Overall belief remained unchanged, suggesting improved discrimination rather than generalized scepticism. The findings support the educational potential of gamified inoculation approaches for strengthening adolescents' resilience to migration‐related disinformation.

La Muerte del Coyote: Human Smuggling in the Age of Asylum

Jeremy Slack, Emilio Alberto López Reyes

Full text
Over the last decade, the prominence of asylum or asylum‐adjacent migration has overtaken undocumented migration, where many people are seeking to hand themselves into authorities rather than avoid detection. This has dramatically altered services offered by human smugglers. This article explores how smugglers adapted to current migration processes at the border and what strategies have been employed to continue to be profitable. By building on the ‘migration industry’ framework, we explore how illegal enterprises evolve in response to changes in migration policy in ways that both facilitate migration but also hinder migration and seek rents. This article expands on the concept of ‘bastard’ industries, helping understand how and why smuggling has developed an interconnected network of kidnapping and extortion. We argue that these changes expose some fundamental aspects of smuggling rather than representing an aberration of the smuggling industry.

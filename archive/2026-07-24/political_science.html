American Journal of Political Science

Correcting measurement error bias in conjoint survey experiments

Katherine Clayton, Yusaku Horiuchi, Aaron R. Kaufman, Gary King, Mayya Komisarchik

Full text
Conjoint survey designs are spreading across the social sciences due to their unusual capacity to estimate many causal effects from a single randomized experiment. Unfortunately, by their ability to mirror complicated real‐world choices, these designs often generate substantial measurement error and thus bias. We replicate both the data collection and analysis from eight prominent conjoint studies, all of which closely reproduce published results, and reveal high levels of measurement error in all. We then discover a common empirical pattern in how measurement error appears in conjoint studies and, with it, introduce an easy‐to‐use statistical method to correct the bias. Along the way, we provide a much simpler and simultaneously more powerful approach to designing, organizing, understanding, and analyzing conjoint data analyses.

Electoral contests over voter beliefs

Dan Alexander, Alexander Evert

Full text
Voter perceptions of candidates are the substrate on which campaigns act. It is costly, however, for voters to devote enough attention to politics to change their own perceptions of candidates. This paper views campaigns—and the time, effort, and money they expend—as subsidizing rationally inattentive voters' adoption of new beliefs about candidate platforms. We employ an all‐pay contest to model costly competition over voter perceptions. In our baseline model, campaigns seek to improve perceptions of their own candidates. In a variant, candidates engage in negative campaigning. Among other results, we observe platform divergence under more reasonable informational assumptions than previous spatial models, replacing candidate uncertainty about voters with voter uncertainty about candidates. This model serves as a bridge between behavioral research on campaigns and theoretical literature on elections, offering a richer set of empirical predictions while preserving the insights of the canonical Downsian framework as a limiting case.

Contesting repression in divided democracies: Can counternarratives reduce support for police violence?

Yanilda María González, Natán Skigin

Full text
Democracies often struggle to contain police violence, which undermines the rule of law and reproduces inequality by targeting marginalized citizens. Yet, scholars have shown that repressive policing enjoys considerable public support. Focusing on Brazil, we demonstrate that attitudes toward police violence mirror polarization between left‐ and right‐wing voters, and investigate how competing narratives change attitudes toward police violence through multiple mechanisms. We argue that marginalized victims’ counternarratives can counteract police discourses justifying violence through perspective‐getting, enabling citizens to hear about the experiences of stigmatized groups. Using a nationwide survey experiment, we find that although police narratives increase endorsement of lethal violence, statements from victims’ mothers emphasizing innocence and disadvantage reduce support for police killings—even among right‐wing voters—by increasing empathy and reducing victim‐blaming. We demonstrate that, even in deeply unequal and polarized democracies where exclusionary attitudes align with entrenched ideological divides, counternarratives from marginalized groups can shape public attitudes toward injustices.

Comparative Political Studies

For Everything There is a Season: Seasonality and Retrospective Voting in African Elections

Brian Engelsma

Full text
Are African elections subject to seasonal effects? I argue that agricultural seasonality patterns election outcomes by influencing voters’ retrospective performance evaluations. When countries hold elections after harvests, voters reward incumbents. As the year continues and conditions worsen, voters begin to sour on incumbents. I construct a measure of agricultural seasons using satellite imagery and pair it with an original dataset of 145 executive-election results from 32 countries, as well as with over 100,000 Afrobarometer survey responses. I first show a curvilinear relationship between the time from harvest and support for incumbent executives. Initially, voter support for incumbents increases following a harvest, but this relationship eventually reverses before the next harvest. To account for endogenous election timing, I also examine quasi-experimental evidence from two Zambian by-elections triggered by the deaths of incumbents. Finally, I analyze survey responses to probe for causal mechanisms and find evidence of sociotropic rather than pocketbook voting.

Property Rights and Social Institutions in Urban Africa: Experimental Evidence From a Land Formalization Program in the DRC

Pablo Balán, Augustin Bergeron, Gabriel Tourek, Jonathan L. Weigel

Full text
Formal property rights to land remain rare in sub-Saharan Africa. We argue that social institutions shape citizens’ demand for land formalization. When offered the opportunity to formalize, citizens weigh the insurance and tenure-security benefits of informal institutions against their monetary and social obligations. We study a randomized land titling program in a large Congolese city that sharply reduced the costs of acquiring a title. The program caused large increases in both initiation and receipt of titles. Demand was strongest among citizens more engaged in social institutions and more connected to city chiefs, yet such ties did not predict completion of the titling process. Program assignment also reduced citizens’ participation in social institutions and worsened their evaluations of chiefs. These findings suggest that, in urban settings where land values are higher and social institutions are more costly, citizens may exit social institutions when formal alternatives become available, illustrating how formalization can reshape engagement with informal authority.

British Journal of Political Science

When Legislators Do Not Differentiate: A Field Experiment on British MPs’ Responses to Constituent Policy Queries

Daniel Bischof, Vanessa Cheng-Matsuno, Gidon Cohen, Sarah Cohen, Florian Foos, Patrick M Kuhn, Kyriaki Nanou, Neil Visalvanich, Nick Vivyan

Full text
How do legislators respond to constituent policy queries? Existing research suggests that legislators vary their responsiveness and tailor response content depending on constituent congruence, raising normative concerns about legislator–constituent communication. We report results from a pre-registered audit study of UK MPs which is the first to test whether legislators in party-centred parliamentary democracies vary substantive response rates and content depending on constituent–party policy congruence. We theorise that electoral incentives lead MPs to respond more to congruent constituents, and to place more emphasis on self over party when doing so. Our audit study uses real constituents as confederates, allowing better measurement of substantive responses. We find little evidence that constituent–party congruence affects MPs’ responsiveness or the personal versus party emphasis of their replies. These null findings refine understanding of legislator behaviour in parliamentary systems and are normatively encouraging regarding the role that legislator–constituent policy communications can play in parliamentary democracies.

Thinly Veiled: The Use of Femonationalist Speech in European Parliaments

Jorge M. Fernandes, Bonnie M. Meguid, Ana Catalano Weeks

Full text
Femonationalism – the use of gendered rhetoric to promote nativist anti-immigration views – is thought to be on the rise across Western European democracies, but scholars lack a systematic understanding of its prevalence across parties and countries, the conditions under which it appears, and the role gender plays in its use. To make progress on these questions, this study explores the use of femonationalism in legislative speeches from five Western European countries from 2009 to 2024. Combining dictionary methods with topic modeling, we find that far-right parties are most likely to use femonationalist rhetoric, increasing these appeals when party opponents threaten their issue distinctiveness by converging towards their anti-immigration policy position. Crucially, our analysis also highlights the role of gender in shaping femonationalist rhetoric: while the overall strategy is party-driven, men and women legislators emphasize different dimensions of femonationalism, with men focusing on security concerns and women invoking gender equality.

Perspectives on Politics

Do Voters Understand the Benefits of Taxes?

Benjamin Carter, Alessandro Del Ponte, Peter DeScioli

Full text
We use economic experiments to study a fundamental question in political economy: Do voters understand the benefits of taxes? Participants play an economic game as members of a virtual society composed of computer players. In each round, the players earn low or high income, see the distribution of income, and then vote for low or high taxes. We test whether participants vote rationally for high taxes when poor and low taxes when rich, following a classic model in political economy, or whether they focus on the burden of taxes more than the benefits. Across three experiments, poor players voted against high taxes 30% to 50% of the time, and they were more opposed when the same policy was labeled “taxes” than when it was labeled “sharing.” In Experiment 2, Singaporean participants opposed taxes at about the same rate as those of Americans in Experiment 1. In Experiment 3, participants’ opposition to taxes persisted when they received low income every round. These results support the burden hypothesis: voters focus more on the burden of paying taxes than on the benefits they provide. We reflect on what the results imply for the politics of taxes.

Silencing Dissent: Academic Freedom and Transnational Suppression of Palestinian Solidarity

Sevgi Doğan, Donatella Della Porta, Federico Alagna

Full text
Israel’s war on Gaza has prompted a wave of protests against human rights violations that has spread across most European countries. As universities have become arenas of contestation, there has been an increase in forms of repression of academic freedom and of political rights within academia. Our systematic, case-oriented analysis of an original database enables us to identify specific repertoires of repression employed by different institutions, as well as the characteristics of the targeted academics. By comparing Germany and the United States as case studies of high and increasing levels of repression—albeit characterized by differing systems of higher education and conceptions of academic freedom—we analyze the strategies used by these actors and how they interact. In this article, we focus on the mechanisms through which repression has been promoted, anchored in a resignification of antisemitism that treats it as distinct from racism and accords it a special status in the defense of Western civilization. We use the concept of moral panic to analyze the construction of a narrative that portrays a specific challenge to society’s fundamental values, which has resulted in the scapegoating of the Left and migrants. We also identify the main moral panic entrepreneurs responsible for the creation of this environment both within and outside universities.

Encompassing Authority in Election Campaign Mobilization

Prisca Jöst, Ellen Lust

Full text
Where central state authority is limited, local leaders play a crucial role in political mobilization. But why are some leaders more effective than others? Dominant explanations emphasize leader characteristics, such as social centrality and shared social identity with voters, or individuals’ traits like wealth and gender. We argue that a critical factor has been overlooked: the breadth of leaders’ perceived influence across various domains of individuals’ lives. We introduce and test the concept of encompassing authority – the extent to which individuals perceive a leader as influencing multiple aspects of their lives. Drawing on original survey data from Zambia’s 2021 elections, we examine three types of local mobilizers: religious leaders, chiefs, and local councillors. We show that encompassing authority predicts mobilization in campaigns and community meetings independently of leader and individual characteristics. We identify three mechanisms that explain this effect: belief in the leader’s legitimacy, fear of sanctions for noncompliance, and confidence in the leader’s ability to coordinate collective action. By conceptualizing authority as a dyadic, relational property rather than fixed characteristics of leaders or individuals, our study offers new insights into the underpinnings of political mobilization and opens avenues for future work.

The Right to the Campus: Student Occupations, the University, and the Right to the City

Samantha Puzzi

Full text
While the 2024 Gaza solidarity encampments and campus occupations before them have been the objects of significant debate among academics and broader public audiences, the spatialized and radical democratic dimensions of these protests remain undertheorized. This article argues that turning to Henri Lefebvre provides valuable tools for better understanding campus occupations and answering the questions that universities face today. Grounded in Lefebvre’s comments about students, knowledge production, and campus life following May 1968, I argue that the university is a key site for demanding, clarifying, and expanding the “right to the city.” Extending Lefebvre’s analysis through a discussion of the spatial production activities of contemporary elite North American universities, I propose a novel “right to the campus” defined by radical democratization of university governance and use-driven spatial design. Finally, I draw lessons from the 1968 Columbia University occupations before applying the right to the campus to analyze the successes and disappointments of the more recent wave of encampments. Viewed through this framework, campus occupations posit control over university “property” as a mechanism for challenging the undemocratic management and market logic characterizing both campus spaces and university activities off campus.

Education and Political Attitudes: The Power and Limits of Field Theory

Liesbet Hooghe, Gary Marks, Jonne Kamphorst, Julia Schulte-Cloos

Full text
A growing body of research suggests that a person’s field of education shapes their political worldview. Yet basic questions remain unanswered. How strongly is the content of education associated with political attitudes relative to income, gender, ethnicity, and, most importantly, level of education? Which political orientations are most closely linked to field, and which are not? Using original and public surveys from the United States and Europe, we show that the human-centeredness of a field is strongly related to liberal positions on a wide range of political attitudes. The association with field is comparable to, and sometimes larger than, the association with level of education. However, the theory we propose has clear scope conditions: field of education is not systematically associated with interest in politics, efficacy, participation, or affective party identification.

European Journal of Political Research

The democratic ideal under stress: Citizen commitment to democratic rights and procedures in challenging times

Christian Rauh, Heiko Giebler, Jan Paul Heisig, Johannes Gerschewski, Jianghong Li

Full text
To what extent do citizens support democratic rights and procedures in the face of major societal challenges? Drawing on a Dahlian ideal of democracy, we exploit the context of the Covid-19 pandemic to address this question with two preregistered survey experiments covering 9,000 respondents in six countries (Germany, Hungary, Japan, Poland, South Korea, and Spain). A conjoint experiment on preferences for pandemic policies reveals that citizens strongly oppose restrictions that interfere with their private lives but are largely indifferent to constraints on core democratic rights (elections, rallies, freedom of the press). A framing experiment assessing citizens’ procedural preferences, elicited using best-worst scaling, shows that expert and business input are consistently ranked higher than parliamentary votes or partisan consensus – regardless of whether Covid-19, climate change, or generic ‘societal challenges’ are at stake. We discuss alternative readings of our results and conclude that commitment to democracy does not appear particularly robust when citizens confront concrete crisis-related trade-offs, with largely similar patterns across countries. While our findings diverge from those of some other experimental studies, they are broadly consistent with accounts that point to rising technocratic preferences, concerns about stealth democracy, and risks of gradual democratic erosion rather than abrupt autocratic shifts.

Political power and the global expansion of the value-added tax

Guy Heilbrun

Full text
How does political power shape tax collection? This article addresses this question by focusing on the most prominent fiscal phenomenon of the recent decades – the rise of the value-added tax (VAT). Drawing on insights from the political economy literature, I test the argument that governments with strong legislative support raise more revenues from the VAT as an effective tool to improve public finances in the short term. Results from a global sample of democracies between 1990 and 2020 provide support for this argument. The effect is particularly pronounced among developing countries, where the VAT is often the tax type that accounts for the largest share of public revenues. These findings illustrate the role of domestic political dynamics in shaping the rise of the VAT to its status as a primary revenue source for the modern tax state.

The online transnationalisation of populist radical right publics

Francesco Vittonetto

Full text
International cooperation between radical right populists has escalated over the last decade as parties and leaders worldwide increasingly establish formal and informal connections. Although studies have investigated the role of populist radical right parties (PRRPs) in transnationalisation processes, we know little about the transnationalisation of their online audiences, who are key to amplifying the circulation of PRR narratives across countries. This article therefore addresses the following research question: Which factors explain transnational online engagement with PRR communications? I argue that their digital publics play a role in constructing a transnational network for the PRR, facilitated by online spaces. I hypothesise that engagement varies according to party strategies, platform architectures, and linguistic and geographical factors. To investigate my research question, I analyse YouTube data from 2016 to 2024 of nine PRRPs in eight countries across three continents – the Americas, Europe, and Oceania. Using content and social network analysis, I find transnational engagement is on the rise, that it intensifies around public joint appearances of PRR actors from various countries, and is driven by language and content popularity. These results indicate that transnationalisation is not only catalysed by party elites but is shaped from below and by the architecture of digital platforms amidst a complex interplay of grassroots activity, algorithmic visibility, and party communications.

Occupational labor markets, skills, and immigration concerns: Panel data evidence for Germany

Josep Serrano-Serrat

Full text
Economic crises, technological change, and labor market transformations have long shaped political attitudes, particularly toward immigration. While existing research links occupational risks to anti-immigration preferences, which specific occupations drive this relationship remains underexplored. This research note addresses this gap by examining how occupation-specific skills (OSS) – defined as the amount of skills required to work in a given occupation – moderate the relationship between occupational labor market conditions and immigration attitudes. Drawing on sociological and comparative political economy perspectives, the paper argues that individuals with high levels of OSS are especially sensitive to deterioration in their occupation’s labor market conditions. The central theoretical implication is that adverse changes in occupational labor markets generate stronger political anti-immigration reactions among high-OSS workers, including Managers and (Associate) Professionals. Importantly, OSS are conceptually distinct from other occupational dimensions used in the literature, such as task specificity, and from other skill dimensions, such as general skill. The empirical analysis draws on over 20 years of longitudinal data from the German Socio-Economic Panel (GSOEP), finding evidence consistent with the argument. The findings contribute to ongoing debates about the political behavior of high-skilled workers, a group whose attitudes toward immigration remain contested.

Fair enough? Citizens’ preferences on refugee distribution in the EU

Philipp Lutz, Ben Crum

Full text
How should asylum seekers be distributed across EU member states? And can EU citizens agree on the principles underlying such distribution? While scholars have proposed various fairness principles – such as allocation based on population size, wealth, or past intake – we know little about which principles citizens support. Using an original population survey in six EU member states, this study examines public preferences for distributive fairness in asylum governance. We find widespread dissatisfaction with the status quo of large asymmetries in refugee numbers and broad support for European responsibility sharing based on fair distribution. However, there are significant differences between member states in the principles that citizens support, and these preferences are also affected by the principles that would keep the number of refugees in their own country at a minimum. These findings suggest that while there is a transnational consensus on the need to move to a fairer distribution of refugees in the EU, its practical operationalisation reveals the deeper political divides on the matter.

Public Opinion Quarterly

Reevaluating News and Opinion Dynamics in the Iraq War: New Evidence for Social Identification Effects

Zachary Jablow, Scott L Althaus

Full text
What causes changes in popular support for American wars? Competing theories posit that either the tone and content of wartime news or social identity cues conveyed through mass media should play the larger role. We offer new evidence in this debate by analyzing a novel set of rolling cross-sectional opinion data collected continuously over 13 months during an early phase of the Iraq War and pairing it with data on news intensity and the evaluative tone of news coverage during that same period. Contrary to conventional information updating explanations of war support but consistent with a social identification process, we find that greater intensity of wartime news coverage is associated with increases in aggregate support even after controlling for the evaluative tone of that news coverage.

Political Science Research and Methods

Benchmarking in public updating of economic perceptions

Derek A. Epp, Christopher Wlezien

Full text
How do people respond to new information about social conditions? For example, does an improvement always lead people to say things are “better”? We argue that people update their views differently depending on whether preexisting perceptions are positive or negative, meaning equivalent changes in conditions can elicit different public reactions. To test this possibility, we update a classic model of aggregate public responsiveness to economic conditions by including recent public perceptions as a moderator variable. This reveals a substantial source of heterogeneity: changes in the economy matter either a great deal for public perceptions or not at all, depending on the prior levels of those perceptions. The results imply that updates of public perceptions are benchmarked to the recent past.

From foe to friend? Government–opposition conflict and the appointment of cabinet ministers

Alexander Herzog, David Schmuck

Full text
This paper introduces a dynamic theory of ministerial appointments in coalition governments, arguing that party leaders reward members of parliament (MPs) who fulfilled their expected roles in the prior government–opposition conflict. Using all speeches from the German Bundestag of six decades (1961–2021), we estimate MPs’ expressed positions on the government–opposition conflict dimension and assess their effect on cabinet selection. Contrary to the expectation that party leaders prefer moderate ministers to foster cooperation, we find that more loyal MPs—strong government defenders or fierce critics—are more likely to be appointed. These findings highlight how past parliamentary behavior affects future cabinet formation and suggest that ministerial appointments are shaped by partisan loyalty and role fulfillment.

Political Psychology

Identity threat, personal values, and political attitudes: Evidence from a large‐scale survey across the European Union

Colin R. Kuehnhanss, Mario Scharfbillig

Full text
In political contexts, identities are typically based on shared values. Populist rhetoric often utilizes real and perceived threats to evoke such identities, especially on the far right. Using dedicated items in a survey covering all 27 EU Member States ( N = 27,032), we present evidence on the understudied link between perceived identity threat and values, reflecting foundational motivations of people, as well as downstream political attitudes. At the EU level, personal values related to anxiety avoidance correlate positively with perceived identity threat, whereas growth‐focused values correlate negatively. The positive relationship between identity threat and values focusing on increasing one's self‐efficacy and status is especially pronounced, suggesting compensatory motivational processes. National‐level analysis reveals a more nuanced picture. Perceived identity threat correlates strongly with lower support for liberal policies. This result is relatively homogenous across the EU‐27 and holds when controlling for personal values. Overall, the findings resonate with populist rhetoric focusing on in‐groups and status while attacking liberal policies and universalist values. Conversely, the findings offer hope that policies and communication reducing identity threat may reinforce support for liberal policies and aligned values.

European Political Science Review

“Don’t Know, Don’t Care?” How information and institutional reform affect voting in supranational elections: experimental evidence from the 2024 European Parliamentary elections

Alexandru D. Moise

Full text
European elections consistently show lower turnout compared to national elections. Two types of explanations have been proposed. The first is the “don’t know” explanation: European citizens are not adequately informed about what the EU does, what the role of the European Parliament (EP) is, and what impact their vote can have. The second explanation is that they “don’t care”: voters do not care about European elections because they might think that EU policies do not affect them or that the European Parliament is not an important actor in EU politics. I use a between-subject survey experiment in 16 EU countries, with approximately 30,000 respondents, one month before the 2024 European elections, with a follow-up in the days after the vote, to see whether giving voters more information about what the European Parliament does and presenting respondents with hypothetical increases in the powers of the European Parliament, affects their intention to vote and eventual real-world vote. Results show that information increases likelihood to vote. Importantly, these effects last and increase actual voting propensity a month later. Surprisingly, hypothetical increases in the powers of the EP decrease the likelihood to vote among almost all groups of respondents. Among respondents that are skeptical of the EU or perceive a democratic deficit, this negative effect is more pronounced. Even among pro-EU respondents and among those that prefer to see a stronger role for the Commission, the backlash is strong. When considering increasing the powers of the EP, the European Union has little to gain and much to lose when it comes to civic participation. The EU needs to first improve its legitimacy before considering political reforms.

Politics & Gender

Voting the Same, Feeling Differently: Sister Circles, Black Feminist Epistemologies, and Black Women’s Political Emotions During the Harris Campaign

Nadia E. Brown, Camille Burge-Hicks, Christine M. Slaughter

Full text
Kamala Harris, a first multi-racial Black woman, became the official Democratic presidential nominee on August 5, 2025. This historic achievement only amplified the intensity of discussions about Black women’s politics in the United States. As Black feminist political scientists studying Black women’s politics, we knew we needed to document the nuance, dynamism, and historical nature of Harris’s campaign. We were keenly aware that we needed to capture the narratives of Black women voters to fully make sense of this historic moment. The media narrative that Black women were excited about Harris’s nomination (Jones 2024; Walker 2024) and scholarly findings that Black women voters preferred Black women candidates (Brown et al. 2019; Philpot and Walton, Jr. 2007) seemed superficial. We believed that beneath Black women’s support for Harris lay a particularized set of emotions and wide variation in candidate evaluations, which are underexplored because Black women consistently vote Democratic and are the demographic group most likely to support Black women candidates. These varied emotive responses lead to the same political behavior: a vote for Harris. Our research examines how Black women feel about politics and how these emotions translate into political action.

Atmospheres and Activism: Rethinking Solidarity Politics

Akwugo Emejulu, Leah Bassel

Full text
How and why does left solidarity fail women of color? Given the rising tide of far right extremism, this is an urgent question for social movement actors who wish to build sustainable coalitions for progressive politics. In this paper, we examine the dynamics of solidarity via a novel approach to the study of “atmospheres.” While scholars of emotions have identified the importance of atmospheres in social and political life, they are unable to answer their own important question: What do atmospheres make possible politically? We bring together the concept of racialized solidarity with the sensuous inequalities of activist spaces. We identify visual, sonic, somatic, and emotional inequalities that work together to produce what we name as the racialized atmospherics of activist spaces, in which the mood is systematically mobilized against women of color.

Baby Slump: The Political Consequences of Pregnancy for Candidates

Michelle Irving

Full text
Do women candidate’s parental status, whether childfree, pregnant, or a mother, influence how U.S. voters evaluate their leadership, commitment, and policy expertise at the state executive and legislative levels? Using data from a nationally representative survey experiment, I assess how voters rate these candidates’ competencies. Regardless of partisanship, I find voters reduce support for pregnant candidates, particularly at the executive level, due to concerns about time availability. I also discover a parenthood timing gap effect — while pregnancy is perceived as a limitation to do the job as an elected official, having young children as a mother enhances leadership evaluations, at least among Democrats. A partisan divide emerges, with Democrats rating maternal-cuing candidates higher on leadership competencies compared to Republicans. This research has important implications for how candidates need to strategize their presentation as we see more women and (expecting) mothers run for all types of office.

The Green Gender Gap in Adolescence: A Case Study of Norway

Ingvild Zinober

Full text
Previous research from predominantly Western contexts finds that men consistently show less concern for climate change and the environment than women, a pattern known as the Green Gender Gap. This article investigates whether political gender socialization contributes to this phenomenon. Drawing on two unique data sources from Norway, I find that among adolescents aged 15 to 18, girls are more supportive of the Green Party, consider environmental protection more important, and are more likely to prioritize the environment over oil production and industrial development. I also find that girls express greater concern about climate change than boys from as early as age 13, the earliest age at which data are available. The findings are consistent with the hypothesis that early-life political gender socialization plays a key role in shaping the Green Gender Gap. The gap emerges before structural gender sorting into different educational and occupational trajectories, and while most adolescents still live at home and share the same formative environment. These findings are particularly striking given the Norwegian context, which is characterized by minimal structural gender segregation among adolescents. The findings in this article point to gendered socialization, rather than structural differences between women and men, as one of the driving forces behind the Green Gender Gap.

Party Politics

Divergence or nationalization? Voting across constituencies in four European countries

Valentin Pautonnier, Ruth Dassonneville, Richard Nadeau

Full text
While the nationalization of elections is often viewed as a key characteristic of modern European democracies, the growing body of evidence on urban-rural electoral divergence suggests a reversal of nationalization, due to regional disparities in electoral outcomes. In this paper we study this issue by analyzing constituency-level election results from Denmark, England, France, and Germany over six decades. Using a divergence index, we uncover a trend toward increasing electoral divergence in election outcomes between constituencies in three of the four countries (not in Denmark), particularly since the 2000s. While we find evidence that non-mainstream parties and party system fragmentation contribute to this increase, even the results of mainstream parties have diverged more in recent elections. We also show that rural-urban electoral polarization is an important predictor of divergence which, alongside party system fragmentation and non-mainstream party strength, accounts for much of the temporal trend—suggesting that the growing structuring of party competition along the density gradient is a key driver of de-nationalization. Our findings challenge former analyses suggesting the inevitability of nationalization and underscore the importance of geographical divides in shaping European political landscapes.

Comparative Politics

Hydraulic State: Hydropower Construction and Extension of State Capacity in Contemporary China

Renhao Ye

Full text
This study explores how government-invested infrastructure, especially hydropower construction, increases state capacity at the subnational level. Using a county-level dataset of over 3,400 hydropower stations built in China between 1953 and 2020, this study finds that government-led hydropower construction likely allows a strong central state to transmit and institutionalize its penetrative and extractive capacity in localities. It remains robust to an instrumental-variable approach using geographic slope and river density. A within-case study further shows that ad hoc grassroots administrative institutions created to support construction logistics, resettle citizens, and comprehend local society are often retained after completion and become permanent local authorities. These findings advance state capacity literature by empirically showing that the uneven subnational reach of state can emerge as a durable byproduct of top-down infrastructure projects.

The Sirens of Revolution: Revolutionary Legacies and State Building in Early Communist China

Kevin Wei Luo

Full text
Why do revolutions produce sometimes strong yet unstable states after revolutionary victory? To answer this question, I utilize an original dataset and archival evidence to examine patterns of subnational-level state building in Communist China after 1949. I find that prior revolutionary experiences improved social compliance of state policies, but weakened the building of state institutions and incited public disorder. The key mechanism lies in the entrenchment of social antagonism developed during intense revolutionary struggle, which catalyzed public support for state-led social reform but also planted suspicion of perceived counterrevolutionary forces in both state and society. These findings challenge the notion that revolutions naturally create strong states and contribute to our broader understanding of how social mobilization affects state building processes.

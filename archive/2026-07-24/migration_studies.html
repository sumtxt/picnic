Comparative Migration Studies

Do restrictive policies suppress national identification? A comparative study of muslim and non-muslim immigrant descendants in Scandinavia

Karen Nielsen Breidahl, Rasmus Sandy Harboesgaard Nielsen, Kristian Kriegbaum Jensen, Per Mouritsen

Full text
There is a widespread assumption in many Western European countries that certain immigrant groups and their descendants are reluctant to identify with the host nation. A substantial body of research has documented how factors such as religiosity, perceived discrimination and inter-ethnic contact may serve as determinants of national identification, broadly defined as the emotional and cognitive bond between self and nation. By contrast, we know less about whether restrictiveness in the immigrant policy context - e.g. the extent to which policies limit immigrants’ rights, opportunities, and access to resources - also shapes these bonds. To address this gap in existing knowledge, this study draws on unique comparative survey data collected in Denmark, Norway and Sweden in 2018 covering young adults with a minority background (both Muslims and non-Muslims) as well as a majority control group. Comparing three Scandinavian countries that differ substantially in the restrictiveness of their immigrant policy environments - yet share many structural and cultural characteristics - allows us to examine whether national policy conditions are linked to gaps in national identification between minority and majority groups. The comparative analyses reveal a striking pattern: the restrictiveness of a country’s policy environment does not appear to influence national identification gaps. Thus, Sweden - often portrayed as the least restrictive of the three countries - does not exhibit larger identification gaps than the two other countries. Moreover, the differences between Denmark and Norway are remarkably small.

Population, Space and Place

Diasporic Futures and Transnational Hope Among Iraqis in Sweden and Denmark

Alice Al Maleh

Full text
Based on ethnographic fieldwork among Iraqis in Sweden and Denmark, this paper examines diasporic narratives of hope that surrounded the removal of the Saddam Hussein regime in 2003 as well as the mass protests that swept Iraq in 2019, situating this hope against an overarching narrative of despair, expressed as a simultaneous sense of stuckedness and cyclical repetition. By approaching hope as a diasporic orientation, this paper invites us to think about diasporic subjectivities in terms of affective pulls and proximities in space and time, that shift and intensify as notions of what is possible change radically in the country of origin. Moreover, I suggest, an attentiveness to diasporic narratives of hope as an affective politics broadens our understanding of the collectivities in which diasporic subjects inscribe themselves and how notions of belonging are constructed.

Pathways of Decline: Long‐Term Population Change in Rural Podlasie, Poland

Monika Wesołowska, Adam Czarnecki, Mariusz Dacko, Agata Mróz

Full text
This study examines how combinations of local demographic, economic, infrastructural, and spatial conditions have shaped long‐term population trajectories in rural settlements in Podlasie, a peripheral region of north‐eastern Poland, between 1950 and 2021. Drawing on an adapted Chi–Ventura framework, Classification and Regression Trees (C&RT) were applied to detect patterns indicative of diverging long‐term demographic trajectories. The analysis identifies configurations of conditions associated with population decline, stability, and growth. Population decline was the overwhelmingly dominant trajectory across the study period. Agricultural dependence in 1950 emerged as the decisive determinant: villages where the majority of residents relied on farming were highly likely to depopulate, an effect reinforced by population ageing, poor housing infrastructure, and limited accessibility to large urban centres. Growth occurred only in settlements where a more diversified economic base, younger age profiles, better housing, and stronger urban connectivity were jointly present. The study concludes that long‐term population decline in peripheral rural areas is strongly path‐dependent and rooted in historically inherited economic and demographic structures. Infrastructure improvements may mitigate the decline but are often insufficient on their own to reverse it, highlighting the need for territorially sensitive policies that account for local demographic, economic, and spatial conditions.

Beyond Ageing in Networks: Networked Privilege and Classed Geographies of Ageing Well in Singapore

Arthur Chia, Ad Maulod

Full text
How does social class shape the capacity to age well, and what does this reveal about the structural conditions that ageing policy tends to obscure? Drawing on focus group discussions with 45 higher socio‐economic status older adults in Singapore, this paper develops the concept of networked privilege to examine how accumulated economic, social and cultural capital shapes network formation, aspiration and place‐making in later life. We engage critically with Ho et al.‘s (2024) framework of 'ageing in networks', arguing that its emphasis on older adults’ agency and unbounded geographies rests on what we term the fallacy of universal agency—the mistaken assumption that the capacity to build and sustain meaningful networks is equally available to all older adults regardless of structural position. Through three dimensions of networked privilege—aspirations, friendship and place‐making, we show how advantage is enacted and reproduced by relational configurations that appear to result from individual choice but reflect class‐based positioning shaped by Singapore's meritocratic habitus. We further demonstrate how even well‐resourced older adults face persistent vulnerabilities that networked privilege can defer but never fully resolve, pointing towards the fundamental interdependencies that characterise human ageing across class positions. The paper argues that a genuinely caring approach to ageing policy must move beyond the promotion of individual resilience towards what Tronto (2013) terms 'caring with' that attends to care as a collective, political and redistributive project rather than a matter of individual responsibility or market provisioning.

Unpacking the Differential Impact of Development on Internal Migration Across Educational Groups of Population and the Urban Hierarchy

Dorothee Beckendorff, Wenxiu Du, Mathias Lerch

Full text
This study examines how internal migration responds to development drivers across educational groups in cities of the Global South. While internal migration redistributes human capital, city‐level evidence remains limited. To better understand the urban gradients of migration by education over the human development process, we exploit census data that we aggregate to city populations, enabling a sub‐national examination of urban out‐migration across 13 countries, 313 cities and 29 census years. We apply fixed‐effects Poisson regression models to examine how out‐migration varies across education levels in response to development in the health, education and wealth domains, and how the relationship varies by city sizes. Results show that out‐migration rises with development but is strongly stratified by education. The secondary‐educated are the most responsive group, displaying the steepest increases in migration, particularly at mid‐levels of development and especially with rising wealth. The least educated group remains constrained by limited resources, while university graduates respond more slowly but in a highly selective manner, especially to local educational expansion. Developmental progress increases out‐migration in all city tiers of the urban hierarchy, with secondary cities showing consistently higher out‐migration for the highly educated. These findings nuance classic theories by demonstrating that migration transitions are stratified by education and mediated by city size.

Why Migrants Settle in Small Towns: Polish ‘New Immigrant Destinations’

Anne White

Full text
The article contributes to literature on place attachment, migration transition and migrants' motives for settling in new immigrant destinations (NIDs). It focuses on small towns of under 20,000 population. Such towns are often claimed to be unattractive to migrants. Nonetheless, some migrants do choose smaller towns, even in Central and Eastern Europe, where many countries have only just acquired net immigration. Based on 38 interviews with migrants from different countries in two Polish towns, the article presents migrants' perceptions about the benefits of smallness, particularly the quality of social relations, and the suitability of small locations for people like them. Despite being labour migrants and refugees, many self‐identified as people who chose a small‐town lifestyle, also invoking identity factors such as age and parental status to back up their claims that the towns were just right for people like themselves. Interviewees compared their home locations in the sending and receiving countries, engaging in ‘elastic geography’ to assert and sometimes exaggerate similarities in population size. Place attachment is a process, and the study showed how some participants—particularly middle‐aged parents—increasingly identified with the small town in Poland and actively ‘made it their own’.

International Migration

The Domestic Border: Race, Labour and the Making of Migrant Subordination in Lebanon

Jasmin Lilian Diab

Full text
This paper contends that Lebanon's Kafala system is not a flawed labour framework but a racialised apparatus of statecraft that governs migrant domestic workers (MDWs) through legal dependency, spatial confinement and moral surveillance. Drawing on 50 interviews with Filipino, Ethiopian and Sri Lankan MDWs, and key informants from civil society and international organisations, the study reveals how race, gender and legal status intersect to produce subordination within both domestic and public spaces. The paper argues that the home functions as a domestic border—where employers act as de facto migration authorities and racial hierarchies are naturalised through everyday interactions. Through a critical race and intersectional lens, it explores how nationality operates as a proxy for moral value, labour suitability and sexual respectability, shaping the experiences and perceived legitimacy of MDWs. Yet, the study also highlights forms of everyday resistance, from informal solidarity networks to micro‐acts of refusal and care, which challenge the racialised moral order of Lebanon's migration regime. Ultimately, the paper reframes migration governance as a technology of racialised control and argues that the domestic space is a site where state power is diffused, privatised and enacted—rendering the politics of race, labour and belonging most visible in the intimate.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

The Political Geography of Identity: Local Determinants of Pan-Ethnicity among Asian Americans

Jennifer Wu

Full text
How does one’s community shape their racialized and political identity? Research studying group-based resources and political behavior focuses primarily on the overall size of a group. This study refines our understanding of how community context shapes Asian pan-ethnic identity by examining a second dimension of population: Asian national origin diversity. Using both existing and new survey data, I find that Asians living in more heterogeneous areas view Asian identity as less important and report lower levels of linked fate. I then examine variation in the role of physical and social resources across localities that differ in group size and diversity. Respondents in localities with larger Asian populations and lower levels of diversity are more likely to report higher levels of pan-Asian salience and availability of cultural resources. My analysis highlights the role of within-group diversity and the importance of community resources and institutions in cultivating connections between identity and politics.

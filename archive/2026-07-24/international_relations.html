Norm Making from the Margins: Armed Groups, the Algerian War, and South–South Legal Politics

Boyd van Dijk

Full text
How do armed groups waging asymmetrical wars assert political and legal subjectivity in an international order that historically excluded them? This article recovers the FLN’s groundbreaking legal activism during the Algerian War of Independence, arguing that it staged the most far-reaching challenge of its kind to international humanitarian law’s (IHL) imperial order from below through South–South legal politics. Acting as norm entrepreneurs, FLN jurists contested the existing limits on IHL’s scope and applicability while developing new institutional practices of insurgent normativity that established a disseminable model for other armed groups. In doing so, they put forward a set of legal arguments and practices that helped structure how later armed groups would invoke and substantially develop IHL under conditions of asymmetry. Based on previously inaccessible Algerian, Southern African, and state socialist archival materials, this article deepens existing accounts of normative change by reconstructing the concrete legal practices, institutional strategies, and legal-political thought through which Southern actors challenged IHL and international organizations from the margins of the international order.

Protecting at Home or Providing Abroad: Refugee Policy in Civil Conflicts

Jessica S. Sun

Full text
When civilians flee conflict, concerned donors adopt policies intended to curb both violence and large refugee flows, viewing these objectives as complementary. Can such policies reduce the flow of refugees while safeguarding civilians? Using a model of refugee flight in civil conflict, I find donors who link aid and migration policies may fail to restrain government violence, risking increases in displacement and repression. Specifically, when donors lack leverage or have inconsistent priorities, their goals are undermined by strategic government violence. Policies aimed at reducing migration, like increasing humanitarian aid or reducing access to asylum, can yield more intense fighting because they leave a larger base of support for rebels. Threats to suspend aid similarly do not deter violence when it is clear donors’ primary concern is migration. I find if donors condition assistance on reducing both migration and violence against civilians, but prioritize curbing repression, they can achieve both goals.

Review of International Organizations

Shifting political influence? UN General Assembly behavior during debt crisis

Martin C. Steinwand

Full text
This paper looks at the political influence that China and Western official creditors exert on how debtor countries vote at the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA). The theoretical framework emphasizes the common agency problem that both China and the West face. Starting from the premise that countries that owe more debt to China than the West are more aligned with Chinese positions at the UNGA, we argue that debt crisis changes voting behavior to be more in line with the West. Empirically, we construct a dataset of debt exposure and UNGA voting behavior covering 2000 to 2020 and perform panel analysis with two-way fixed effects. Identification is addressed by arguing that omitted variable bias resulting from political proximity to China likely has a conservative direction and a formal sensitivity analysis. We find that countries with a high debt exposure to China move towards US UNGA policy positions when they default on their sovereign debt obligations, indicating lowered political Chinese influence.

The politics of location and aid effectiveness: Political siting versus political implementation

Christopher Kilby

Full text
This paper uses GODAD data on World Bank-funded project locations and World Bank Independent Evaluation Group project ratings to assess the impact of recipient country domestic politics on the development effectiveness of aid projects. Following recent research on the impact of spatial dimensions of recipient country domestic politics on project location selection (political siting), this paper considers how these same factors impact project implementation (political implementation). I explore how political siting and political implementation impact World Bank-funded investment project outcomes using a sample of projects approved between 1995 and 2020 and evaluated between 1998 and 2024. The analysis uncovers little evidence that political siting impacts project outcomes (likely because results are confounded by politically driven cherry picking) but does identify substantial variation in project performance linked to political implementation. This effect is only apparent when the recipient government has leverage over the World Bank because of its importance to the United States.

Journal of Peace Research

When the river changes course: aid shocks, security partnerships, and regime stability

Andrew Boutton

Full text
Existing research has demonstrated that regimes with access to aid and other forms of support may be better able to forestall challenges to their rule, become more institutionally entrenched, and thus survive longer. Occasionally, however, the donors that provide such assistance withdraw it suddenly. This is often to signal disapproval over some action or to coerce the recipient in some way. Surprisingly, little work has been done toward understanding the effects of these sudden shocks to foreign assistance on the political stability of the recipient country. If one of the primary mechanisms through which external sources of support affect survival is the ability to prevent challenges to the regime, it follows that sudden negative deviations in such support should have significant consequences for regime stability. Aid—especially military aid—is often highly strategic: donors reward with aid those they wish to keep in power, and withdraw it from regimes they wish to punish. Aid shocks can be either deliberate or coincidental. In either case, such shocks may have destabilizing effects, as domestic actors may interpret them as signals that a coup might be welcome. Using an original measure of aid shocks, I find support for this intuition, but only in regimes without a broader security relationship with the U.S. I argue that in these cases, the security relationship deters regime opponents from moving against the government. These findings add to our knowledge of the effects of external revenue and support in regimes, and also underscore the critical role that international signals play in regime survival.

In times of peril: how liberals embrace national defence

Eric Gabo Ekeberg Nilsen

Full text
Earlier literature has found that individuals with more liberal values are less willing to fight for their country in a potential conflict. Because liberal values emphasize self-enhancement and minimize in-group versus out-group distinctions, sacrificing one's life for the nation may appear unnatural. However, these studies have neglected how international conditions shape the attitudes of liberal individuals. In many Western countries, “willingness to fight” has often meant participation in foreign wars rather than defence against direct aggression. This paper argues that while liberal values are negatively correlated with willingness to fight when the state experiences a high degree of security, liberal individuals have a high degree of conditional fight-willingness when confronted with an external threat. A potential conflict will have large consequences for their ability to engage in the goals coming from such values, something that liberal individuals would want to protect. Using a combination of the World Values Survey and a dyadic latent hostility measure, the analysis shows that in threatened countries liberal values exert a positive and substantial effect on individuals' willingness to fight. This conditional effect is strongest in autocratic states, whereas in democracies the threat level must be considerably higher for the same positive relationship to emerge. However, more than 64 country–years in the sample fall into the high-threat category. For small states facing external danger, this dynamic constitutes a crucial component of military capability in an increasingly threatening international system.

The return to compulsory voting: Invalid voting and its representational challenges in Chile’s subnational elections

Gonzalo Contreras, Mauricio Morales Quiroga

Full text
Compulsory voting (CV) reliably raises turnout, but its effects on invalid ballots and the inequalities these produce are less well understood. Chile’s return to CV in 2022 provides a rare within-country test of how the shift from voluntary to compulsory participation reshapes invalid voting and what this produces in the context of shifting inequalities. We analyse the last local elections held under voluntary voting (VV, 2021) and the first under CV (2024) across all 345 municipalities, exploiting concurrent contests held under distinct electoral rules. Our central finding is that CV shifts inequality from participation to vote validity, with rates of invalid voting varying across majoritarian and proportional elections. Specifically: (1) invalid voting increased substantially under CV, though valid votes for parties and candidates also rose; (2) the entry of new voters is associated with a shift from abstention under VV to invalid ballots under CV; (3) ballot size is more strongly associated with invalid voting under CV in proportional than in relative-majority systems; (4) poverty is unrelated to invalid voting under VV but becomes significant under CV, especially in the capital; and (5) rurality is negatively associated with invalid voting under CV but not under VV. These results contribute to debates on whether CV corrects or displaces class bias in participation, on the structural determinants of invalid voting, and on how electoral system type conditions invalid ballot rates under compulsory participation.

Does support for democracy increase losers’ consent?

André Blais, Damien Bol, Carolina Plescia

Full text
Regime legitimacy and survival rely on citizens’ consent to election results, even when these results are unfavorable. Theories posit that individuals with pro-democratic attitudes are more accepting of electoral losses. We test this assumption by analyzing the empirical relationship between support for democratic principles, such as universal suffrage, and electoral consent to randomly generated, hypothetical but plausible electoral outcomes. Using data from a representative sample of Germans, we find a negative correlation between the two variables. Furthermore, those with the strongest democratic convictions are those most influenced in their consent by partisan preferences. Part of the explanation stems from reactions to the far-right, anti-democratic party Alternative for Germany, as many respondents strongly dislike this party and are unwilling to consent to outcomes in which it performs well. However, even among other respondents, democratic support does not correlate positively with losers’ consent.

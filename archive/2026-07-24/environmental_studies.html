Drought amplifies the psychological burden of war

Stefan Döring, Tobias Hecker, Jonathan Hall

Full text
The convergence of climate shocks and armed conflict poses a critical threat to human security and sustainable development. However, their joint impacts on human populations, particularly the erosion of psychosocial resilience, remain poorly quantified. Here, integrating georeferenced drought data from respondents’ pre-displacement regions with survey data from Syrian and Iraqi refugees ( N = 3,751), we find that the association between war exposure and post-traumatic stress is stronger among those from more drought-affected areas of origin. Under severe drought conditions, the increase in post-traumatic stress associated with war exposure was more than twice as large as under average moisture conditions. This pattern is consistent with resilience corrosion, whereby climate-induced hardship may degrade mental health and weaken the adaptive capacities communities need to navigate future shocks. These findings support policy approaches that integrate mental health into climate adaptation and humanitarian response in settings shaped by overlapping crises.

Annual Review of Environment and Resources

Rural Household Cooking Energy Transitions in the Global South: Towards Integrated User-Centered Cooking Energy Systems

Omar Raúl Masera, Victor Ruiz-García, Astrid Schilmann, Montserrat Serrano-Medrano, Luz Angélica de la Sierra-de la Vega

Full text
Approximately 2.2 billion rural people in the Global South cook with solid biomass fuels. Despite decades of promoting the so-called clean fuels, the transition from biomass to these fuels in rural areas remains very limited. High fuel-price-to-income ratios, weak last-mile supply chains, single-fuel program designs, and strong culinary and cultural preferences limit exclusive use and lead to stacking with open fires. Drawing on representative rural household energy initiatives in the Global South, we conclude that systemic, integrated strategies—grounded in local cooking practices, designed to sustain long-term use, and enabling cleaner stacking across fuels and technologies, including improved biomass stoves—are more likely to succeed. Programs should prioritize participatory co-design, context-appropriate technology options, reliable supply chains and financing, and ongoing monitoring to promote sustained use. Ultimately, lasting progress in household cooking depends on combining energy interventions with broader efforts to eradicate extreme poverty and strengthen local livelihoods.

Fast Today, Fair Tomorrow: Competing Priorities Shaping EU Climate Law and Policy

Alessandra Accogli, Jonas Meuleman, Aron Buzogány, Elena Fasoli, Diarmuid Torney

Full text
This review critically assesses academic literature on how EU legal frameworks and institutions have navigated the dual imperatives of urgency (“fast”) and justice (“fair”) in climate governance, since the launch of the European Green Deal (EGD) and during the first term of the von der Leyen Commission. It focuses on core legal instruments and on the roles of key institutions tasked with developing and enforcing climate obligations: the European Commission (EC), the European Parliament (EP), the Council and European Council, the Court of Justice of the EU (CJEU), and national courts. Positioned at the intersection of EU studies and sustainability transition studies, the review bridges legal and political science perspectives. Drawing on urgency and justice as analytical lenses, we identify a recurring temporal mismatch across EU climate governance, where urgency is embedded ex‐ante, whereas fairness tends to be appended ex‐post. This pattern is visible in EU climate laws—which mandate fast action through ambitious targets and accelerated transitions—and institutional dynamics. The EC and Parliament broadly align on urgency imperatives, with justice often reduced to distributive compensation. In contrast, the Council's and the European Council's intergovernmental, consensus‐driven logic emphasizes nationally framed justice, which can temper urgency. National courts strongly engage with urgency but generally defer burden‐sharing decisions to political actors. We argue that embedding justice concerns early in the policymaking process is essential for ensuring that fast and fair transitions reinforce one another. The review concludes with a research agenda to guide future interdisciplinary work on urgency‐justice dynamics amid changing political landscapes. Annual Review of Resource Economics

Economics of Tipping Points

Anne-Sophie Crépin, Therese Lindahl, Aart de Zeeuw

Full text
We provide a critical appraisal of the literature related to the economics of tipping points and regime shifts. Simple modeling examples of pollution control and resource harvesting illustrate the key aspects of the economics of tipping points and regime shifts. We show how economic management should be modified to consider tipping points in deterministic and stochastic settings and how they influence common pool resources and public good provision. This review also presents relevant behavioral experimental designs for tipping point experiments and discusses further in what ways models and empirical work could better speak to each other in future research.

From Basic Needs to Higher Fulfillment: Resource Use and Its Impact on Sustainable Well-Being

Ugur Soytas, Ramazan Sari

Full text
Understanding the association between resource use and well-being is critical for addressing global sustainability challenges. Resources such as energy, water, and minerals are foundational in satisfying human needs, yet their consumption is linked to complex trade-offs across individual and societal well-being. Well-being encompasses multiple facets—hedonic (pleasure-based) and eudaimonic (meaning-based), subjective and objective, and individual and societal—all of which interact differently with resource use. This review synthesizes insights from different disciplines and identifies key patterns, discrepancies, and gaps in the literature. It recommends a holistic framework for examining how resource use can foster sustainable and equitable societal well-being within planetary limits. The review concludes by offering directions for future research and actionable policy and resource management strategies. Thresholds for over- and underconsumption can help policymakers design just policies and prioritize across well-being dimensions.

Global Environmental Politics

Measure-ability: The Paris Agreement’s Transparency Regime and the Making of the Calculating State

Eva Lövbrand, Maria Jernnäs, Aarti Gupta

Full text
Transparency is at the center of the Paris climate regime and one of the key mechanisms by which it seeks to catalyze voluntary climate action. In this article, we analyze the micropolitics of transparency and the power that resides in the seemingly technical and mundane reporting provisions of the Paris Agreement. Informed by Michel Foucault’s decentered account of power and government, we trace the ways of thinking and reasoning that underpin the Paris Agreement’s push to disclose, measure, and evaluate climate performance and how its complex transparency apparatus seeks to arrange things so that states behave as they ought. We identify measure-ability as the governmentality that gives the Paris Agreement’s transparency regime a particular matrix of means and ends. It is a liberal form of climate rule, we argue, that seeks to foster measure-able states that do not need to be governed by others but will govern themselves.

The Show Must Go On? Revisiting the Script of International Climate Cooperation Ten Years After Paris

Florian Steig, Eva Lövbrand, Angela Oels, Jonathan Barnes, Diana Eriksson Lagerqvist, Marie Fischer, Jennifer Gabrys, Aarti Gupta, Maarten Hajer, Phoebe Hamilton Jones, ... (+4 authors)

Full text
This special issue reflects on the state and direction of global climate governance ten years after the adoption of the Paris Agreement. Informed by Michel Foucault’s political thought and the wider field of climate governmentality studies that his work has inspired, we trace the power/knowledge regimes, political rationalities, and many practical tools and techniques that make climate change thinkable and knowable as a political problem. In this introduction, we draw upon the contributions to this special issue to examine how the Paris climate regime reinvents itself to maintain legitimacy amid geopolitical rivalry, rising anti-climate populism, and accelerating climate crises. We identify four practices that work to stabilize this governmental regime by promising future improvements, governmentalizing new frontiers, normalizing overshoot and climate disaster, and funneling climate expertise. We conclude by reflecting on the role and responsibility of global environmental politics scholarship in upholding, resisting, or rewriting this script for international climate cooperation.

The Promissory Governance of Climate Finance

Jonathan Barnes

Full text
Climate finance is central to the politics of the UNFCCC Paris Agreement. Drawing on Beckert’s conceptualization of promissory legitimacy, I argue that the reported results of adaptation finance projects facilitate claims about the future, which consolidate the political authority of the UNFCCC. Drawing on thinking from science and technology studies and the sociology of monitoring, evaluation, and learning (MEL), I explore the knowledge politics of adaptation finance. Specifically, I ask how the Green Climate Fund’s (GCF) adaptation portfolio contributes to the governance of climate change. I demonstrate how the Fund’s results processes incentivize projects to be designed for approval, rather than for implementation. The GCF Secretariat gains credibility and political authority via promissory claims about its results. Theoretically, my analysis clarifies how MEL underlies powerful claims about adaptation, despite ambiguity about the impacts of projects.

Remaking the Amazon? The Emergence of Transterritorial Spaces of Governmentality

M. Cecilia Oliveira

Full text
Since the Paris Agreement, the Amazon has been reimagined not merely as a sovereign territory but as a critical “body” of planetary physiology. This Forum article argues that this shift represents the emergence of the Amazon as a “transterritorial space”—a zone governed by transnational flows of capital, data, and carbon metrics that override local knowledge, modes of life, and conflicts. Drawing on the work of political scientist Edson Passetti and Caribbean scholar Katherine McKittrick, I analyze how the region and its environments are being governmentalized. I propose that current climate governance functions as a rationality that reduces the Amazon to a monoculture of carbon sequestration for the benefit of global elites and capitalist mechanisms in the North and the South. As evidenced by the outcomes of the thirtieth Conference of the Parties, the danger lies in the preservation of structures crucial for the continuity of fossil fuel exploration and the proliferation of market-oriented mechanisms. These mechanisms frequently ignore urgent scientific alerts regarding deforestation, while local knowledge is legitimized only when forced to fit within the dominant rationalities underpinning global climate governance.

The Script of the Science–Policy Interface: Performative Routines in Climate Politics

Jeroen Oomen, Maarten A. Hajer

Full text
The urgency of political action to safeguard against anthropogenic climate change is widely acknowledged. Much of this urgency stems from environmental scientists’ projections that have consistently shown dire future risks. A key part in providing such projections with political legitimacy is institutional “science–policy interface” (SPI) arrangements, which influence the ways in which environmental knowledge is produced and the ways in which political actors respond. In this contribution, building on the scholarship on the interaction between scientific knowledge and policy, we investigate the “script” of the SPI and its implicit rules. We identify seven well-established performative routines that most actors adhere to, often implicitly. Drawing on the literature on the coproduction of knowledge and governmentality, we investigate this script as a technology of governance. On the basis of secondary analysis of academic literature, prior interviews, document analysis, and autoethnographic observations, we show how these routines are upheld and challenged.

More Than an Empty Bucket? The Competing Rationalities in the Operationalization of the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage

Marie Fischer, Angela Oels

Full text
In 2023, the UN climate conference celebrated the successful operationalization of new funding arrangements (FAs), including the new Fund for responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD) from climate change. However, given how little money has been received by the fund, the FRLD has been compared to an “empty bucket.” We use a governmentality analysis based on Mitchell Dean to explore the rationalities that have competed in shaping finance for loss and damage toward specific ends, and how the fund is designed as a result. In total, four competing rationalities have been at play: optimizing, additional voluntary support, scaling finance to needs, and historical responsibility. We show that governing takes place through an empty fund aiming to empower recipients toward self-help and resilience. As questions of historical responsibility and liability have been silenced, the FRLD has been emptied of its radical potential for climate justice.

American Sociological Review

Connective Resource Mobilization: A Relational Approach to Movement Continuity in Transnational Activism

Pamela P. Tsui

Full text
Social movement studies have long centered social movement organizations (SMOs) as the primary vehicles of mobilization. Yet contemporary movements are increasingly decentralized and digitally mediated, challenging SMO-centric frameworks such as resource mobilization theory (RMT). As contention becomes more individualized and non-conventional, organization- and event-centered approaches struggle to explain how movements persist, evolve, or dissolve under shifting conditions. Drawing on RMT and economic sociology, this article advances a relational resource mobilization framework through four propositions: resource mobilization (1) is historically contingent; (2) translates into tactics differently across organizational configurations; (3) is relational; and (4) provides an analytic for examining movement continuity across time and space. Within this framework, I introduce the model of connective resource mobilization to theorize how digitally mediated movements generate strategic capacity through personalized earmarking practices across borders and phases of contention. Based on 179 interviews and observation at 187 events in Hong Kong and Canada, I trace how the Hong Kong Pro-Democracy Movement evolved from its 2019 protest peak through its subsequent downturn across domestic and diasporic contexts. The analysis demonstrates how a relational approach to resource mobilization can illuminate divergent trajectories of movement continuity across historical, geographic, political, and socio-technological contexts.

Sociological Methods & Research

Forging Better Axes: Evaluating and Improving the Reliability of Semantic Dimensions in Word Embeddings

Andrei Boutyline, Elle Johnston

Full text
Word embeddings are a powerful tool for measuring cultural meaning using large text corpora. In sociology, applications commonly estimate the meanings of concepts by projecting keywords onto axes constructed from pairs of opposing anchor keywords. At present, there are no established metrics for assessing axis reliability from embedding data. We investigate three potentially relevant forms of reliability: parallelism between offsets, synonymy between same-endpoint anchors, and antonymy between opposed anchors. Using an original N = 1 , 750 survey of respondents’ ratings of several hundred terms along 36 semantic dimensions plus existing ratings for 3 more, we find that parallelism robustly outperforms the other metrics in predicting the accuracy of 39,000 axes that operationalize these dimensions. Our decomposition of the parallelism–accuracy relationship between and within latent dimensions and anchor sets further shows that, while reliability is robustly related to axis accuracy at all levels, it provides a relatively weak guide for selecting specific keywords. Instead, the primary utility of parallelism may lie at higher levels of methodological decision-making: strongly identifying the most empirically tractable semantic dimension and, secondarily, suggesting the best embedding model for an anchor set.

Insufficient Effort Responding and Adolescent Respondents: Measurement, Extent, and Prediction

Thomas Krause, Susanne Vogl, Christine Sälzer

Full text
This article addresses insufficient effort responding (IER), an issue in survey research affecting data quality. We focus on predicting reactive-check-based IER classifications through nonreactive measures and estimating the proportion of respondents classified as IER in a pupil population, using a sizeable adolescent survey. The analysis highlights IER as a nonmarginal issue that varies considerably by gender, migration status, and school type. Utilizing Random Forest models, we evaluate nonreactive measures’ predictive power for reactive-check-based IER classifications, notably response time, intraindividual response variability, and Mahalanobis Distance. The findings show these measures’ future research value, emphasizing the strong influence of response time. We also explore the relationship between predictors and our target variable and find that shorter response times and less response variability correspond to a greater likelihood of IER. This study illustrates the potential of nonreactive measures and advanced machine learning techniques for identifying IER and highlights the necessity for further research.

Do Respondents’ Commitments Improve Data Quality in Opt-in Online Surveys? An International Experiment on Participants’ Attentiveness, Cheating, Satisficing, and Skipping

Farsan Ghassim

Full text
Online surveys are increasingly the norm in public opinion research. Self-administered internet surveys present opportunities, but also specific risks for data quality. In search of solutions, scholars are returning to commitment as one way of encouraging participants to provide accurate responses. This article provides an extensive study of commitment as a potential method for improving data quality in the context of opt-in online surveys. Theoretically, I argue that the effectiveness of commitment in motivating good conduct depends on respondents’ material and immaterial cost-benefit calculations. In an experimental survey in 2021 including more than 32,000 respondents from the general populations of 10 countries worldwide, I find that commitments make participants less likely to cheat on knowledge questions, but are ineffective or even counterproductive with respect to respondents’ attentiveness, satisficing, and skipping. Researchers should thus use commitments carefully and selectively, particularly in the context of opt-in online surveys.

Sociological Theory

What Counts as a Stronger Cause? Six Meanings of Causal Importance in the Social Sciences

Sebastian Kohl

Full text
Philosophers have analyzed the meaning of binary causal statements (“A causes B”), yet many disputes in the social sciences concern a different question: the relative weight of causes, or what it means to claim that “A is more important than B in bringing about C.” This article argues that the ambiguity behind this claim underlies many stable disagreements between different schools of social scientists. Building on the philosophy of the social sciences and key examples from social-science practice, I distinguish six distinct meanings of causal strength: associational, contributional, difference-making, replacement, stability, and specificity. These meanings frequently yield conflicting verdicts about which cause is stronger. Moreover, each meaning is associated with a theory of causation, a family of methods, a characteristic vocabulary, and a metaphorical register. The article concludes with a plea for “causal literacy”: Making these meanings explicit will not dissolve all disagreement, but it may help separate true empirical from verbal disputes.

Enduring links: mental health effects of ex-partner’s life events among separated parents

Philipp Dierker, Mine Kühn, Sanna Kailaheimo-Björkqvist, Mikko Myrskylä

Full text
Union dissolutions between parents do not necessarily end interdependencies between them. This study examines whether life events of ex-partners with a shared child affect each other’s mental health, extending life course theory on linked lives beyond intact families. Using Finnish population register data from 1987 to 2020 and panel data methods, including two-way fixed effects and dynamic difference-in-differences models, the analysis follows separated parents of minor children and tracks psychotropic medication purchases as an indicator of mental health. The findings reveal that ex-partners’ stepfamily formation events, like re-partnering and childbirth, are followed by modest improvements in mental health, whereas adverse health events experienced by the ex-partner, including cancer diagnosis and death, lead to declines in mental health, particularly among mothers. More distant life events, like the ex-partner’s parent’s death, show no consistent associations. The results demonstrate that parents’ lives remain interdependent after union dissolution, thereby extending the linked lives concept within the life course framework to encompass post-separation family ties.

A Prophet Not Accepted in His Own Time: Hyman Minsky, the Disruption of the Postwar Growth Model by Financial Innovation, and the Vision of a Truly Wage-Led Economy

Pierre-Christian Fink

Full text
Since the financial crisis of 2007–9, the economics of Hyman Minsky has inspired calls to reduce financial instability so as to rebalance the current US growth model or even to replace that growth model with a new one that would distribute prosperity more evenly. Why did Minsky's economics fail to achieve even a modicum of such uptake during his lifetime? This article conducts the first systematic analysis of Minsky's archived correspondence to study his little-known but sustained efforts to influence policymakers. In the late 1960s, Minsky collaborated with the Federal Reserve to begin collecting data on the innovation that he considered the greatest source of financial instability, the money market. Yet Fed officials grew concerned that the project would be opposed by banks. They terminated the collaboration when Minsky said in a speech before the Board of Governors that there was no way of rebalancing the extant growth model that would be acceptable to its core constituencies. In the 1970s, Minsky developed a blueprint for a new growth model that he pitched to congressional Democrats. It centered on regulating the money market and shifting the backbone of employment and aggregate demand from private investment to a public job guarantee. The more Minsky rose to the challenge of explaining the economics of his proposal through narrow-bandwidth channels such as newspaper articles and congressional hearings, the more Democrats grew concerned about its politics. The article demonstrates that bringing growth model analysis into dialogue with the sociology and history of economics can be mutually beneficial.

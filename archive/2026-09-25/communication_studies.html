Rejection of cross-cutting news depends on content, sources, and cultural contexts: evidence from online trace data

Wonjeong Jo, Magdalena Wojcieszak

Full text
We examine the conditions in which people reject cross-cutting news in naturalistic settings of online comments. Theoretically, we account for article content and source to test whether commenters’ rejection and its intensity depend on whether cross-cutting news (a) is hostile to their in-group or favorable to the out-group and (b) is “unexpected” (from in-group sources) or “expected” (from out-group sources). Methodologically, we use online traces from news aggregators in South Korea (SK; 7,607 comments) and the United States (116,308 comments). We find that most commenters (79% in SK and 64% in the United States) reject cross-cutting news, with left-leaning commenters expressing stronger rejection than right-leaning commenters. Regarding article content, the SK commenters more strongly rejected in-group hostility, while the U.S. commenters rejected out-group favoritism more. Regarding article source, SK commenters more strongly rejected unexpected cross-cutting news from in-group sources than expected cross-cutting news from out-group sources. We discuss the implications of these findings.

International Journal of Press-Politics

When Movement Widens the Gap: The Contingent Effect of the Chinese #MeToo Movement on Feminist Attitudes

Shuning Lu, Haixin Mu, Tian Yang

Full text
Social movements are often associated with various cultural outcomes, including shifts in public opinion regarding movement-related attitudes and ideologies. This study examines the shift of feminist attitudes among the public following the Chinese #MeToo movement. We develop a sociotechnical contingency framework that emphasizes how identity-based movement relevance and access to information channels shape the shifts in public opinion following social movements. Using nationally representative data from the Chinese General Social Survey (2010–2023), we find that the #MeToo movement was significantly associated with an increase in pro-feminist attitudes. These changes were unevenly distributed across groups, revealing widening attitudinal gaps along gender identities and digital divides. Our findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of how contemporary digitally mediated movements catalyze cultural change and underscore the need to contextualize their impact as it interacts with structural dynamics.

When Past Hurt Meets Present Care: How Family History Shapes Adult Caregivers’ Communal Coping With Siblings in Their Caregiving for Parents With Health Challenges

Abdullah S. Salehuddin, Andy J. Merolla, Tamara D. Afifi

Full text
This sequential, explanatory mixed-methods study explores how adult caregivers’ perceived family functioning and unresolved hurt toward siblings from childhood are associated with their present-day communal coping (CC) with their siblings and caregiving outcomes. Study 1 tested a hypothesized moderated mediation model using survey data ( N = 776). The results indicate greater childhood family functioning (consistent with hypotheses) and unresolved hurt (counter to hypotheses) were associated with more CC with siblings. CC, in turn, was associated with less flourishing and greater caregiver burden and sibling resentment. Considering the unexpected positive association between unresolved hurt and CC, Study 2 involved semi-structured interviews with adult caregivers ( N = 30) about their experiences enacting CC with siblings despite residual hurt. The findings suggest unresolved childhood hurt toward siblings often stem from challenging experiences, including neglect and abuse. In addition to impeding sibling CC, unresolved hurt can also complicate efforts toward CC (or render them dysfunctional). This study underscores how childhood experiences have an enduring effect on CC with siblings decades later, especially if past family issues are left unresolved.

Democratic Media Performance and Democratization: A Global Longitudinal Perspective

Florian Arendt

Full text
Media that effectively perform their democratic functions are widely considered a key driver of democratization, yet longitudinal evidence on their impact remains limited. This study analyzed global annual time-series data on democratic media performance and democratization from 1946 to 2018. ARIMA models indicated a covariation between annual increases in democratic media performance and gains in democratization worldwide, measured by Vanhanen’s Index of Democratization. The analysis also revealed covariation with V-Dem’s liberal, participatory, deliberative, and egalitarian component indices of democratic quality. Evidence on temporal ordering, consistent with Granger causality, was found for Vanhanen’s index and the participatory and deliberative V-Dem component indices; evidence was marginal for the liberal component and absent for the egalitarian component. Acknowledging limitations regarding causal interpretation, the results are consistent with the claim that media can act as a leading force in democratic development rather than merely reflecting it, particularly in electoral, participatory, and deliberative dimensions.

Policy and Internet

The End of the Observable Infodemic: Privatised Observability and Public Health Monitoring

Christoph Dockweiler

Full text
Infodemiology and infodemic management rest on a premise so foundational that it is rarely stated: health information behaviour leaves traces that researchers and public institutions can observe. This paper argues that the premise is dissolving. Health information seeking is migrating from publicly observable websites and platforms into conversational artificial intelligence, where exchanges unfold in private dyads between user and system and are visible in full only to the providers operating them. The paper develops a typology of three observability regimes, termed open, brokered and enclosed observability, which differ in trace custody, conditions of third‐party observation, attainable granularity, stability of access and the epistemic position of public health. It traces the arc from the open web through the platform era, whose brokered observability collapsed in the post‐API age, to the enclosed regime of conversational agents, in which knowledge about population health information behaviour is produced and selectively disclosed by commercial providers. The analysis derives implications for infodemic management, for the evaluation of health information environments and for regulation, and argues that privacy‐preserving research access to conversational health data should be established as public health infrastructure.

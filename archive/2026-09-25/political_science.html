American Political Science Review

Imperial Aid and Tax Mobilization in Late Colonial Africa

DIDAC QUERALT

Full text
In 1929, the British Empire launched a four-decade development initiative to modernize colonial economies without self-government. Known as the Colonial Development and Welfare Fund, the program required colonial administrations to raise local taxes to cofinance development projects. This article shows that imperial aid expanded colonial taxation only after organizational reforms in the Colonial Service screened in officials who shared the metropole’s developmental agenda and made tax mobilization incentive-compatible with career advancement. Drawing on original data from 12 African colonies and extensive archival evidence, I show that aid increased tax revenues when patronage-based governors were replaced by career officers and promotion within the Colonial Service became conditional on fiscal performance. These findings highlight how bureaucratic incentives shaped the effectiveness of imperial aid and offer a new explanation for the expansion of fiscal capacity in late colonial Africa.

The Punitive Dimension of Presidential Particularism

LUKAS K. ALEXANDER

Full text
Existing accounts of presidential particularism emphasize the targeted distribution of benefits to electorally valuable constituencies. This research note demonstrates that presidential particularism can also operate punitively, through the selective imposition of costs on constituencies that provided weaker electoral support for the president. I examine this dynamic through the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), a highly centralized and politicized agency created at the start of Donald Trump’s second term. Leveraging data on nearly 700,000 federal contracts linked to DOGE contract cuts and 2024 county-level election returns, I show that these cuts were systematically concentrated in counties that provided weaker electoral support for Trump. These patterns hold across contract- and county-level analyses. Together, these findings broaden theories of presidential particularism by showing that presidents may use administrative authority to impose costs on electorally oppositional constituencies. More broadly, I demonstrate how presidents can wield administrative power as a tool of partisan punishment.

American Journal of Political Science

Breadwinner backlash: The gendered effects of industrial decline

Richard Clark, Roza Khoban, Noah Zucker

Full text
Low‐skilled men have long been vulnerable to globalization, decarbonization, and other forms of economic disruption. When shocks are realized, women often increase their paid labor to compensate for men's loss of income. We study how layoffs of low‐skilled men and concurrent shifts in workforce composition toward women affect voting. We theorize that this labor market rebalancing produces “nostalgic” coalitions of men and women that seek a return to male‐breadwinner family models. Such attitudes fuel right‐wing movements that pledge to protect conservative gender roles. Evidence from local labor markets and voting in the United States over the last two decades, as well as a longitudinal study tracking individual Americans over several decades, support this theory. These findings contribute a new gender‐based account of the globalization backlash.

Comparative Political Studies

Listening to Leaders: Illiberal Speech as a Symptom of Democratic Decline

Seraphine F. Maerz, Dean Schafer, Carsten Q. Schneider

Full text
Political leaders signal their commitment to democratic or authoritarian values through public speeches. Such signals can constitute a breach of democratic norms and indicate intent to undermine democratic institutions. By listening to what leaders say, we are able to capture a hitherto overlooked sign of a crisis of democracy. We develop indicators for illiberal speech that capture the language by leading representatives of political regimes across the globe. Utilizing a machine learning approach, we fine-tune DeBERTa models to score 27,683 speeches by 452 political leaders representing over 400 administrations in 123 countries between 1989 and 2024, yielding over 400,000 semantically coherent paragraphs. The scores place leaders on an illiberal-liberal scale and have been validated with openai’s gpt-5.2 and human coders. We find that illiberal public discourse is part of the multi-dimensional symptom of democratic decline. Our analysis opens up a new research agenda on the role of public rhetoric in the study of political regimes and their transformation.

British Journal of Political Science

Selection, Stability, and Shock: Political Attitudes of Chinese Students at Home and Abroad

Yingjie Fan, Jennifer Pan, Yiqing Xu

Full text
Theories of democratic diffusion predict that exposure to liberal democracies fosters support for political liberalism, while recent studies on Chinese overseas students suggest studying abroad generates backlash against liberal values. Using a unique four-year longitudinal dataset of nearly 1,000 Chinese college students in China and the United States, we examine how studying abroad shapes students’ ideology, attitudes, and identity. Our findings challenge both perspectives. Students who study in the United States are already more politically liberal, market-oriented, and cosmopolitan, and less nationalistic than their peers upon departure, underscoring the importance of selection. Ideological preferences remain remarkably stable throughout college, regardless of location. However, support for China’s political system and affective identification with Chinese national identity declines among students in both countries from 2022 to 2023, with a greater decline among students in the United States. These results show that selection, home-country shocks, and host-country context jointly shape overseas students’ political attitudes.

Religious Cross-Pressures and Electoral Decision Making

Yara Sleiman

Full text
For many Americans, religious teachings are central to political identity, yet these teachings often do not align neatly with party platforms. How do voters decide when their faith crosses partisan lines? I examine this question by focusing on Catholic and Muslim Americans, whose moral and social world-views span both sides of the partisan divide. Using a novel ‘dynamic’ choice experiment, I show that religious cross-pressures shape how voters arrive at their choices. Contrary to classic accounts linking cross-pressures to disengagement, I find no evidence of increased decision avoidance; instead, voters remain engaged but simplify their choices. Cross-pressure salience is linked to greater decision difficulty, reduced information search, and greater reliance on issue prioritisation. These findings challenge the view that attitudinal conflict improves the quality of democratic participation, suggesting instead that cross-pressures constrain deliberation and may weaken the representational signal voters send to parties.

Ideas at Work: Educated Colleagues Shape Political Attitudes

Hilma Lindskog

Full text
Workplaces are increasingly sorted by education. How does the educational composition at the workplace shape political attitudes? To address this question, I use Swedish population-wide administrative data combined with a representative election survey – an empirical approach that links respondents to their colleagues. The central finding is that the share of university educated at the workplace influences individuals’ attitudes independently of the individual’s own educational background. Further analysis indicates that the effect is partly caused during the time spent in the labor market and varies with the educational field composition of the workplace. The results shed light on how social connections at work, as opposed to personal experiences, shape political attitudes. This has far-reaching implications for our understanding of the politically relevant aspects of the work life, as well as for the formation of the education divide.

Perspectives on Politics

Positional Blurring in Context: How Dimensionality and Ideology Constrain Strategy

Nicolás de la Cerda, Jacob R. Gunderson

Full text
Positional blurring is a common and effective party strategy. It enables parties to downplay some issues while highlighting and clarifying their profile on others. However, we know little about how parties use this strategy outside European party systems, in which (right-wing) populist parties are particularly noteworthy for their blurred economic positions and clear cultural positions. Is that strategy inherent to populism or a reflection of context-specific incentives? This article uses data from Europe and Latin America to argue that populists tailor their strategies to the dimensionality of their party system and populism’s host ideology. We find that multidimensional party systems and exclusionary thick ideologies drive populists to blur their economic positions. Extending this research field beyond Europe for the first time, these findings show that populists are heterogeneous actors whose varied thick ideologies embedded in diverse political systems systematically constrain their strategic behavior.

The Democracy–Decarbonization Dilemma: Can Climate Assemblies Provide an Escape Route?

Fergus Green, Michele Zadra

Full text
Meeting the goals of the Paris Agreement on climate change demands rapid and systemic economic transformation. Yet, contemporary democracies seem tragically ill-suited to this task. Proposals to insulate climate policy making from democratic control—for instance, through delegation to expert bodies—seem likely to fuel the rise of authoritarian populists, which is already eroding democratic norms and institutions. Deepening democratic engagement with the process of decarbonization may provide an escape route from this apparent “democracy–decarbonization dilemma.” In this vein, there has recently been much enthusiasm for climate assemblies. Such processes can have profound effects on their participants. But can they influence attitudes to democracy and support for ambitious climate policy among the wider public? We conduct a narrative review of the theoretical and empirical literature on democratic mini-publics (DMPs) to answer this question and critically reflect on our findings. We find that DMPs can influence the wider public’s attitudes toward democratic institutions and public policy to some extent. Yet, DMPs alone—even if well publicized—seem unlikely to be sufficient to stimulate the widespread shifts in knowledge, capacities, attitudes and behavior necessary to escape the democracy–decarbonization dilemma. We propose expanding the use, institutionalization, and orientation of DMPs—so that they become a widely recognized and routinized feature of deliberative systems, interacting with both governments and the wider citizenry—while advancing complementary democratic reform initiatives.

Competitive Authoritarianism In America

Steven Levitsky, Lucan A. Way

Full text
Abstract: The United States crossed the line into competitive authoritarianism during Donald Trump's second presidency: Elections remained real, but incumbents systematically abused state institutions to tilt the playing field against opponents. The administration weaponized the Justice Department, the FBI, and other agencies against critics, universities, law firms, and the press, while national gerrymandering and coerced corporate money skewed competition. Judged by the eleven criteria the authors devised to score 35 regimes in their earlier research, the U.S. case is comparable to Hungary under Viktor Orbán, if far softer than 1990s Russia or Serbia. The new regime is unlikely to consolidate, however: The United States’ vast countervailing power—its wealth, private sector, and civil society—dilutes authoritarian abuse and makes redemocratization by electoral turnover likely. Yet turnover would not mean democracy survived intact. Opposition parties do win unfair elections, and a restored U.S. democracy will remain unconsolidated until both major parties commit to its defense.

Does Nonviolent Action Still Work?

Breza Race Maksimovic, Jamila Raqib

Full text
Abstract: From Burma to Belarus, one question keeps surfacing: Does nonviolent action still work? The authors argue that the theory hasn’t failed, but rather that the field has fallen behind. Authoritarian regimes have modernized repression while movements have reduced strategic nonviolent struggle to street protest, mistaking visibility for leverage. That gap helps explain why organizers facing lethal repression have turned to arms, even though nonviolent campaigns still outperform violent ones. The authors also challenge how success gets measured: Leader-centric, single-cycle standards obscure real gains. What the field needs now isn’t new doctrine but investment in countering smart repression, rebuilding noncooperation, and sharing lessons across movements.

The Real Liberation Technology

Rabah Arezki

Full text
Abstract: Larry Diamond’s “liberation technology”—the internet and social media—has not freed closed regimes: Autocrats own the off switch and weaponize it. The real liberation technology is the democratization of finance. Unlike social media, digital payments are incentive-compatible: They enlarge the economy autocrats live off, so rulers promote them even while blocking the internet. Yet they erode authoritarian rents and create a new digital bourgeoisie whose demand for the rule of law—when it is a challenger, not an incumbent—drives political change. Grounded in evidence from Findex, mobile money, and tax revolts, the argument reframes how outsiders can support democratic evolution.

Authoritarian AI’s Last-Mile Problem

Jie (Jason) Lian

Full text
Abstract: Is artificial intelligence the autocrats’ silver bullet? No. AI-empowered autocrats are constrained by the “last-mile problem”: AI can help regimes to see, sort, and predict, but human police officers, soldiers, and local officials remain indispensable for physical coercion. In the long run, human agents will stay relevant: They not only guarantee an effective repressive system but also signal the autocrats’ popularity. For prodemocracy actors, this suggests that the conventional wisdom on combating autocracies, such as encouraging defections among security forces, still holds. The bottom line: Repression in the AI era remains a human affair.

Will AI Surveillance End Bureaucratic Autonomy?

Maria Nagawa

Full text
Abstract: Civil servants are a neglected source of democratic resilience. Their partial autonomy lets them resist abuses of state power. This resistance is possible because politicians cannot perfectly observe bureaucrats’ preferences or behavior. Artificial intelligence is closing this gap. States are collecting more data on their own officials, and AI tools can merge and analyze extensive records that once sat in separate systems. Illiberal leaders can now identify and punish bureaucrats they consider disloyal at scale. Drawing on cases from democracies and authoritarian regimes, this article argues that the legal architecture protecting bureaucratic autonomy must be updated for the data age.

The Age of Authoritarian Abundance

Senem Aydın-Düzgit, Berk Esen, Sebnem Gumuscu

Full text
Abstract: This essay argues that the post-unipolar international order, combined with unprecedented interdependence, has created an “age of authoritarian abundance.” As the relative power of the United States and Europe declines and democratic erosion spreads within the West, Western governments increasingly repurpose their influence toward transactional partnerships with illiberal actors. At the same time, autocrats can draw political, financial, technological, and diplomatic support from a diverse array of great powers, authoritarian middle powers, international institutions, and private firms, reducing dependence on any single patron and exploiting asymmetries in areas such as migration, energy, and digital infrastructure. These mutually reinforcing trends expand the resources available for autocratic survival while leaving prodemocracy forces with little external support. Democracy’s prospects are not foreclosed, but in a fragmented order without a major power committed to promoting democratic norms, they depend on many actors whose interests rarely align with democratic ends.

Why Welfare States Save Democracy

Joseph Wong

Full text
Abstract: Rising inequality threatens democratic durability, as societies become increasingly polarized. South Korea and Taiwan have resisted global trends in democratic backsliding by continually expanding their social welfare regimes. Democratic resilience and welfare deepening have been mutually reinforcing in South Korea and Taiwan. Democratization during the 1990s created the political conditions and incentives to deepen the government’s social policy commitments. Over the past three decades, the continued expansion of the social safety nets in South Korea and Taiwan has mitigated the effects of growing inequality and deepened citizens’ trust in democratic institutions and practices.

Polarization Is Not the Problem

Lilliana Mason

Full text
Abstract: Partisan polarization is not the greatest threat to U.S. democracy, but a symptom of a deeper struggle over who belongs and deserves equal rights. Rapid gains by racial minorities, women, and LGBTQ citizens have produced an ethnonationalist backlash whose adherents now wield disproportionate power through the Republican Party. This conflict has intensified partisan animosity and moral disengagement, but a “both sides” diagnosis obscures the asymmetry between a faction seeking to reverse social progress and those resisting it. Polarization is therefore less the disease than democracy’s immune response to an unpopular, antidemocratic movement. Suppressing that response in the name of civility would only strengthen the movement and imperil pluralistic self-government. The United States can still become its most inclusive democracy, but only if leaders defend equal rights and voters hold accountable those who will not.

Taiwan’s Deadlocked Democracy

Kharis Templeman

Full text
Abstract: In May 2024, Taiwan's President Lai Ching-te took office while facing off against an opposition party majority in the legislature. Since then, the island has been caught in a slowly escalating constitutional crisis. The Constitutional Court is now paralyzed, key regulatory bodies sit vacant, the annual budget bill is a half-year late, and the premier has refused to countersign legislation. This political dysfunction has been blamed on nationalist polarization, PRC influence, or reckless and irresponsible opposition parties, but these explanations overlook a simpler cause: Taiwan’s institutions are simply not well-designed to handle divided government. The political system lacks adequate safeguards to protect the independence of accountability institutions. Unified government masked this design flaw for many years, but it now threatens Taiwan’s democracy.

How to Build the Right Coalition

Rachel Kleinfeld

Full text
Abstract: In competitive authoritarian and autocratic contexts, political parties win elections more frequently by running as public unity coalitions. However, in eroding democracies, voters are often highly polarized. The incumbent often won because the main opposition party was disliked by a plurality or majority of voters. Joining with such a tainted party can harm candidates’ chances. Where elected leaders are eroding once-strong democracies, opposition parties often do better with quiet coalitions in which parties remain fully independent but work together; choosing not to run against one another, or competing electorally to offer voters’ choice, but promising to caucus together post-election.

How to Protect the Media in Eroding Democracies

Maya Tudor

Full text
Abstract: Independent media is not merely one institution among those under attack in eroding democracies; it is the connective tissue linking the electoral, institutional, and popular forms of resistance on which democratic survival depends. Yet the terrain has shifted: A media ecosystem once defined by scarcity and dispersed ownership is now fractured, networked, and funneled through a few platforms that capture most digital-advertising revenue. Comparing democratic decline in Hungary, India, Poland, and Sri Lanka, the author finds that erosion ran deepest where governments dominated digital-media ecosystems—India's ruling party built a propaganda “machine” on WhatsApp, while Hungary's never achieved comparable dominance online, leaving room for Péter Magyar's challenge. Five lessons follow: win the battle for digital distribution, build networked media ecosystems before they are needed, diversify media-financing models, develop legal-defense infrastructures, and connect institutional erosion to citizens' everyday concerns. The question is no longer whether journalism survives, but whether independent media can still help citizens discover common interests and act on them.

How Courts Survive Democratic Backsliding

Andrew O’Donohue

Full text
Abstract: When elected leaders attack the courts, why does judicial independence sometimes survive? This essay argues that courts rarely prevent executive power grabs on their own. Rather, judges depend on judicial allies—especially legislators, civil society groups, and international actors—who empower courts to resist executive aggrandizement. By analyzing cross-national evidence as well as case studies of Brazil, Israel, and Poland, the author finds that judicial independence is most resilient when opposition parties retain power in the legislature. Yet even when illiberal governments control the legislature, protests and international actors can still protect the courts. The case studies illustrate that mobilization by judicial allies often generates positive feedback effects, as mobilization empowers judges, whose decisions energize further resistance. At a time when courts are under attack in the United States and globally, judicial allies must not only defend courts but also, in the longer term, reform them.

How to Break an Autocrat’s Coalition

Laura Gamboa

Full text
Abstract: Authoritarian coalitions look unbreakable, but they are not: Allies defect when the costs of loyalty rise and the costs of defection fall, and opposition parties and civil society can engineer both. Moderate institutional strategies—contesting elections, courts, and legislatures—force stealth autocrats to abandon their democratic façade, as in Guatemala, where Bernardo Arévalo's Semilla party stayed on the 2023 ballot and pushed the elite alliance known as the “Corrupt Pact” into overt manipulation that split it. Persistent journalism makes loyalty reputationally costly, as years of corruption reporting in Hungary did before Péter Magyar broke with Viktor Orbán. Depolarization lowers the price of defecting, as when Colombia's opposition refused Alvaro Uribe's provocations, or when Indiana activists sent thank-you notes to Republicans resisting mid-decade redistricting. Such wins are partial—Uribe's chosen successor still won—but defections that build “broad thin coalitions” across ideological lines do shift power, and marginal gains are worth the effort.

How Protest Can Halt Backsliding

Saskia Brechenmacher

Full text
Abstract: Mass protest can halt democratic backsliding, but only when it shifts political power away from autocratizing incumbents before they become entrenched. Comparing Brazil, Georgia, Poland, Serbia, and South Korea, this essay identifies three pathways through which protest can make that shift: Empowering institutional actors to curb executive overreach, mobilizing previously disengaged citizens to vote, and eroding support for incumbents among voters and elites. Success depends less on the sheer size of demonstrations than on whether organizers reach beyond the existing opposition, counter polarizing government narratives, and connect street mobilization to effective parties, legislators, and electoral strategies. Where opposition parties are weak, institutions captured, and repression advanced, even sustained mass mobilization may not yield an immediate breakthrough. Protest should therefore be treated as one tool in a broader democracy-defense strategy—one that can slow authoritarian consolidation, build civic capacity, and prepare future electoral or institutional openings.

European Journal of Political Research

Conditional gender gaps in welfare state support: The role of labor market and household inequality

Kaitlin Alper, David Attewell, Andreas Jozwiak

Full text
Why are women more supportive of the welfare state than men? Existing literature on this attitudinal gender gap focuses mainly on how men and women, on average, experience varied risks in the labor market. Feminist scholarship argues that the welfare state has the potential to equalize within-household bargaining power between men and women, but has not fully explored the political implications of contemporary inequality dynamics within low- vs. high-income households. We theorize that the gender gap in welfare state support is shaped by the interplay between households’ position on the income distribution and women’s relative incomes within households. Using Luxembourg Income Study (LIS) data from 1963 to 2024, we first find that, within households, women’s income relative to male partners falls systematically moving up the income distribution. Next, our analyses of 2016 International Social Survey Programme data show that gender gaps in welfare state support are narrow and statistically uncertain among lower-income households, where need is high. However, in higher-income households, gender gaps emerge and grow moving up the household income distribution. Furthermore, attitudinal gaps in higher-income households are sensitive to inequalities in relative income – large where male incomes predominate and narrow or nonexistent in households where women contribute half or more of income. Gender inequality and its political consequences are thus intertwined with broader structures of income inequality.

Quarterly Journal of Political Science

The primary premium: Why legislators are incentivized to side with primary voters over general election voters

Sarah E. Anderson, Daniel M. Butler, Laurel Harbridge-Yong

Full text
How are legislators incentivized to behave when their primary and general electorates disagree about an issue? We argue that primary electorates are generally more unified in their issue preferences and more sensitive to roll-call positions than general electorates, incentivizing legislators to prioritize primary voters when facing conflicting demands from the two groups. Using the 2016 Cooperative Election Study and an original survey experiment with over 13,000 respondents, we show that primary voters are both more unified and more likely to adjust their vote choice based on policy alignment. When we combine electorate unity and voter sensitivity, we find that US senators had more to gain by siding with their primary electorate in 84% of the votes where they were cross-pressured. This helps explain why politicians are incentivized to cater to their primary electorate − shaping patterns of representation and policy outcomes in American politics.

Political Science Research and Methods

Religiosity and local debt repayment: evidence from Italian mayors

Luca Vitale

Full text
Does a politician’s religion influence their policymaking? While existing research has examined how gender, class, and ethnicity shape political decisions, the role of religiosity remains understudied. This paper investigates how religious values influence debt repayment decisions among local politicians. I develop a novel measure of individual religiosity, based on names matching local patron saints, and apply it to 62,908 candidates in Italian municipal elections. Using a regression discontinuity design, I find that narrowly elected religious Catholic mayors are more likely to increase debt repayment. This effect likely reflects two mechanisms: Catholic moral teachings, which stress the obligation to repay debt, and risk aversion rooted in religion. Overall, these findings demonstrate that religion meaningfully shapes the fiscal behavior of elected officials.

From referendum to reconciliation: How shared values and identities align with a commitment to long‐term collective action

Emily A. Haines, Emma F. Thomas, Susilo Wibisono, Winnifred R. Louis, Janquel D. Acevedo, Michael Wenzel

Full text
One formal mechanism for effecting change to progress the rights of structurally disadvantaged groups is via citizen referenda. However, referenda are often characterized by divisive public debate. We suggest that sustaining action for long‐term change (beyond a referendum itself) may be associated with identifying with an opinion‐based group and perceiving shared values between advantaged and disadvantaged groups. Among a nationally representative sample of Australians ( N = 2467), we examined collective action intentions for the Reconciliation Movement 1 month before, 1 week before, and 1 week after the 2023 Voice to Parliament referendum. Study 1 used a random intercept cross‐lagged panel model to show that value consensus, opinion‐based identification as a supporter of Reconciliation, and reconciliation action were positively associated at a temporally stable level, but within‐person effects did not vary much from these levels. Study 2 used growth mixture models and revealed five diverging trajectories of reconciliation action, which were explained by differences in value consensus, opinion‐based identification, and support/opposition to the Voice referendum. Our findings offer some of the first evidence showing that value consensus and opinion‐based identification are associated with sustaining action for social change, especially during times of polarization and division.

Lobbying by Rebel Groups in the United States: Introducing the Rebel Lobbying Dataset, Version 1, 1945–2012

Yu Bin Kim, Ilayda B. Onder

Full text
During civil wars, some rebel groups hire lobbying and public relations (PR) firms to cultivate favorable perceptions abroad. Almost 15% of rebel groups, for example, lobbied through PR firms at least once in the United States between 1945 and 2012. Yet, data limitations have made it difficult for scholars of rebel diplomacy to study rebel lobbying systematically. To help mitigate these limitations and facilitate future research, we introduce the first version of the Rebel Lobbying Dataset (RLD), which captures whether and when rebel groups hired professional lobbying firms in the United States. The RLD covers 230 rebel groups engaged in 103 civil conflicts worldwide from 1945 to 2012. After presenting several descriptive patterns, we demonstrate the dataset’s utility by examining whether rebel lobbying in Washington is associated with subsequent U.S. support for rebel groups. By making rebel groups’ use of professional lobbying firms visible at scale for the first time, the RLD opens new avenues for studying the determinants, timing, and consequences of how rebel groups pursue foreign influence.

European Political Science Review

Not my democracy: conceptions, discontent, and participation in Europe

Bernd Schlipphak, Caner Simsek, Oliver Treib

Full text
Citizens differ not only in how strongly they support democracy, but also in what they expect democracy to be. Yet we know less about whether these democratic expectations shape satisfaction with democracy (SwD) and, through it, political participation (PP) in established European democracies. We first develop a mechanism linking citizens’ prioritized understandings of democracy (UoD) to SwD and unconventional political participation (UPP). Satisfaction with democracy is treated as an indicator of perceived congruence between citizens’ democratic ideals and how democracy works in practice. Second, we test this mechanism systematically in Europe using ESS Round 10 data, including almost 60,000 respondents in 31 countries. We find that (a) citizens’ conceptions of democracy affect SwD and (b) that SwD is a mediator for the impact UoD have on UPP.

A risky business: navigating autocratization through ideological legitimation claims

Claudio Balderacchi, Luca Tomini

Full text
Autocratization typically results from measures aiming at seizing, strengthening, and consolidating political power. However, it is not without risks: it may produce adverse reactions from multiple actors and possibly lead to the fall of its perpetrators from power. What makes autocratizing incumbents more capable of surviving during such a perilous process? We argue that the adoption of ideological legitimation strategies makes incumbents more capable of surviving during autocratization in democratic and electoral authoritarian regimes. We test our argument through survival analysis of incumbents in power during processes of autocratization from 1900 to 2024. Though relatively understudied in the context of autocratization, our study highlights ideology as a key factor in the process of transformation of a political regime toward autocracy.

Party Politics

How members and elites make sense of ideological change: Natural evolution, pragmatic adaptation, or betrayal in the (Northern) League

Mattia Zulianello

Full text
How do party members and representatives make sense of ideological change? This article tackles this question by studying one of the most important party transformations in contemporary European politics: the shift of the (Northern) League from populist regionalism to populist radical right politics under Matteo Salvini. Drawing on 60 semi-structured in-depth interviews with members and representatives, the analysis identifies three recurrent narratives: natural evolution (the national turn as the completion of the federalist project), pragmatic adaptation (change as electorally and strategically necessary), and betrayal (a rupture with the founding identity). The survival of the residual Padanian identity is no longer sustained by ideology, but loosely surfaces in rituals such as the annual gathering at Pontida, where it is blended with the state-wide symbolism. The findings suggest that the mass party model has facilitated the transformation while preventing overt infighting. This invites further research into the interpretive work during party change.

Political Research Quarterly

Candidate Gender and District Ideological Alignment in U.S. Congressional Elections, 2002–2020

Jordan Kujala, Danielle Joesten Martin

Full text
Candidate ideology impacts a wide range of electoral outcomes, but are there gendered differences in the effects of candidate ideology? Using a unique dataset of U.S. congressional elections between 2002 and 2020, we examine candidate-level factors (gender, ideology, and partisanship) and district-level political factors (district ideology and partisanship), and the interaction between these candidate and district-level factors. We first compare two measurements of candidate ideology for congressional incumbents of both major parties: absolute extremity and ideological distance from the district. Although there are partisan differences, we generally find that women are more ideologically extreme than men. We then examine how ideological distance from the district impacts three dependent variables, likelihood of running unopposed, vote share, and probability of winning election, and test how candidate gender interacts with ideological distance from the district. We find that incumbent women are less likely to run unopposed and have a lower vote share than incumbent men. Overall, it appears that women congressional candidates may be more ideologically extreme and are more likely to be punished for their ideological positions than men.

Leveraging National Party Leaders in Congressional Campaigns: The Case of Nancy Pelosi

Nichole M. Bauer, Erin C. Cassese, Natalie M. Standridge

Full text
Existing scholarship on negative campaigning has focused on the communication strategies of candidates who are actively contesting specific seats or offices. Yet, political leaders are often featured in ads where they are not a contestant. For example, between 2018 and 2022, the Republican Party spent over 227 million dollars on negative campaign ads featuring Nancy Pelosi, hoping to capitalize on her unpopularity with a wide swath of the voting public. In this paper, we develop a theoretical perspective on this kind of attack by association that emphasizes two processes – ideological signposting and transfer of affect – to explain why campaigns might choose to adopt this strategy. Using Pelosi as a case study, we analyze her depictions in televised U.S. House race ads from 2010 to 2020 and compare them with similarly situated male Congressional leaders. Our approach blends a content analysis of the visual and verbal elements of campaign ads with metadata from the Wesleyan Media Project, providing insights into the conditions under which attacks against Pelosi are likely to emerge. Our findings have implications for understanding not only negative campaigning but also the nationalization of political discourse and the challenges facing women as they advance to leadership positions within Congress.

Survival Before Diplomacy: How Electoral Competition Constrains Leader Diplomacy in Turkey

Full text
Do election campaigns reduce leaders’ engagement in foreign diplomacy? Whereas existing research argues that elections divert leaders’ time and attention away from international affairs, most evidence comes from the United States and relies on yearly data. This article examines the relationship between electoral cycles and diplomatic travel using the case of Turkey. Drawing on a monthly dataset of official foreign visits by Turkish prime ministers, presidents, and foreign ministers between 1990 and 2025, the analysis reveals that foreign travel declines significantly during election months and the immediately preceding period. Gender-Role Traditionalism and Opposition to Trade in the United States

Andrew Kerner, Jane L. Sumner

Full text
A recently popular line in American trade politics is that free trade threatens male blue-collar manufacturing employment, thereby undermining “traditional” family gender roles in which men work outside of the home and women work as caregivers within it. This study asks whether American mass politics mirrors these ideas. Does a preference for “traditional” gender roles lead Americans to protectionism? Our analysis of American National Election Studies data suggests that it does. We find a significant and highly robust empirical relationship between gender conservatism and protectionism. That relationship has been evident since 2008 and it decidedly is not a product of Trump-era politics. That relationship holds among men and women and among Republicans and Democrats, and it is robust to the inclusion of controls meant to isolate gender conservatism from other expressions of conservatism. The recent rise of gender-conservative trade skepticism should be understood as elite politics catching up with the public rather than leading it.

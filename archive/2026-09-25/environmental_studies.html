Public support for novel carbon removal hinges on procedural and distributive fairness

Livia Fritz, Lucilla Losi, Christine Merk, Michela Boldrini, Valentina Bosetti, Chad M. Baum, Benjamin K. Sovacool

Full text
Alongside the need for rapid emissions reductions, removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is emerging as a central pillar of global climate policy. Scaling up carbon removal raises pressing questions about how this can be done in an equitable and socially legitimate manner. Using vignette experiments with nationally representative surveys in six global south and global north countries ( N = 10,852 respondents across Brazil, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, Italy, Norway and the UK), we examine which modes of implementing novel carbon removal methods gain public support across diverse socio-political settings. We find that support hinges on procedural and distributive fairness: opening planning processes to public and expert scrutiny, benefit sharing and not-for-profit arrangements consistently increase public support for carbon removal across all countries and technologies. Respondents are unwilling to trade such fairness considerations for technical performance. Just governance and implementation are critical for securing public support when scaling up novel carbon removal methods.

Far-right political support and clean technology adoption in Germany

Sebastian Reining, Moritz Wussow, Chad Zanocco, Benedikt Kau, Rita Sousa-Silva, Corinna Köpke, Sahani Pathiraja, Dirk Neumann

Full text
The diffusion of clean technologies is critical for climate goals, yet political polarization influences adoption. Here we examine how ideology affects residential solar photovoltaic (PV) and electric vehicle (EV) uptake in Germany, drawing on responses from a three-wave individual-level survey, municipality-level observations of PV and EV registrations and election results. Supporters of the far-right Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) show significantly lower willingness to adopt EVs and, to a lesser degree, PV systems compared with supporters of the pro-environmental Bündnis 90 / Die Grünen (GRÜNE) party. However, polarization is not static. The relative adoption gap between municipalities with strong AfD support and those with strong GRÜNE support declined from 76% to 31% for solar PV between 2000 and 2023, and from 84% to 60% for EVs between 2015 and 2019. Here, using an instrumental-variable strategy based on wolf attacks as a predictor of AfD vote share, we provide complementary evidence on the relationship between far-right voting and the adoption of clean technology. Counterfactual simulations indicate that, by 2023, solar PV diffusion could have been up to 1.3% higher and EV diffusion up to 2.9% higher in the absence of rising electoral support for the far right.

Global Environmental Politics

Difference-in-Differences Estimation: A Quasi-Experimental Approach to Sustainable Development Interventions

Elisa D’Amico

Full text
How can we rigorously assess whether sustainable development interventions affect conflict? This methodological note demonstrates the difference-in-differences (DID) approach as a powerful tool for strengthening causal inference regarding sustainable development projects and their broader social and political consequences. While DID has been widely used to study climate-related shocks, practical guidance for applying these methods to intentional development interventions remains limited. This research note explains the core logic of DID methods in accessible terms, outlines the statistical framework, and demonstrates the approach using hydropower development as an illustrative case. The analysis reveals how DID can capture localized effects of development interventions with stronger causal evidence than correlational methods by comparing subnational variation across countries. The findings highlight both the promise and the limitations of this methodology for scholars and practitioners working at the intersection of development and environmental politics.

Introducing Audience Analysis into Just Securitization Theory: A Study of Climate Security Perceptions in Türkiye

Başar Baysal

Full text
This article examines how the public and members of parliament in Türkiye perceive climate change as a security issue and asks whether their perceptions create favorable conditions for its just securitization. Drawing on a survey of 3,827 participants and semistructured interviews with twenty parliamentarians, the study integrates audience analysis into Rita Floyd’s just securitization theory (JST) by evaluating how audience perceptions align with its normative criteria. Theoretically, it bridges the ontological divide in securitization scholarship by combining the Copenhagen School’s constructivist account of threat construction with Floyd’s moral criteria. Findings show broad consensus across both audiences: climate skepticism is minimal, water and food security and extreme climate events are the prioritized implications, and human beings and ecosystems are the primary referent objects. Both audiences support stronger state action. The article argues that integrating audience perceptions into JST strengthens the societal legitimacy and practical viability of climate securitization.

Here Come the Cartographers: Mapping the Seabed in the Digital Age

Elizabeth Havice, Lisa M. Campbell, Noella Gray

Full text
Members of the international oceans science and governance community are working to accelerate the effort to finish a complete mapping of the seabed by 2030. Based on our observations at the One Ocean Science Congress and the Third United Nations Oceans Congress, in this piece, we review the knowledge, technological, and regulatory-based arguments made in support of this effort and contextualize these within the broader history of mapping that highlights the relationship between science-driven legibility of space and political-economic change. Overall, we find that proponents of mapping generally present the act as a depoliticized, technical exercise that offers a general “good” to society. We point to the ways that this approach obscures the politics of mapping and urge attention to the more-than-technical nature of mapping. The processes and outcomes of mapping cannot be assumed, and proponents of seabed mapping should account for the scrambled relations and contestations that mapping drives and reflects.

On the Economization of Environmental Policy: Examining the Workings of Green Competitiveness in the Pursuit of Emissions Reduction

Juan Ignacio Staricco, Mónica Buraschi

Full text
Hybrid, transnational governance frameworks such as the European Union’s Renewable Energy Directive (EU-RED) are designed to shape production and trade practices in third countries by linking market access to compliance with sustainability criteria. Evidence on their effectiveness in these contexts, however, remains limited. This article examines why such frameworks often fail to achieve their environmental goals, focusing on EU-RED’s regulation of biofuels in the Argentina–EU biodiesel global value chain. We argue that EU-RED is based on a form of economization of environmental regulation that we define as “green competitiveness,” in which environmental performance becomes constitutive of competitive advantage rather than merely an external constraint. Economic actors in Argentina adopt this logic to strengthen their market positions, yet their strategies frequently decouple from meaningful environmental outcomes. By conceptually identifying the defining elements of green competitiveness and empirically analyzing their implementation in the case study, we show how these tensions manifest in concrete practices on the ground.

Foreign Aid and the Performance of Bureaucrats

Maria Nagawa

Full text
Although much work examines foreign aid’s impact on development outcomes, its effect on bureaucracies—institutions that are key to development and profoundly influenced by aid interventions—remains understudied. I argue that project aid channeled through recipient governments alters financial and social aspects of work over which bureaucrats hold salient preferences, generating trade-offs that drive bureaucrats to redirect effort from routine work toward donor-funded initiatives. Drawing on interviews, surveys, and experiments with more than 600 Ugandan bureaucrats, I find that despite preferring government funding and autonomy, bureaucrats are drawn to better-paid aid projects, thus diverting effort away from regular duties. They also prefer departments with substantial donor funding even if it undermines the equity and teamwork they value. These findings provide micro-level insights into the unintended consequences of external development assistance: the same incentives that boost performance on discrete donor-funded projects divert effort from government programming and erode organizational cohesion.

Review of International Organizations

Diffusion of environmental treaty dynamism: EU and US influences

Tobias Böhmelt

Full text
Multilateral environmental agreements (MEAs) can be dynamic in that they evolve with the introduction and adaptation of new legal commitments. This research contributes to explaining such treaty dynamism by exploring its diffusion across institutions. I focus on the role of the European Union (EU) and the United States (US) in the broader network of MEAs, eventually contending that dynamism diffuses more strongly through EU inter-agreement ties. The US inter-treaty network, however, is likely to play a limited role in facilitating the diffusion of new commitments. It is argued that these patterns are plausibly driven by the EU’s consistently pro-environmental agenda and its economic interests geared toward regulatory norm harmonization across MEAs. The empirical evidence is based on spatial models analyzing the most comprehensive data set of MEAs concluded since 1945. The results show that EU linkages promote treaty dynamism, whereas US ties have no significant effect on dynamism diffusion.

Does bilateral aid work better through multilateral channels? Evidence from the GODAD database

Bernhard Reinsberg

Full text
How does the choice of aid modality influence development effectiveness? While scholars have extensively studied the drivers of aid delegation, the relative effectiveness of different modalities remains under-theorized. I develop a theory of aid effectiveness rooted in the interplay of donor motives, recipient incentives, and principal−agent dynamics. I argue that earmarked aid to international organizations (IOs) will succeed in achieving specific sectoral outcomes by leveraging IO capacity to constrain recipient slack, though its rigid mandates and high transaction costs will limit its broader development impact. Conversely, bilateral aid—which often serves donor particularistic interests and grants recipients more autonomy—is generally ineffective, except when political competition disciplines recipients to prioritize long-term gains. To test these propositions, I utilize the GODAD database to analyze geolocated aid projects from 19 Western donors across 1,753 subnational regions from 1990 to 2024. Using two-step counterfactual difference-in-differences estimators, I examine the impact of different funding types on household wealth, child mortality, and governance. The findings reveal that earmarked aid is effective for specific goals, such as health aid reducing child mortality, whereas the success of governance aid depends on low baseline development levels. In contrast, bilateral aid is frequently ineffective or counterproductive, correlating with increased mortality and decreased state capacity. Sectoral targeting has limited effects, positively affecting only household wealth creation. These results highlight how the institutional design of aid delivery mediates the tension between donor control and recipient agency, offering critical implications for the study of multilateral delegation and aid effectiveness.

Journal of Peace Research

How citizens update their preferences on foreign policy: experimental evidence on Western sanctions

Anton Peez, Felix S Bethke

Full text
This study examines how citizens update their foreign policy preferences, focusing on the role of policy effectiveness in shaping support for economic sanctions. Economic sanctions often require sustained public support, which depends on perceptions of effectiveness. Using a two-stage experimental design, we investigate how individuals' preferences evolve when presented with information about effectiveness. We examine this updating behavior in “ordinary” times and in times of real-world foreign policy crisis. The study covers three major sanctioning countries—the United Kingdom, Germany, and the United States—and four hypothetical scenarios. Our results suggest that citizens indeed update their preferences: effectiveness causes a moderate increase in support, while ineffectiveness causes a significant drop, showing clear negativity bias. For the U.S. sample, we repeated the experiment across four survey waves collected before and after the Russian invasion of Ukraine, a major real-world foreign policy crisis. We find that responsiveness to effectiveness information remained remarkably stable. This demonstrates that citizens update their policy preferences based on instrumental reasoning, showing great consistency across countries, scenarios, and even in times of crisis.

Powerful or Powerless? The Influence of Gender Advocates in Peace Negotiations

Elizabeth Good, Alexandra K. McAuliff

Full text
While the Women, Peace and Security sector advocates for the inclusion of gender experts in peace processes to improve outcomes for women, empirical support for their effectiveness remains inconclusive. This paper tests whether individuals designated to advocate for gender-specific issues are influential or ineffective. Leveraging an original dataset capturing the role of 2,300 delegates across 116 comprehensive peace agreements finalized between 1990 and 2020, we find that gender advocates increase the likelihood that agreements contain provisions for women. However, investigating the case of Northern Ireland using interviews and archival analysis, we find that the systemic masculinized structure of peace negotiations hinders advocates’ overall influence. Consequently, this paper explains how these actors are simultaneously powerful and powerless. Findings capture gender advocates’ limitations, caution against individuals bearing sole responsibility for gender considerations in peace processes, and expand the conceptualization of what it is to be an expert.

Peace Under the Influence? The Role of Illicit Drug Economies in Peace Processes and Rebel Splintering

Myung Jung Kim, Do Young Gong

Full text
How do the economic foundations of rebel groups shape civil war dynamics? While prior research has explored how different resource types affect conflict, less is known about how the illicit nature of certain resources—particularly drug economies—affects peace processes. We argue that the inherent illegality of drug-based financing imposes structural barriers to peace, distinguishing it from other forms of rebel revenue. Using cross-national dyadic data from 1990 to 2011, we find that drug-reliant rebel groups have 34 percent lower hazard of initiatiating peace talks and over six times more likely to fragment once peace processes begin. These findings suggest that drug-based financing generates a self-reinforcing cycle that sustains violence. In contrast to accounts emphasizing grievances produced by peace processes, we show that some groups may be structurally predisposed to reject peace altogether. Our findings call for theoretical and policy approaches tailored to the distinct challenges posed by illicit drug economies.

International Studies Quarterly

Dollars Don’t Make Sense: How Russian Savers Responded to Sanctions

Daniel McDowell, David A Steinberg

Full text
Abstracts Theory suggests that financial sanctions provoke targets to de-dollarize because they raise the risks of holding dollars. However, sanctions can also cause target state currencies to depreciate, which should increase demand for dollars within those countries. This study examines how sanctions influence individuals’ desire for saving in foreign exchange (FX) like US dollars or euros. To do so, we draw on data from eight nationally representative surveys of Russian citizens before and after the imposition of Western sanctions in February 2022. We find that Russians’ preference for holding FX dramatically decreased after sanctions were imposed and that the fall in FX demand was most pronounced among wealthy Russians. A follow-up survey experiment further indicates that the drop in FX demand among wealthy Russians was driven by concerns about Western sanctions themselves rather than a response to the Russian government’s repressive FX policies. These results, which are consistent with the growing literature linking sanctions to de-dollarization, provide unique micro-level evidence from a high-profile sanctions’ episode, revealing how sanctions alter economic preferences within targeted states, across different groups within society.

Fragmented Agents and Ambiguous Mandates: A Process Theory of Norm Conflict Within International Organizations

Tom Buitelaar

Full text
Abstracts This article proposes a process theory explaining how crises can prompt principal intervention in international organizations (IOs) deadlocked over competing norms. Building on research in norm dynamics, sociological institutionalism, and principal–agent theory, it views IO bureaucracies as arenas where several groups compete over how to order competing norms into a hierarchy. When such conflicts cannot be resolved internally, an exogenous crisis may drive principals to revise the delegation contract, formalizing a new normative hierarchy. The theory is illustrated through an interpretivist process-tracing study of the United Nations Mission in the Democratic Republic of Congo (MONUC) in 2009–2010. MONUC faced tension between supporting Congolese state forces and protecting civilians. Leadership prioritized state support, while advocates for a conditionality policy sought to link assistance to human rights compliance. Civilian harm during joint operations, amplified by external advocacy, media scrutiny, and legal warnings, elevated the internal battle’s urgency for the UN Security Council, prompting a mandate change institutionalizing conditionality. The analysis reveals how crisis framing escalates intra-organizational norm disputes to the principal level, and shows how ambiguity does not only create opportunity but can also lead to unresolvable battles for ideas. As such, the paper contributes to the norms and IO literatures.

Symbolic Statecraft: Reactive and Projective Renaming in International Relations

Sefa Secen

Full text
Abstracts State renaming has received scant attention from International Relations scholars. This article advances a theoretically grounded typology explaining why and when states intervene in their own symbolic foundations through official name changes. I argue that renaming is not a residual outcome of political transformation but a performative mechanism through which states manage disruptions in self-identity narratives or pursue deliberate projects of identity redefinition. It operates along two logics. Reactive renaming emerges in moments of identity rupture—postcolonial transition, regime collapse, or ideological reorientation—when ontological insecurity renders symbolic intervention salient. Projective renaming is forward-looking, occurring without acute rupture, as states proactively assert status, redefine roles, or reposition themselves within international hierarchies. Through comparative analysis of Zimbabwe, Türkiye, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, North Macedonia, and Afghanistan—and three contrasting cases of unresolved or stalled renaming (India, Aotearoa/New Zealand, and Kazakhstan)—I show how renaming functions as an instrument of identity repair, status and role-seeking, and regime legitimation, and identify the scope conditions under which it becomes politically realizable. By theorizing symbolic acts as mechanisms of state-making rather than epiphenomenal gestures, the article advances our understanding of how states actively construct political subjectivity and hierarchy in international relations.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

‘Change at home and change abroad?’ Visualising the (im)possibilities of British foreign policy in the aftermath of Corbyn’s response to the Manchester Arena bombing

Thomas Howe, Maximillian Guarini

Full text
The Manchester Arena bombing occurred at a moment of heightened anxiety and uncertainty in the United Kingdom. One of several attacks over a matter of months in 2017, it was the deadliest and most politically affronting. The bombing brought the consequences of liberal interventionism into focus and triggered a contest to define the moment, producing competing visions of Britishness. Drawing on insights from Lacanian international relations and visual politics, the article analyses responses to the bombing through a multimodal discourse analysis of national newspaper coverage and political speeches. It shows that Jeremy Corbyn challenged the elite liberal-interventionist consensus by linking foreign intervention to terrorism at home and advocating policies of restraint, sovereign equality and democratic multilateralism. Resonating with sections of the public, Corbyn’s response generated acute anxiety among elites and elicited an affectively charged defence of the status quo. In response, politicians and legacy media sought to construct ideological equivalences between Corbyn and hostile Others, mobilising multimodal presentations which recast him as anti-British and a threat to national security. This article contributes to debates on continuity and change in British foreign policy and the role of images in managing dissent.

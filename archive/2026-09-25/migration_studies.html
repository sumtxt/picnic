International Migration Review

Cross-Country Variation in Deportation Risk: Stylized Facts and a Research Agenda

Christian Ambrosius, Marina Luna

Full text
While considerable effort has been devoted to measuring international migration and improving data on migrant remittances, comparable information on deportation and forced return remains fragmented across countries. To address this gap, we assembled administrative data from official governmental statistics in deporting countries and complemented them, where necessary, with information obtained through freedom-of-information requests. The resulting DepRisk database covers 42 deporting countries, including most OECD members, and reports returns by citizenship for 204 origin countries and territories, yielding approximately 7,200 bilateral deportation corridors with multiple indicators of migration enforcement. Using these data, this research note maps deportation and return practices across countries and deportation corridors. Two main findings emerge. First, meaningful cross-country comparison remains challenging because deportation statistics reflect important differences in legal definitions, administrative procedures, and reporting practices. Second, despite these measurement challenges, enforcement intensities vary substantially across both deporting countries and bilateral corridors. Building on these findings and the emerging literature on deportation regimes, the note identifies three priorities for future research: improving the availability and comparability of deportation statistics, refining the conceptualization and measurement of deportation risk, and explaining variation in deportation regimes across different political and institutional contexts.

Population, Space and Place

Independent Ageing Amongst Older Adults Who Live Alone: Social Networks and Support in Singapore

Samantha Shu Fang Lim, Grace En Ying Chan, Siyao Gao, Vincent Chua, Chen‐Chieh Feng, Elaine Lynn‐Ee Ho

Full text
This paper examines how older adults who live alone draw on local and extra‐local social ties—including kinship relations, community networks, and friendships—to age independently in Singapore. Conceptually, it engages with extant debates on the hierarchical‐compensatory and task‐specific theories. The paper draws on a mixed‐methods study that combines survey data with qualitative data from interviews, observations and GPS tracking of participants who live in two neighbourhoods. We show how older adults who live alone access and mobilise support in response to their changing life circumstances. Our findings indicate that older adults who live alone are not passive. Rather, they maintain their independence through interdependent relationships with a range of local and extra‐local social networks, which are fluid and will evolve over time. Emotional closeness determines most of all how positively older adults perceive and experience care and support. This paper demonstrates the importance of considering emotional proximity, changing social networks, and interdependence in understanding how older adults age independently.

Friend and Neighbour Ties Among Unpartnered, Childless and ‘Kinless’ Older Adults in Europe

Christine A. Mair

Full text
Declining fertility, increased geographic mobility and changes in nuclear families have contributed to the rise of older adults who are unpartnered, childless or both unpartnered and childless (‘kinless’). For this growing global population, non‐family ties such as friends and neighbours may be particularly important. Although often conflated, friends and neighbours represent distinct ties that often provide different resources, with neighbours being geographically proximal and friends being geographically flexible. Yet, very few cross‐national studies distinguish between friends and neighbours, and no study to date has examined these ties among older adults with limited family availability cross‐nationally. To address this gap, I analyze social network data from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (Wave 6; N = 56,093 respondents across 17 countries) and country‐level indicators from multiple sources. Using multivariable logistic regression models, I examine whether older adults lacking partners and/or children are more likely to list a friend compared to a neighbour in their discussion networks and explore how these patterns vary across macro‐level contexts. Lacking family is associated with greater reliance on non‐family ties, and friend ties are substantially more common than neighbour ties in all countries and contexts. Friend ties are particularly more common for kinless older adults in Northern and Western Europe, especially in higher‐GDP contexts. Although neighbour ties are much less common overall, they are more pronounced in lower‐GDP and Southern European contexts. These findings highlight heterogeneity in later‐life support systems and underscore the importance of both geographically‐bounded and geographically‐flexible support ties to promote ‘aging in place’ and ‘aging in networks’, respectively.

Geographical Mobility and Occupational Achievement: Examining the Role of Italian Cities as «Escalator» Regions

Rocco Molinari, Roberto Impicciatore, Nazareno Panichella

Full text
This study investigates the relationship between geographical mobility and occupational achievement focusing on internal migration processes that occurred in the second half of the 20th century in Italy. Using retrospective information from the Italian Household Longitudinal Survey (IHLS), the study clarifies whether certain destinations, particularly large urban centres in central and northern Italy, provide inter‐regional migrants with systematic occupational advantages, enabling migrants to attain higher‐status positions after relocating. Findings reveal that migrants to large cities in central and northern Italy achieve, on average, higher occupational standings compared to stayers in the area of origin, with strong differences by gender and geographical area of origin. Furthermore, the occupational advantage associated with migration to large cities substantially varies by social class of origin, suggesting the interrelation between urban escalators and the intergenerational transmission of social inequalities.

International Migration

Azimat wa Bazgasht: Emigrant Governance and Limited Statehood in Afghanistan (1919–2026)

Damla B. Aksel, Maryam Asifi, Fatima Zhazykbayeva

Full text
Research on migration from Afghanistan has focused largely on migrants' experiences and on return dynamics driven by host states and international organizations, leaving the origin state's practices toward emigrants analytically underdeveloped. This article examines emigrant governance across five regimes, from the monarchy to the second Taliban administration (1919–2026), drawing on laws, decrees and bilateral arrangements. Because limited statehood has remained a near‐constant condition while regime ideology has shifted radically, the Afghan case allows the effects of ideology on emigrant governance to be isolated. The analysis reveals continuity in institutional form, through citizenship laws, travel documentation or repatriation invitations, alongside systematic variation in their ideological content. Emigrants were constructed as resources or liabilities according to each regime's political project and relationship to external sources of legitimacy and support, rather than the scale of displacement itself. Constrained by limited implementation capacity, regimes used these instruments to render populations legible and to perform sovereignty in negotiation with international actors and an increasingly assertive diaspora. Across all five periods, these dynamics were also deeply gendered: women's mobility became a key site for projecting competing visions of Afghan identity, ranging from education abroad as a symbol of modernity to legally enforced immobility as a moral imperative. The Afghan case thus contributes to debates on migration governance in the Global South by showing how emigrant governance persists under conditions of limited statehood, where sovereignty is fragmented and authority contested.

Factors Affecting Future Mobility of South Asian Migrants in Saudi Arabia

Areej Jamal, Nasra M. Shah, Richa Shivakoti

Full text
Saudi Arabia hosts numerous South Asian families facing uncertainty under the transient residency regime. This article examines how planned length of stay, willingness to settle permanently, and desire to relocate to a third country (with Canada as the focus) shape the mobility intentions of South Asians in Saudi Arabia. Analysing survey data from 1019 Indian, Pakistani and Bangladeshi migrants, the multivariate analysis reveals that belonging, religion, and family living arrangements predict these outcomes. Findings show that strong local belonging (OR ≈ 2.7) and living with family significantly predict longer planned stays, signalling a preference for permanence. Notably, being Muslim systematically increases willingness to stay permanently if legal pathways exist (with belonging raising the odds nearly six‐fold), driven by spiritual belonging within the ‘Land of Islam’. Conversely, second‐generation status, strong belonging to Saudi Arabia, and family presence predict onward migration rather than returning to their country of origin, while higher education specifically predicts choosing Canada; these migrants strategically leverage class resources to navigate point‐based Western immigration systems. Thus, family‐based migrants actively navigate temporary regimes, positioning Saudi Arabia as a global migration stepping stone.

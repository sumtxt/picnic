Propaganda, Administrative Deformation, and the Erosion of State Capacity: The Case of the “Censorship Industrial Complex”

Renée DiResta, Dean Jackson

Full text
This paper examines how a propaganda frame alleging the existence of a “Censorship Industrial Complex” was institutionalized and then helped justify the dismantling of US election information resilience infrastructure across multiple federal agencies. Drawing on documentary evidence and process tracing, we show how political entrepreneurs reframed routine, non‐coercive election information coordination and communication practices as a covert censorship regime targeting conservative speech. The article argues that this case is best understood as administrative deformation: weakly evidenced claims were laundered into higher‐status venues, including congressional investigations, litigation, executive action, and budget rationales, where they altered the incentive environment facing public agencies. The result was staff reductions, program cuts, and chilling effects, and weakened state capacity in a core domain of democratic administration. We conclude with recommendations for protecting administrative resilience under conditions of narrative attack.

Managing Collaboration Dynamics Under Pressure: Lessons From the COVID ‐19 Medical Supply Shortage

Christopher Bowers, Qian Hu, James L. Olds

Full text
In crisis responses such as the COVID‐19 medical supply shortage, senior policy makers often emphasize urgency, a strategy that prioritizes the rapid achievement of intermediate goals. While this approach can drive swift action, it also introduces challenges and trade‐offs that can hinder collaboration toward long‐term success. These challenges often arise from misalignment between organizational norms and priorities of collaborating partners, which can undermine legitimacy and performance. Policymakers should consider how personnel composition, such as large military contingents, shapes task force culture and operational dynamics. Even in fast‐moving crises, clearly aligning roles, goals, and timelines from the start can reduce confusion, while leaders must navigate cultural norms and challenge ingrained practices to support collaboration.

Small Polity, Hybrid Administration, International Research: Public Administration in Switzerland

Fritz Sager, Adrian Ritz, Johanna Hornung

Full text
Switzerland presents a most peculiar political system whose institutional idiosyncrasies shape not only the practice of public administration but also the scientific study thereof. This country study presents the development and state of both public administration as practice and Public Administration as a scholarly discipline. It argues that both are largely determined by three properties of Switzerland as a political context and field of study: Semi‐direct democracy turns the administration into an important political actor. The “Milizsystem” (Switzerland's tradition of non‐professionalized, part‐time public engagement) enables hybrid careers between the public and private sectors and produces a structurally weaker political class that the professional administration regularly outmaneuvers. And the small size of the country has pushed the discipline toward international orientation while simultaneously entangling researchers with their object of study. Taken together, the Swiss case illustrates how political institutions do not merely constrain public administration but fundamentally constitute it.

The DOGE Experiment: Algorithmic Governance, Operational Capture, and the Case for Hybrid Intelligence

Haris Alibašić

Full text
This study examines the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE) as a case of algorithmic governance whose stated purpose, improving federal efficiency, diverged systematically from its operational effect: enabling unprecedented private‐sector access to federal data systems while dismantling the oversight and civil‐service infrastructure that constrains such access. Drawing on state capacity, bounded rationality, organizational learning, and public value theory, the analysis demonstrates that the conventional efficiency‐failure reading, though correct on its own terms, obscures what made DOGE structurally distinct from the Grace Commission, the National Performance Review, the President's Management Agenda, and Program Assessment Rating Tool, and the Government Performance and Results Modernization Act. The article advances hybrid intelligence, structured human‐AI collaboration with protected institutional capacity, as a paradigm resisting both reform failure and algorithmic capture. International implementations operationalize it. The analysis contributes a stated‐versus‐operational‐goal analytic to the AI governance literature and identifies legislative grounding as a precondition for durable reform.

What Does Representation Represent? Disaggregating Demographic and Behavioral Components

Rachel (Soyeon) Cho

Full text
Representative bureaucracy research has often linked demographic representation directly to favorable outcomes, inferring active representation post hoc while rarely separating the behavioral component presumed to accompany such associations from the representation variable itself. This study addresses such gap by drawing on gender differences in leadership literature to operationalize supportive leadership as one behavioral component associated with female supervisory representation and examine the constituent elements of what has been interpreted as a representation effect. Using gender pay inequality across US federal agencies as the empirical context, the findings show that while both female supervisory representation and supportive leadership are each positively associated with nonsupervisory salaries when examined separately, the association attributed to representation loses significance once both are modeled together. These findings do not suggest that representation is unimportant; rather, they indicate that prior representation effects may partially reflect a behavioral component that has not been separately identified.

Epistemic Capabilities of Artificial Intelligence for Public Administration and Policy Research: A Framework for Scholarship in the Age of Intelligent Machines

Yanto Chandra, Jianxiang Tan

Full text
Artificial intelligence (AI) has gained much interest in public administration and public policy fields in recent years. Research on and that uses AI has so far treated it as an object or context within the broader administrative and governance discourse. In this article, we aim to shift the locus of scholarly attention on AI by focusing on its premise as a new epistemic technology that will shape how scholars conduct research. In doing so, it proposes a framework for leveraging AI as a methodological enabler that augments researchers' cognitive and analytical capabilities, thereby overcoming longstanding methodological barriers in the fields. We propose a framework distinguishing AI applications along two epistemological dimensions of research that make use of synthetic data and real‐world data: (1) inductive approaches to discover patterns and build theory to address exploratory questions (“what,” “so what”), and (2) deductive approaches to test hypotheses and establish causal relationships to serve explanatory and predictive purposes (“why,” “how”). We contend that AI has propelled the fields toward a “new normal science” characterized by human‐machine collaboration.

From Policy Neglect to Research Agenda: Administrative Burdens and Donor‐Conceived Mobilization

Ashley Splawinski

Full text
Donor‐conceived adults are a growing population, mobilizing against the very regulatory arrangements that determined the terms into which they were conceived. In Canada and the United States, where donor conception remains persistently under‐regulated, gamete donation policy has historically prioritized the presumed needs of donors, recipient parents, and fertility clinics, structuring regulation around treatment access and donor supply rather than the long‐term interests of those conceived. The result is a population that bears disproportionate administrative burdens and is now mobilizing through advocacy organizations, legal claims, and the pathways consumer DNA testing has made possible. Yet donor‐conceived adults remain largely outside the scope of policy scholarship. This paper argues that donor conception offers a theoretically distinctive site for the study of administrative burden, policy feedback, and the social construction of target populations. The paper proposes a research agenda organized around the politics of administratively produced populations and the deliberate distribution of administrative burden through policy design.

Regulatory Capture, Public Trust and Pro‐Environmental Behaviors

Songsong Chen, Jianhua Xu

Full text
Regulatory capture, where special interests steer regulation away from public interest, is widely discussed, but its broader public consequences remain underexplored. We explore the relationship between perceived regulatory capture, public trust and pro‐environmental behaviors using online survey data in China. Findings show that perceived material and information capture (the latter measured with a single scenario‐based proxy) have significant negative associations with public trust in local ( b = −0.154, −0.105) and central government ( b = −0.087, −0.064). Direct associations with non‐activist pro‐environmental behaviors in the public sphere are consistently negative and supported by bootstrapped confidence intervals across specifications (material: β = −0.066 to −0.082; information: β = −0.036 to −0.047), whereas several corresponding private sphere associations are not. Indirect associations are supported only in selected models. These findings highlight the public implications of regulatory capture, distinguishing material and information capture, and extend understanding of public trust and pro‐environmental behaviors.

A Replication of “It is Not Merely About the Content: How Rules Are Communicated Matters to Administrative Burden”

Qi Bian, Luning Xin, Ben Ma

Full text
This study replicates prior research to examine how rule communication forms shape administrative burden, while also extending their research by investigating how artificial intelligence (AI) services moderate this relationship. AI services are understood to perform two distinct functions: the labeling function, where chatbots output policy rules directly from databases in response to user queries without further processing, and the optimization function, where chatbots collect, analyze, and distill rule information to present users with simplified, prioritized key points. In Study 1, the original research is replicated in the context of natural disaster relief applications in China. The findings demonstrate that bureaucratic language raises learning and compliance costs, stress, and autonomy loss, while complex information structures also increase learning costs, compliance costs, and autonomy loss. These results confirm that rule communication shapes administrative burden across policies, countries, and languages. However, differences emerge compared to the original study, in which bureaucratic language induced stigma and information complexity had no effect on compliance or autonomy loss, potentially due to contextual factors such as the complexity of Chinese logographic writing. Building on this, the extension experiment in Study 2 demonstrates that AI services reduce administrative burden. Notably, and contrary to expectations, the labeling function reduces learning, compliance, and psychological costs, while the optimization function achieves even greater reductions across all dimensions. Collectively, these findings provide empirical evidence supporting the use of AI to improve rule communication and ease administrative burden.

Managing policy expertise through think tank–state relations in Kazakhstan

Vera Axyonova

Full text
The interconnections among expertise, politics, and policy making remain of persistent scholarly interest, yet they are still insufficiently examined outside consolidated liberal democracies. Addressing this gap, this article analyses think tank–state relations in Kazakhstan, approached here as a case of soft authoritarianism – a political system that imposes systematic restrictions on political rights and civic freedoms while retaining formal democratic institutions and relying more on citizen persuasion, agenda management, and depoliticization than on overt coercion. The study examines how soft authoritarianism shapes the conditions under which think tanks operate and how these conditions evolve during complex crises that heighten the demand for expertise. Empirically, it focuses on Kazakhstan over the period 2010–25. Drawing on original expert interviews, the analysis shows that think tanks in Kazakhstan operate within a selectively pluralistic yet tightly managed policy environment characterized by resource dependence, co-optation, and personalized access to decision-making. Rather than excluding or eliminating independent expertise altogether, the state strategically incorporates and manages it through informal networks, selective funding, and bounded participation in policy consultations. The findings further demonstrate that major crises create temporary openings for expert engagement and role redefinition, but do not fundamentally transform underlying power relations. By examining how expertise is incorporated, constrained, and instrumentalized under soft authoritarianism, while drawing comparisons with both de-democratizing and more restrictive authoritarian contexts, the article contributes to advancing comparative debates on think tanks and policy expertise beyond democratic environments.

Regulation & Governance

Artificial Intelligence, Regulatory Capacity, and the Adaptive State

Chris Doyle, Martin Cave, William Webb

Full text
Artificial intelligence is changing not only what regulators govern but the informational environment through which regulation is conducted. Regulators increasingly use advanced analytics to process evidence and target supervisory attention, while regulated organizations deploy AI across their operations. This paper develops the concept of the adaptive regulatory state to analyze how changing informational capabilities generate behavioral and institutional adaptation. Comparative cases from financial supervision, competition, tax administration, and electronic communications identify three possible trajectories: constructive learning, governed co‐evolution, and adversarial escalation. Effective oversight does not require technological parity with regulated firms, but sufficient informational, organizational, and verification capabilities to respond as the regulatory environment changes. Four institutional principles follow: functional rather than technological capacity; effective inspectability and proportionate assurance; learning with bounded revisability; and coordinated but accountable capacity. The adaptive regulatory state is defined by whether regulatory institutions remain capable, accountable, and legitimate as their informational environment evolves.

Contested Coexistence: Transnational Private Governance and Public Regulatory Reform in Global Value Chains

Pauline Jerrentrup, Jade Kosché

Full text
This article examines how a transnational coalition of NGOs, unions, and labor rights organizations drove both private and public regulation on gender‐based violence and harassment in Lesotho's garment sector, and how the resulting regulatory systems coexist despite ongoing tension between them. Drawing on 15 months of ethnographic fieldwork, stakeholder interviews, and archival analysis, we show that the coalition leveraged reputational and diplomatic pressures, enabled by Lesotho's structural dependence on the United States as both buyer and donor, to secure the Lesotho Agreement and subsequent public regulatory reforms. The same dependencies that enabled these reforms also generated sustained tensions once the regulatory systems were in place. We term this dynamic contested coexistence —a governance configuration in which a supplying country's structural dependence on a buyer country forecloses rejection of a private regulatory system while constraining open endorsement of it—and identify it as a new form of private‐public interaction within Cashore et al.'s framework.

American Political Science Review

Are Voters Myopic or Looking for Improvement?

MARKUS PRIOR, TALBOT M. ANDREWS

Full text
Timing of valence outcomes plays an important role in key theories of electoral choice, and different models place different emphases on past and future outcomes. Whereas sanctioning models consider only retrospective evaluations as an accountability mechanism, selection models hold that voters use past and proposed future conditions to infer the incumbent’s ability to deliver benefits. Drawing on four surveys run on six different samples, we demonstrate that voters prefer improving conditions in both retrospection and prospection. In retrospection, this pattern is difficult to distinguish from overweighting of temporally proximate conditions, which gave rise to the notion of “the myopic voter.” Distinguishing the two processes, we find that people do not ignore early datapoints and reward improving conditions (in both retrospection and prospection). This preference for improvement does not simply reflect an uncritical heuristic for upward trends. People are not universally myopic and many genuinely value improving aggregate conditions retrospectively and prospectively.

Measuring Partisanship and Representation in Online Congressional Communications

MICHAEL KISTNER, MICHAEL HESELTINE, ROBERT ALVAREZ, MAYA FITCH, LUCAS LOTHAMER, ELIZABETH SIMAS

Full text
Social media and the internet have created new ways for representatives to communicate. How have members of Congress responded to these opportunities? We introduce a multi-platform dataset of congressional communications extending back to the onset of the social media era. Using computational language processing, we classify approximately 4.7 million tweets, 2.4 million Facebook posts, and 184,000 email newsletters authored by members of Congress between 2009 and 2022 based on intended purpose, and scale the partisanship of each message along a continuous left–right dimension. After validation, we demonstrate how our data can be used to study partisanship and representation in the contemporary Congress. Importantly, our data show congressional rhetoric has become more partisan and negative as social media usage has increased. We identify one potential mechanism contributing to this trend: partisanship and negativity receive inflated levels of positive engagement on social media relative to other forms of messaging like credit claiming or constituency service.

American Journal of Political Science

The territorial rights of wild animals: Justification

Pablo P. Castello

Full text
Territorial rights (TR) have become foundational in normative political theory, addressing pressing questions about boundary drawing, governance, and resource use. Influential TR theorists Cara Nine and Margaret Moore justify humans’ TR based on the functional, social, and identity‐anchored normative significance of non‐substitutable places. However, these arguments treat animals as resources, excluding them from normative consideration and political standing. This article analyzes Nine and Moore's arguments to assess whether their justificatory logic applies to wild animals. Using mule deers as a case study, I test whether they have sufficiently weighty interests in occupancy, residency, resource use, and control to ground duties on humans and whether TR theories should continue to treat animals as mere natural resources.

European Journal of Political Research

Get out to deliberate (GOTD): Door-to-door canvassing to improve civic engagement

Katy Tabero, Zohreh Khoban, Paolo Spada, Rafael Mestre, Matt Ryan

Full text
Several decades of experimentation have found that ‘Get-Out-the-Vote’ (GOTV) campaigns can effectively increase voter turnout. Yet, little has been said about the ability of similar-style campaigns to encourage participation in other types of civic engagement, such as citizens’ assemblies, which require much higher levels of commitment. Furthermore, although studies have found general support for citizens’ assemblies, willingness to participate is higher amongst those already more politically engaged and with higher levels of education, presenting a challenge to claims surrounding legitimacy and representation. In this article, we conduct a GOTV-style field experiment, complemented by a doorstep survey, to increase and understand perspectives towards registration for the 2023 Southampton Citizens’ Climate Assembly. We find that canvassing can be an effective method for increasing registration, including from under-represented groups. However, there are considerable barriers to participation, particularly the level of commitment, personal interest, and concerns about impact, which limit recruitment efforts.

The rhetoric of responsibility in party manifestos (1980–2021)

Andrea Ceron, Alessio Scopelliti

Full text
Focusing on the responsibility–responsiveness dilemma, we analyze responsibility rhetoric (defined as the emphasis on elements such as national solidarity, the public interest, and public-spiritedness), hypothesizing that it is used by parties moderating their policy positions, to justify deviations from their traditional standpoints, as well as by large incumbent parties seeking to claim credit for their responsible actions. In contrast, populist and niche parties, which prioritize responsiveness, are expected to avoid such rhetoric. We apply the manifestoberta Large Language Model (LLM) to classify natural sentences from up to 1,927 party manifestos (1980–2021), covering 38 countries worldwide, into the traditional MARPOR/Comparative Manifesto Project (CMP) coding categories. We then adopt two methods, a semisupervised Latent Dirichlet allocation topic model (Seeded-LDA) and the OpenAI GPT-4o-mini LLM, to refine one of these categories by selecting only those sentences that align with our concept of responsibility rhetoric. The results of statistical analysis support our hypotheses and are robust across different operationalizations of the dependent variable.

Cool Citizens? How Affectively Unpolarized Citizens View and Participate in Democracy

Peter Luca Versteegen, Joseph B. Phillips, Lilliana Mason

Full text
Does affective polarization change how citizens view and participate in democracy? We posit that the answer to this crucial question depends on how affectively un polarized citizens view and participate in democracy. Yet, the most common operationalization of affective polarization––measuring sympathy ratings for one party relative to another––does not differentiate between people’s absolute sympathy ratings for the parties, which may mask essential heterogeneity among unpolarized citizens. This paper theorizes the methodological origin of this heterogeneity, describes it, and highlights its implications. Cluster analyses and regressions from three nationally representative surveys in the US provide three conclusions. First, substantial shares of the US population are affectively un polarized. Second, unpolarized citizens cluster into two groups: one feeling lukewarm and one feeling cold toward both parties. Third, these unpolarized groups report diverging democratic attitudes and participation patterns. We discuss the methodological implications and the need for future research on affectively unpolarized citizens.

Women Politicians’ Role Model Effects: The Influence of Shared Identities Beyond Gender

Louise Halberg Nielsen

Full text
Women consistently report lower levels of political interest and political ambition than men. Studies have examined how women politicians as role models can make women more politically engaged, but studies have not typically examined additional shared identities beyond gender. I propose that women politicians with whom women share additional identities will be perceived as more relatable and inspiring and, consequently, have stronger role model effects on women’s political engagement. Through a conjoint experiment (Study 1), I find that having additional shared identities with a woman politician increases perceptions of her as relatable and inspiring. However, a survey experiment with novel video treatments depicting a woman politician’s story (Study 2) did not produce positive role model effects on women’s political interest, internal political efficacy, or nascent political ambition – regardless of additional shared racial and partisan identity with the woman politician. Moreover, the video treatment had remarkably different effects across White and Black women, regardless of additional shared racial and partisan identity. An observational analysis (Study 3) replicates the finding that additional shared racial and partisan identity with women political representatives is not systematically correlated with higher levels of political interest. These findings carry important implications for understanding women politicians’ role model effects and the potential of role models to increase women’s political engagement.

Political Science Research and Methods

When habit prevails: a causal test of policy feedback theory using a pension reform

Michael Ganslmeier, Margaryta Klymak, Tim Vlandas

Full text
Elderly voters are a growing, politically powerful electoral bloc, and their pensions often constitute the largest component of welfare state. We know that cuts can provoke electoral backlash, but it is less clear whether benefit expansions mobilize retirees. Some policy feedback theories predict increased participation following material gains, but recent evidence on policy expansions is mixed. While income and political participation appear correlated among the elderly, habit formation theory implies that older voters may be a least likely case for benefit expansion leading to mobilization. Leveraging a pension reform in Illinois that increased benefits by 36% for retirees, a regression discontinuity design on administrative data covering 93,000 public employees across twelve elections shows no turnout effect, even among low-income groups.

Just to be clear? Strategic vagueness of the European Parliament in EU policy negotiations

Aleksandra Khokhlova, Nikoleta Yordanova, Anastasia Ershova

Full text
Vague legislative language can facilitate law adoption and implementation but reduce policy effectiveness and application uniformity. So, when do legislators employ it? Building on policymaking and responsiveness theories, we argue that legislators use vague language to overcome institutional conflict and public opinion constraints but avoid it when they seek to assert their positions in legislative output and policy outcomes. Empirically, we study the conditions under which the European Parliament (EP) introduces vague amendments to European Union (EU) legislative proposals using dictionary-based content analysis of EU legislative negotiations between 2009 and 2019. The results show that EP sticks to more concrete positions when inter-institutional conflict threatens its policy goals but employs vagueness to maintain a broad appeal and avoid public backlash given public opinion divisions and contentiousness. These results add to our knowledge of legal text formulation, EU law making, and EP representation.

Echoes of conflict: quantifying redundancy in fatality time series

Thomas Schincariol, Thomas Chadefaux

Full text
Do wars follow predictable patterns, or are they chaotic and inherently unpredictable? We explore this question by quantifying temporal redundancy—how often similar patterns recur—in conflict-related fatalities. Using entropy-based analysis—a measure of randomness—and Dynamic Time Warping, we compare conflict dynamics with other complex systems such as seismology and finance. Our findings reveal that the dynamics of fatalities within conflicts exhibit high levels of entropy: they are largely unpredictable. Conflicts generally display higher entropy than climate or earthquake data, which highlights the challenges of predictive modeling. Moreover, conflict patterns are not region- or decade-specific, which suggests a degree of universality. Our framework distinguishes stable from chaotic phases in conflict dynamics and clarifies where forecasting is feasible.

High accuracy with low costs: the pretrain-finetune paradigm for classification with transformer-based language models

Cecilia Y. Sui

Full text
Political science increasingly uses text classification to gauge subtle concepts such as toxicity or anger. Traditional methods treat words in isolation, overlooking the contextual dynamics where meaning resides. Transformer-based language models address these limitations but remain underutilized, especially among applied scholars, partly due to misconceptions about their mechanisms and computational requirements. This article bridges this gap by offering an accessible explication of the pretrain-finetune paradigm, focusing on underlying mechanisms and their potential to improve political text analysis by harnessing models trained on extensive datasets while requiring modest labeled data and computational resources. I demonstrate the approach by identifying toxic language in conversation threads following U.S. Senators’ tweets and provide an online tutorial to support nontechnical scholars in adopting the methodology.

Does preference intensity matter? Reexamining policy representation in the American states

Zachary Peskowitz, Eddy S. F. Yeung

Full text
Preference intensity may play an important role in shaping mass political behavior, yet existing empirical scholarship on representation typically relies on survey measures that capture public opinion in terms of preference directionality without intensity. We use a variety of measures—not only the standard Likert and “most important policy” items but also the quadratic voting method—to reassess responsiveness and congruence of a diverse set of American state policies that vary in issue areas and salience. Across multiple novel measures of individual policy preferences, we consistently find that state-level preference intensity does not reliably predict actual policy outcomes. We contribute new evidence that the lack of policy representation in the American states remains—even if preference intensity is systematically integrated into the empirical analysis.

What does the public know about their State’s abortion laws in the post-Dobbs era?

Marisa Abrajano, Marianna Garcia, Aaron Pope, Robert Vidigal, Jonathan Nagler

Full text
Following the Dobbs decision in the U.S., there were widespread reports of misinformation regarding the ruling. Many Americans may therefore have faced uncertainty about abortion laws in their state, and Latinos—approximately 40% of whom are foreign-born—may be particularly vulnerable due to limited familiarity with the U.S. political system. To better understand awareness of state abortion policies, we fielded a national online panel survey containing large samples of Latinos ( N = 2326) and non-Hispanic whites ( N = 769). We find that Latinos were 6 percentage points less likely to correctly identify their state’s abortion law relative to white Americans and were 10.7 percentage points more likely to overestimate the strictness of those laws, even when controlling for demographic, religious, and political covariates. Foreign-born Latinos were also 19.5 percentage points more likely to overestimate the strictness of their state’s abortion law compared to their U.S.-born counterparts. These findings contribute to our understanding of differential learning following sudden policy change and highlight significant knowledge gaps that may affect whether individuals seek abortion care if they misunderstand the legal status in their state. Such gaps may have downstream consequences for health care access and economic inequality.

Journal of Experimental Political Science

Shared Discrimination’s Effect on Solidarity between People of Color Is Uniform Across Major Racial, Political, and Social Differences

Kasheena Rogbeer, Emily Ortiz, Efrén Pérez

Full text
Solidarity between people of color (PoC) is key to understanding multiracial coalition-building in the United States. However, limited statistical power has constrained research on whether shared discrimination’s effect on solidarity is reliably moderated by differences in race, political orientation, and social background, which structure the internal diversity of racially stigmatized subgroups within the mega-category, PoC. We report a preregistered meta-analysis of eight published experiments ( N = 5,813), which used a common design to test shared discrimination’s effects among Black Americans, Asian Americans, Latinos, and Middle Eastern and North African individuals. Despite known heterogeneity in these group’s social and political attitudes, we find that shared discrimination’s effect on solidarity is statistically uniform across race/ethnicity, partisanship, ideology, nativity, education, gender, and age. These results indicate that shared discrimination appeals broadly and reliably activate solidarity across diverse PoC subgroups, further affirming this psychological mechanism’s crucial role in fostering robust multiracial coalitions.

Violence Against Feminist Advocates: Political Violence and Anti-Gender Politics

Uxía Reboiro-Río, Maria Rodó-Zárate

Full text
Research on violence against women in politics (VAWIP) and research on violence against women activists have made crucial contributions by conceptualizing violence as a gendered phenomenon that targets women in response to their political participation. In these approaches, violence operates as a mechanism to discipline, silence, or expel women from political and public life. Alongside these developments, a growing body of scholarship has examined anti-gender politics as a structured and transnational political project, paying particular attention to its actors, discourses, and strategies.

VAWIP in a State-Monopolized Political Field: The Case of Chinese Feminist Activism

Lü Pin

Full text
The literature on violence against women in politics (VAWIP) implicitly treats such violence as boundary enforcement: a political field already exists; the violence women encounter marks the edge they are not permitted to cross; and the state stands as the guarantor of protection and redress under the rule of law, whether or not it delivers (Krook 2020; Krook and Restrepo Sanín2016). In high-capacity authoritarian states like China (Johnston 2019), the premise of this framing does not hold. The political field itself is monopolized and policed by the state, which determines what counts as politics, who is recognized as a legitimate political subject, and what forms of political action are permitted.

Compliant Women, Constitutive Violence: Rethinking the VAWIP Counterfactual

Anya Kuteleva

Full text
The literature on violence against women in politics (VAWIP) has made substantial contributions to understanding how gender shapes political exclusion. Foundational work by Krook and Restrepo Sanín (2016; 2020) defined VAWIP as a form of hate crime and as systematic backlash against women’s entry into political life. This conceptual scaffolding informs Krook’s (2022) subsequent attention to semiotic and symbolic harm, extending the VAWIP framework beyond physical violence to encompass the representational strategies through which female politicians are delegitimized. Bjarnegård and Zetterberg’s (2023) edited volume extends the framework further, recognizing that gender-based political violence operates across a broader range of contexts and along a “psychological-physical continuum” (7) that is neither temporally nor institutionally confined to election cycles or legislative chambers.

Triadic Victimization in Violence Against Women in Politics: Repairing Justice and Restoring Democracy in Japan

Mari Miura

Full text
Violence against women in politics (VAWIP) is a widespread phenomenon that deters women from fully engaging with political activities, particularly from running for office. Not only office seekers but also a broader range of women actors, such as journalists, human rights defenders, and civil servants, experience sexual harassment and assault perpetrated by political actors (Krook 2020). While much of the literature on violence against women in politics focuses on the incidence of such violence and policy solutions, this article broadens the analytical scope to theorize how institutional responses to redress harms may not resolve but instead exacerbate violence through further layers of victimization.

Community-minded communists: A field experiment on party member propensity for community activism in rural Vietnam

Paul Schuler, Eitan Paul, Quynh Nguyen, Markus Taussig, Mai Truong

Full text
In single-party and dominant-party regimes, do party members and non-party members at the grassroots level respond differently to party-linked collective action? This paper examines how different framings of party-linked collective action shape participation among party members and non-party members in Vietnam – one of the world’s most enduring single-party regimes – using a preregistered field experiment involving more than 7000 rural women. Contrary to assumptions that party members engage in party-linked collective action for selective or ideological reasons, we find that party members are also more responsive to collective, community-oriented framings. Using a field experiment and interviews, we show that grassroots party members are more community-minded than their non-party counterparts. These findings suggest that, in addition to careerism or ideological loyalty in authoritarian party membership, altruistic and community-minded engagement is an important additional dimension. The presence of community-oriented grassroots party members has important implications for the legitimacy and durability of dominant-party regimes.

Re-electing the party leader: The 2024 PASOK contest

Panos Koliastasis, Andrew Denham

Full text
This article examines leadership contests in European mainstream parties confronting electoral decline and attempting to restore competitiveness. Focusing on this broader phenomenon, it analyses incumbent leaders seeking renewed mandates through open primary elections in such contexts. The article applies Stark’s (1996) selection criteria (‘acceptability’, ‘electability’ and ‘competence’) to the 2024 Panhellenic Socialist Movement (PASOK) leadership contest in Greece as a demanding empirical and methodological setting: an incumbent leader seeking re-election through a competitive open primary within a recovery major party after only limited electoral improvement. Drawing on candidates’ speeches, press reports, official results and polling indicators mapped onto Stark’s criteria, the study evaluates party members’ and supporters’ perceptions of the main contenders. The findings show that the incumbent leader, Nikos Androulakis, led his opponents on all three criteria and suggest that incumbent re-election in recovery major parties is broadly consistent with Stark’s model when electability is interpreted as credible electoral recovery and unity concerns stem from strategic rather than ideological conflict.

How preferential voting and party ideology shape campaign-focus in proportional electoral systems

Julius Lehtinen, Mikko Mattila, Åsa von Schoultz

Full text
While electoral systems shape candidates’ incentives to cultivate personal reputations, institutional rules do not translate mechanically into campaign behaviour. We argue that both electoral institutions and ideological traditions shape campaign strategies: ideological traditions influence both the attractiveness of candidate-centred appeals and how strongly candidates respond to institutional incentives. We present the first large cross-national test of their joint influence, using data from 37 elections in 16 countries under proportional representation. We show that candidate-centred campaigning is more prevalent in open list systems, but that this institutional effect is not uniform. Candidates from leftleaning parties are more party-centred, and the personalising effect of open lists is weaker among them than among candidates from right leaning parties. These findings show that the consequences of electoral reform depend on the normative and strategic contexts in which parties operate.

Political Research Quarterly

Delegating Away Democracy? Martial Law Support in South Korea

Brandon Beomseob Park, Yuko Sato

Full text
Why do citizens sometimes accept undemocratic actions by political leaders? Building on growing debates about citizens’ democratic attitudes, we examine whether support for executive power grabs is associated not only with citizens’ partisan alignment but also with their perceptions of government performance and animosity toward political opponents. South Korea’s sudden declaration of martial law in December 2024 offers a unique real-world case for examining this question. Using original survey data collected 1 month after the incident from 1,500 respondents, we find that individuals with positive views of the economy, particularly incumbent supporters, are more likely to endorse the stated justifications for martial law. Hostility toward the opposition is also associated with higher endorsement among incumbent supporters and with a weaker observed relationship between economic evaluations and democratic accountability. Our findings suggest that even in established democracies, public support for democratic norms may be fragile in times of crisis—especially under deep polarization and when citizens view the executive as effective.

Congressional Oversight Reports as Legislative Branch Work Product and Research Resource

Grace A. Moore, David Rapallo, Sean M. Theriault

Full text
The Levin Center for Oversight and Democracy at Wayne State University Law School is in the process of creating the first‐ever comprehensive database of congressional oversight reports for all House and Senate Committees since the 106th Congress (1999–2000). European Union Politics

Echoes of the public: Public opinion and subnational compliance with European environmental directives

Marie-Thérèse Wilhelm

Full text
This study investigates whether public opinion shapes compliance with European environmental directives. Drawing on an original dataset of subnational infringements from 2005 to 2018 across 319 subnational authorities in 20 member states, the analysis links variation in public opinion to compliance using mixed-effects logistic regression models. The results show that subnational public opinion does not exert an independent effect on the likelihood of infringement. Instead, national environmental attitudes emerge as consistently relevant for subnational compliance, with subnational authorities in member states that are less supportive of environmental protection significantly more likely to infringe European Union legislation. Subnational environmental attitudes matter where national environmental support is weak. These findings demonstrate that responsiveness in multilevel governance is conditional upon the interaction between national and subnational public opinion.

PS: Political Science & Politics

Where Are the Most Influential Articles in Political Science Published?

Logan Strother

Full text
Where are the most influential articles in political science published? Drawing on citation data from the Web of Science, this article identifies the most-cited political science journal articles each decade from the 1940s through the 2020s. The results depict a changing discipline: the American Political Science Review still leads the field, but its once-dominant position has attenuated as a wider range of journals have become homes for highly cited work. Methodological papers now account for a substantial share of the discipline’s most influential research, reflecting the growing centrality of technical innovation.

“To Hell or to Connacht”: Expropriation, Inequality, and Local Legacies of Settler Colonialism

Jeremy Bowles, Gabriel Koehler-Derrick, Michael Olson

Full text
Violent upheavals in landownership following conquest often change not only the concentration of land across individuals, but also the inequality of its distribution between groups. We argue that variation in landowners’ group identity has important implications for the provision of local public goods. We test this claim by studying the aftermath of the Cromwellian Settlement in seventeenth-century Ireland. Leveraging the lottery-based allocation of expropriated Catholic land to distinct groups of English Protestants, we find significant local variation in the extent of Protestant landownership. Drawing on rich original data, we find fewer local public goods in the long run in areas with more Protestant elites, with the largest reductions in goods that were either directly elite-funded or disproportionately benefited the Catholic majority. The results illustrate how conquest reshapes not just institutions but also long-run local inequalities, and highlight the distinct consequences of between-group inequality for the provision of public goods.

Rebel party stigmatization: measuring the political price of a rebel past

María Ignacia Curiel, Cyrus Samii, Mateo Vásquez-Cortés

Full text
The long-term electoral viability of rebel parties depends, in part, on acceptance of them as legitimate political actors. Yet civilians often punish perpetrators of past violence at the ballot box and conflict generates long-term antipathies that can impede democratic integration of former rebels. This paper offers a theoretical conceptualization of rebel party stigmatization and uses an experimental approach in Colombia, informed by interviews on the ground, to clarify the conditions under which stigmatization can be a relevant barrier for rebel parties. First, in semi-structured interviews, rebel party leaders and members uniformly claim that civilian stigmatization of the party is pervasive. Second, we design an endorsement experiment to measure rebel party stigmatization, whether the penalty is distinct from assessments based on ideology or platform, and for whom stigma is strongest. Civilians penalize policies and statements endorsed by the rebel party even when compared to endorsement by ideologically similar parties, indicative of stigmatization. Such rebel party stigmatization is especially strong among two types of citizens. First, citizens who otherwise share a similar ideology with the rebel party but rejected violence are the most stigmatizing, due to a political victimization effect. Second, citizens who did not live in communities with substantial FARC governance and presence are more stigmatizing—those who lived in rebelocracies do not penalize rebel parties but also do not support them strongly.

Do protest bans make police repression more acceptable? Experimental evidence on the effects of legal repression from Turkey

Mert Arslanalp, Ulaş Erdoğdu, T Deniz Erkmen

Full text
In explaining whether protest repression incites or suppresses mobilization, research on the repression–dissent nexus has underlined the importance of how the public views protests or their repression. This literature, however, generally does not differentiate between various forms of protest repression, examining primarily police violence; nor does it account for possible interactions between these various forms of repression. In this paper, emphasizing the varied and multilayered nature of repression, we posit that legal forms of repression can legitimize police repression in the eyes of the public. Using a 2 × 3 vignette survey experiment in Turkey, we find that the declaration of a protest ban prior to a protest increases public approval of subsequent police repression and more forceful forms of repression. This effect varies by protest issue, gender, and individuals' predisposition to comply with state decisions. Contrary to our expectations, however, it does not vary by the partisan identity of respondents. Using original data from an authoritarian context, this article contributes to the literature on the interplay between protest repression and public opinion by showing that legal repression operates not only through deterrence but also by generating public support for subsequent police repression.

Ceasefires and civilian targeting in civil war

Corinne Bara, Maurice P Schumann

Full text
Civilian suffering in civil wars regularly triggers calls for ceasefires, but do ceasefires actually stop the killing of civilians? Civilians account for a significant share of fatalities in contemporary civil wars, yet it is unclear whether and under which conditions ceasefires alleviate civilian suffering. We argue that ceasefires indeed decrease the intentional targeting of civilians by changing expectations and heightening scrutiny of belligerents’ conduct, which raises the risk of audience costs. To test this argument, we combine global ceasefire data with matching and difference in differences estimators. We find that ceasefires reduce civilian targeting on average but this effect is conditional on support by third parties. When a belligerent is backed by foreign governments, especially liberal democratic ones, restraint toward civilians increases under ceasefire. Additional analyses show that international attention to the conflict country spikes in the month a ceasefire is concluded, highlighting the importance of attentive international audiences for audience costs to materialize. These findings underscore the substantial influence external supporters can exert in enforcing norms of civilian protection and mitigating harm in civil wars.

Do refugee camps impact hosts' security perceptions? Evidence from Africa

Kerstin Fisk, Siri Aas Rustad

Full text
Previous research argues that negative externalities associated with hosting refugee camps can lead to insecurity. But do hosts living in districts with refugee camps generally feel less secure than those living in districts with no camps? To investigate whether the presence or size of encamped refugee populations affect hosts' perceived safety, we utilize geographically referenced survey data from Afrobarometer alongside information on the sizes and locations of refugee camps at the district level. To account for theoretically plausible mediating effects, we also examine the influence of several dimensions of state capacity on security perceptions. We find no general effect of hosting a refugee camp on local-level security perceptions, regardless of camp population size. Rather, our results show that refugee camp hosts feel safer from violence and crime when the state's security and administrative capacity are strong, but not otherwise. Our findings help illuminate the factors that contribute to individuals' opinion formation regarding their security, demonstrating that state-building can act as a significant mediator of security perceptions in countries that host forced migrants.

Civilian compliance and victimization in the Boko Haram insurgency

Imrana Buba

Full text
How does variation in civilian compliance strategies shape wartime victimization? Across civil wars, some contested communities are displaced while others remain in place despite exposure to armed actors. Existing research explains civilian victimization primarily through armed actors' strategies, with limited attention to how civilians shape its form. I argue that civilian compliance, defined as accommodating the demands of armed actors without formally joining them, is a self-protection strategy that can reduce immediate danger but shifts civilians' exposure toward different forms of victimization. Drawing on fieldwork and a subnational comparison of two contested communities in northeastern Nigeria, I show that variation in civilian compliance shapes how armed actors interpret civilian behavior. Fragmented compliance, in which civilians selectively comply with the actor they perceive as most threatening, produces signals that rival forces interpret as support for the opposing side, prompting punitive operations and displacement. By contrast, dual compliance, in which civilians accommodate both rebels and state forces while presenting compliance as exclusive to each, creates behavioral ambiguity. Lacking clear evidence that civilians assist their rival, armed actors tolerate civilian presence and rely instead on surveillance and coercive extraction. The findings show that patterns of civilian compliance shape armed actors' use of violence, producing displacement in some communities and in situ victimization in others. By identifying civilian compliance as the behavioral signal through which armed actors infer civilian support, the article nuances existing explanations of variation in wartime victimization.

Guardians of the regime: secret police formation in autocracies

Marius Mehrl, Mila Pfander, Theresa Winner, Cornelius Fritz

Full text
Autocrats use secret police to stay in power, deterring and suppressing opposition to their rule. Existing research suggests that secret police succeed at this but also that they are not as ubiquitous as one may assume. We thus explore under which conditions secret police emerge in dictatorships. We develop a theoretical framework for potential predictors and apply statistical variable selection techniques to identify which of several candidate variables extracted from the literature on state security forces and authoritarian survival hold explanatory power. Our results suggest that secret police are more likely to emerge where rulers face structural, regime-external threats, such as organized anti-system mobilization and international rivals, witness successful regime-internal contestation abroad, and have sufficient material resources and personalized power to establish such organizations. This research contributes to our understanding of autocrats’ institutional choices and authoritarian politics.

Getting Religion Wrong A Response to Toft’s “Getting Religion Right”

Nils-Christian Bormann, Lars-Erik Cederman, Manuel Vogt

Full text
In a recent contribution to the Journal of Conflict Resolution , Monica Toft presents new data and analysis that suggest a rise in religious civil war, and question our findings on linguistic and religious cleavages' effect on armed conflict onset. We welcome Toft’s contribution, in particular her replication of our results, and the very well documented new dataset on civil wars. Yet we disagree with several of Toft’s specific claims and raise three larger questions about the study of religion and armed conflict. First, what is the relevant counterfactual when analyzing the religion-conflict link? Second, how useful is it to classify entire conflicts as religious? Third, what is the mechanism that links religion and conflict? Revisiting her analysis and adding new empirical evidence, we show that Toft fails to undermine our earlier results and does not find support for her own theoretical mechanism of religious outbidding in the Muslim world.

Class of origin and voting: The activating role of volunteering during youth

Clara Weißenfels, Leo Azzollini

Full text
Differences in political participation between social classes are well documented, yet less is known about the mechanisms that may reduce these gaps. This article adopts a life-course perspective to investigate whether youth volunteering can mitigate class-based inequalities in electoral participation. Using longitudinal data from Germany, we analyse how class of origin affects voting for the newly enfranchised, how volunteering influences turnout, and how both factors interact. Our findings show that, while individuals from working-class backgrounds are less likely to vote and youth volunteering increases electoral participation for all, volunteering during youth nearly closes the participation gap linked to social origin and uplifts specifically blue-collar working-class children’s electoral participation. These results suggest that voluntary engagement in youth can serve as a “school of democracy,” reducing class-based inequalities in political participation starting at an early age, which remain persistent otherwise.

Examining US–China strategic competition: The case of China’s global initiatives at the United Nations Secretariat

Courtney J. Fung

Full text
The well-reported multilateral rise of the People’s Republic of China (PRC or China) is presumed to challenge US leadership in a Western-dominated United Nations (UN) system. Among China’s many multilateral contributions are its advance of “Global Initiatives,” which China frames as “global public goods.” This paper applies sociological interpretations of power, specifically the Barnett & Duvall 2005 typology, to investigate the different strategies used by China to seed three of these Global Initiatives within and upon the UN Secretariat: the Global Development Initiative, the Global Security Initiative, and the Global Civilization Initiative. While these Global Initiatives are still in their infancy, the research finds variation in how China is approaching each Initiative within the UN system and that these “global public goods” are sites of US–China strategic competition. The research draws from primary and secondary sources produced by PRC and UN elites and foreign policy researchers, and is supplemented by interviews of officials of the PRC, the United States, and the United Nations.

From Bombay Street to Ballymena: The repertoires of sectarian violence and 21st-century racism

José A. Gutiérrez, Matthew Whiting, Shannon Doherty

Full text
During June 2025, Ballymena was the epicentre of some of the most serious riots witnessed recently in Northern Ireland. For many days, mobs attacked migrants’ properties and battled the police. These riots soon extended to other areas, the youths involved in the rioting coming overwhelmingly from working-class Loyalist strongholds. We argue that these riots embodied a familiar repertoire of bottom-up Loyalist, non-lethal violence against civilians, shaped by historical sectarianism. We compare the dynamics of the 1969 Bombay Street burnings and the Ballymena riots, demonstrating the persistence of repertoires of violence anchored in sectarianism but assuming new forms in contemporary racism. Although the target group in each instance is different, the repertoire, technique, performative and symbolic dimensions, and underlying ideology of the violence have many continuities, including an effort to cleanse a territory of people seen as undesirable to assert an identity-based control over it, indicating the depth of Loyalist resolve.

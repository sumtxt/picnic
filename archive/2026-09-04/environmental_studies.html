Annual Review of Environment and Resources

Philanthropic Foundations in Environmental Governance

Rebecca L. Gruby, Michele M. Betsill, Xavier Basurto, Jeffrey E. Blackwatters, Ash Enrici, Dustin Garrick, Agni Kalfagianni, Daniel C. Miller, Anne Monier

Full text
This review examines the involvement of philanthropic foundations in environmental governance. Foundation funding for environmental issues has surged in recent years, elevating the role of philanthropic actors in shaping environmental agendas, policies, organizations, and discourses globally. Foundations remain understudied in the literature on multi-actor environmental governance, however, with implications for both research and practice. Increased research attention to foundations as environmental governance actors is especially needed amid growth in green giving, shrinking public environmental funding, and intensifying scrutiny of the philanthropic sector. The review thus outlines trends in funding flows from foundations for environmental issues, conceptualizes foundations as environmental governance actors, highlights existing research on foundations’ environmental governance roles and legitimacy, and addresses methodological challenges for research on philanthropy. We conclude with a call to action for increased research and engagement between environmental governance scholars and philanthropic foundations to both inform environmental philanthropy as a field of practice and enhance environmental governance scholarship.

Urban Informality and Climate Change: Precarity, Governance, and Pathways to Resilience

David Corbett, Pourya Salehi, Debra C. Roberts, Marcus Foth, Maryke van Staden, Aloke Barnwal, Asad Asadzadeh

Full text
Climate change is worsening long-standing inequalities for people living and working in cities’ informal systems, where exposure to heat, flooding, and other hazards intersects with insecure land rights, weak infrastructure, and unstable livelihoods. Using an informality-first approach, this review considers informal housing, economies, and governance not as leftover categories but as essential modes of urbanization and citizenship that define who is protected from climate risk. We review recent research on climate impacts, daily and community-based adaptation, and nature-based solutions in informal settings and combine this with long-standing debates on informality, marginality, and urban governance. The evidence shows that climate change acts as a threat multiplier: It adds stress to informal systems due to climate-induced migration to cities; worsens structural insecurity; and can turn well-meaning adaptation into maladaptation through eviction, green-led displacement, and shifting adaptation burdens onto already overburdened residents. The review suggests that resilience in informal settlements relies less on technical fixes and more on governance structures that recognize informal systems, share authority, and institutionalize coproduction across local, regional, and national levels. Middle-out governance and multilevel governance are crucial for aligning top-down resources with bottom-up knowledge and claims to the city. Ultimately, climate adaptation will either reinforce or challenge urban inequality, depending on whether it treats informality as a core, rather than a peripheral, part of modern urban life.

De‐Risking or Dividing? Jurisdictional Carbon Markets and the Politics of Risk Management

Sasha Maher, Rakhyun E. Kim

Full text
Jurisdictional approaches under Article 6.2 of the Paris Agreement are rapidly shaping global carbon markets. By linking emissions mitigation activities to state action and broader policy frameworks, these approaches are often presented as a way to address some of the integrity and ethical concerns associated with project‐based carbon crediting. Yet the risk of not delivering committed credit volumes persists regardless of scale, governance actor, or institutional structure. To manage this credit delivery risk, financial products such as carbon insurance are increasingly viewed as critical. This paper argues that private performance insurance should not be understood as a neutral technical fix. While it may help manage some delivery risks, it can also create new forms of inequity by making coverage more costly, restrictive, or unavailable for jurisdictions deemed “higher risk”. These jurisdictions may also be those most in need of climate finance. Private insurance should therefore be considered only as part of a broader risk management architecture that includes public or hybrid insurance mechanisms, carbon buffers, temporary crediting, and direct investment in risk reduction. Considered policy design is essential to ensure Article 6.2 initiatives and jurisdictional carbon markets promote inclusive, sustainable, and effective climate outcomes.

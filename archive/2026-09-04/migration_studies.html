International Migration Review

Social Class and Forced Migrants’ Secondary/Onward Mobility Decision-Making: Syrians in Turkey and Lebanon

Sinem Kavak, Mo Hamza

Full text
This article aims first to understand how and why refugees decide to leave or stay in their first country of asylum, and second to contribute to the theory of agentic analyses of migration. Our contribution adds to the growing body of research examining the impact of social class and associated capitals on the trajectories of forced migrants. Our analysis of the experiences of Syrians in Turkey and Lebanon demonstrates that social class is a critical factor in shaping their experiences in their first countries of asylum. We assert that social class has a significant impact on their future mobility and immobility decisions. By using a Bourdieusian conceptualisation of capitals, habitus and social space and relying on in-depth interviews, we analyse the types of capital that forced migrants possess, their convertibility and their chances of re-establishing their class status. We show that convertibility depends on the type of capital and its interaction with social space. The difference between pre- and post-displacement class status is a significant factor in determining decisions regarding onward mobility, along with the capacity for social reproduction. Our findings demonstrate that voluntary immobility may occur among those who have achieved similar livelihoods to their pre-displacement status and are content with their lives. Lower-income households that struggle to restore their livelihoods are more likely to engage in irregular migration or become trapped in involuntary immobility.

The Societal Determinants of Climate Risk for Refugees: Evidence From Global and African Refugee Corridors

Sonja Fransen, Kerilyn Schewel

Full text
While a growing body of research examines the relationship between climate change and displacement in origin communities, less is known about the climate risks that displaced populations encounter in host areas. This study examines how climate risk (disaggregated into hazard exposure, vulnerability, and coping capacity) is reconfigured as refugees move from origin to host countries. We argue that the societal determinants of climate risk—in this analysis, the structural and institutional conditions of host and origin countries that mediate the consequences of hazard exposure—are at least as consequential as hazard exposure in shaping the climate risk environments in which displaced populations live. Drawing on the Climate-driven INFORM Risk Index and United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) Population Statistics from 2013 to 2022, we apply a migration corridor analysis globally and within Africa. We find a global rise in the number of refugees originating from high climate risk countries, and that 75 percent of global refugees reside in high or medium climate risk host countries. In Africa, displacement to neighboring or regional host countries shifts exposure to hazards but often not overall climate risk. as many refugees remain in countries characterized by high societal vulnerability and low coping capacity. Refugees who move intercontinentally typically face lower climate risks. Our findings demonstrate that cross-border displacement often reconfigures rather than alleviates climate risk, as the societal conditions that produce vulnerability prove far more persistent than patterns of hazard exposure.

Family Formation and Residential Mobility: How Do Fertility and Mobility Intentions Influence Residential Mobility?

Sarah Christison, Hill Kulu, Katherine Keenan, Francesca Fiori

Full text
In this study, we examine the association between fertility and mobility intentions, and residential mobility. Existing literature suggests a link between family formation and residential moves. However, these studies have not explored the interaction between fertility intentions and mobility intentions, or how they interact with micro‐level, individual factors such as current family size and housing context, or temporal effects that can serve as proxies for macro‐level contextual factors, such as changes in the housing market or economy. Utilising harmonised data from the British Household Panel Survey and the UK Household Longitudinal Survey, and employing event history analysis techniques, we investigate the interaction between the two life course domains of fertility and residential mobility. We consider not only how jointly held intentions influence the likelihood of a residential relocation, but also how the influence of these intentions varies across population subgroups and over time, particularly focusing on the effect of the 2008 financial crisis. We find that intending to have a(nother) child does increase the likelihood of a residential move; however, this association varies based on individual‐level characteristics and contextual factors. Intentions to have a child in the future are only positively associated with residential mobility for women intending a higher‐order birth and those who are married. Moreover, the positive association between fertility intentions and residential moves for mothers was observed only prior to the 2008 financial crisis. The financial crisis also affected the relationship between mobility intentions and the likelihood of a move, with individuals less likely to realise their mobility intentions following the financial crash and subsequent recession. This temporal effect suggests that people may be more limited in their ability to fulfil their mobility intentions under challenging economic conditions and unfavourable housing markets.

Migration Security and Regional Socio‐Economic Resilience in Ukraine: Dynamics, Differentiation and Implications

Vitaliy Krupin, Olha Mulska, Ihor Baranyak, Marta Kunytska‐Ilyash

Full text
Ukraine presents a critical case for understanding how migration processes shape regional socio‐economic resilience, yet no regionally explicit framework has previously linked internal and external migration security to territorial differentiation across its oblasts. This article addresses that gap by developing a composite regional indicator integrating internal and external migration security dimensions, applied to 24 oblasts over 2010–2021, that captures both the scale and directional influence of migration on regional resilience. Results reveal substantial differentiation that does not follow conventional macro‐regional lines: mean values range from 0.826 in Kyiv oblast to 0.421 in Zakarpattia, yet western and eastern industrial oblasts differ little as groups (0.606 and 0.630) relative to the spread within each, with Chernivtsi, a western border oblast, ranking second nationally and Ternopil, a western agrarian oblast, ninth. Level and temporal stability prove largely independent: the most volatile region, Kharkiv, moved repeatedly between the top and the bottom of the national distribution, while cross‐regional dispersion widened over the period, indicating divergence rather than uniform decline. Extended to 2022–2024, a separate contextual analysis drawing on aggregate official, IOM and UNHCR data examines how far this pre‐war geography corresponds to the uneven consequences of wartime displacement, with 10.5 million people having fled Ukraine and 3.7 million internally displaced by August 2024; the correspondence is partial, and this extension is interpretive rather than a formal causal test. The framework provides both a replicable measurement tool and an empirical baseline for post‐war regional recovery analysis.

Marital Status, Cohabitation and Intentions of Workers to Move to Less Developed Areas

Yuyao Liu, Linghan Ge, Chenxiao Wang, Eric Fong

Full text
While migration for ‘love in destinations’ has gained attention, the link between partnerships, beyond the married‐unmarried dichotomy, in home societies and migration intentions remains underexplored. Meanwhile, economies in the Global South are becoming attractive for talent, yet little is known about intentions of workers to migrate within this region. This study analyzes how marital status and cohabitation relate to Hong Kong workers’ intentions to move to less developed Mainland Chinese cities. Using survey data, it compares short‐term (within 5 years) and long‐term migration intentions across single, partnered non‐cohabiting, cohabiting and married workers. Short‐term intentions are higher among married or cohabiting men than single men. Long‐term intentions are stronger among married than single women. Family presence moderates these associations. Workers with non‐cohabitating partners do not differ from single counterparts. Findings highlighted the role of partnerships and living arrangements on migration intentions and contributed novel insights for theorizing educated migration.

Journal of Race, Ethnicity and Politics

Whose Team Are You On? Evaluating Public Response to Native American Policy Priorities

Elise N. Blasingame

Full text
Native American advocates and Tribal Nations have fought for decades against the use of Native imagery in sports mascots. In recent years, national sports franchises, colleges, and states have started responding to these demands by changing team names and iconography. As these policy changes have been framed as racial equity initiatives rather than matters of Native sovereignty, some professional teams continue to use Native imagery, and many states remain entrenched in debates over legislative mandates to remove such imagery from K-12 school sports. This project uses data from two survey experiments to examine how predominantly White fan bases respond to requests from tribal leaders, White allies, and partisan officials to support tribal policy priorities. Results indicate that the racial identity of the messenger significantly influences support for changing mascot practices, with Native messengers eliciting the strongest positive shifts in opinion.

The mentalizing network updates neural representations of romantic interest in response to social feedback

Benjamin Silver, Christopher Baldassano, Lila Davachi, Kevin Ochsner

Full text
In what ways are social evaluations of others interdependent with what we think they believe about us? In the current study, we were interested in how social feedback changes how we think about a potential romantic partner, as well as how often we think about them. We hypothesized that the mentalizing network would play a role in both of these processes, given that we consider both how we feel about someone and how we think they feel about us when evaluating romantic interest. During an fMRI scan, participants watched dating profile videos of eight different targets, and assessed each target on romantic interest. Participants watched two videos of each target: one before receiving social feedback from the target, and one after. Participants also completed a resting state scan after viewing each set of eight videos. We found that the mentalizing network played a role in forming and updating evaluations of romantic interest. Specifically, neural representations in this network responded most strongly to social feedback that was incongruent with one’s initial evaluation, likely because our romantic interest in another person is at least partially dependent on how we believe they feel about us. Within the mentalizing network, the right TPJ and the dmPFC most strongly represented romantic interest at the beginning of an encounter. In addition, representations in the TPJ and the Precuneus changed more in response to incongruent feedback, while reactivation frequency in the TPJ changed more in response to congruent feedback, further demonstrating the importance of interdependence in social evaluations.

Digital Transformation Capabilities and Supply Chain Finance Resilience: An Integrated AI–Blockchain–Analytics Framework

Dr Jainish Bhagat

Full text
Supply chain finance (SCF) plays a pivotal role in maintaining liquidity and operational continuity across global value networks. However, systemic supply chain disruptions, macroeconomic volatility, and information asymmetry frequently expose SCF programs to severe friction and default risks. While digital transformation is widely touted as a catalyst for supply chain resilience, empirical evidence regarding the explicit mechanisms through which distinct digital transformation capabilities enhance Supply Chain Finance Resilience (SCFR) remains fragmented. Grounded in the Resource-Based View (RBV), Dynamic Capabilities Theory (DCT), and Information Processing Theory (IPT), this study develops and tests an integrated framework evaluating the direct and indirect impacts of Artificial Intelligence Capability (AIC), Blockchain Capability (BC), and Data Analytics Capability (DAC) on SCFR, mediated by Digital Trust in SCF Platforms (DT). Using a computational research simulation methodology, a respondent-level dataset (N=500) representing supply chain, finance, operations, and IT decision-makers across international enterprises was algorithmically generated under a defensible latent-variable covariance structure. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples was executed to evaluate the measurement and structural models. The structural analysis reveals that AIC (β=0.241,p<.001), BC (β=0.312,p<.001), and DAC (β=0.284,p<.001) significantly and positively drive Digital Trust in SCF Platforms, explaining 54.2% of its variance (R^2=0.542). Digital Trust, in turn, exerts a substantial direct effect on SCFR (β=0.385,p<.001). Furthermore, direct effects on SCFR were confirmed for DAC (β=0.218,p<.001) and AIC (β=0.152,p=.002), whereas the direct link from BC to SCFR was non-significant (β=0.071,p=.158). Formal mediation testing using percentile bootstrapping confirmed that Digital Trust fully mediates the relationship between Blockchain Capability and SCFR, while partially mediating the relationships for AIC and DAC. The overall structural model accounts for 58.6% of the variance in Supply Chain Finance Resilience (R^2=0.586,Q_"predict" ^2=0.412). This methodological prototype advances theoretical understanding by unpacking the granular capability configurations necessary to foster digital trust and financial resilience in supply networks. For practitioners and policymakers, the findings highlight that investing in blockchain technology yields minimal resilience benefits unless coupled with platform-wide digital trust mechanisms, whereas AI and analytics offer dual-pathway benefits across operational and relational domains.

Estimating Regional Industry Employment from Economic Location Mechanisms: A Constrained Spatial Disaggregation for European Geographies

Riccardo Boero

Full text
Fine-scale employment by industry is largely unavailable across European municipalities, yet it underpins regional economic analysis, environmental-economic extensions, and the regionalization of input--output accounts. This paper presents a mechanism-driven, constrained spatial disaggregation framework that estimates industry employment down to the Local Administrative Unit level while preserving official totals exactly at every geographical level. The economic mechanisms that shape where activity locates --- settlement, infrastructure and accessibility, natural-resource endowments, and inter-industry co-location --- are represented as a reproducible input dataset assembled from official and remote-sensing sources; a family of constrained allocation models, combined by a cross-validated convex ensemble, is trained on data-rich United States geographies and transferred to Europe under official Eurostat totals as constraints. Validated against independent national statistics in Italy, France, Germany, and Spain, the estimates reproduce the observed spatial distribution of employment within the held-out uncertainty of the models themselves, and the error does not inflate down the four-step disaggregation. The framework is open, inspectable, and reproducible, providing internally consistent fine-scale employment estimates wherever sub-national industry data are sparse.

The Paperwork of Grief: Administrative Burden as a Defence Against Death Exposure in Clinical Care

Chukwudi Egbuonu

Full text
Administrative work (documentation, the electronic record, the proliferation of meetings, the referral of distress onward to specialists) is treated almost universally in the clinician-wellbeing literature as a cause of burnout to be measured and reduced. This paper argues that in the most death-saturated settings of medicine (the cancer ward, the intensive care unit, the palliative service) that same burden may also do something the literature has not noticed: function as a social defence against sustained exposure to dying, offering clinicians a bounded, completable, finite task to retreat into when the bedside offers only problems that often end in death. The argument revives Isabel Menzies Lyth’s 1959 account of hospital routine as a collective defence against anxiety and brings it forward to the era of the electronic record, joining four literatures (on documentation burden, on death exposure and numbing, on terror management, and on the social defences of institutions) that have not previously been read together. If the conjecture holds, the prevailing prescription to strip administration away on efficiency grounds alone risks removing an unrecognised container without supplying a deliberate one, and the proper task is not to reduce paperwork but to distinguish what merely numbs from what genuinely contains, and to design containment for those who work among the dead. The claim is interpretive and bounded to high-mortality settings; the paper sets out how it could be tested and the competing explanations it complements rather than displaces.

When Does Generative AI Become a Developmental Resource? A Multilevel Taxonomy of Enactment Conditions

Andreea Nicolau, Sonja Rispens, Pascale Marie Le Blanc, Kees Maton, Piet van Gool, Ad Kleingeld

Full text
Generative AI (GenAI) creates a developmental ambiguity for organizations: it can support learning, reflection, and judgment, but it can also accelerate task completion while reducing engagement with the cognitive and social processes through which capabilities develop. This study asks which multilevel conditions shape whether GenAI is enacted as a resource for learning and continuous development or as a shortcut for task completion. Drawing on an inductive, multi-stakeholder qualitative study with 41 organizational actors, we use thematic analysis to identify empirical categories and morphological principles to organize them into a multidimensional taxonomy of GenAI enactment. The taxonomy identifies four interrelated dimensions: Organizational Conditions, Capability Conditions, Decision Drivers, and GenAI Enactment. Interpreting these findings through the automation–augmentation paradox, we show that automation- and augmentation-oriented uses are not fixed properties of technologies or tasks but contextually produced orientations shaped by an iterative system of societal/institutional, organizational, and individual-related conditions. The study contributes to AI and management research by specifying the condition architecture through which GenAI becomes more or less developmental in practice. We argue that learning-oriented GenAI integration requires attention to the multilevel conditions that preserve verification, reflection, feedback, and accountable judgment when task completion no longer necessarily develops capability.

Climate policy is domestic politics: Mechanisms shaping the political feasibility of mitigation

E. Keith Smith, Christina Eder, Sarah Gomm, Gabriele Spilker, Isabelle Stadelmann-Steffen

Full text
The Paris Agreement shifted the central challenge of climate mitigation from international burden-sharing to domestic politics. In high-emitting countries, the binding constraint on near-term emissions cuts is no longer the absence of effective policy instruments, but their political feasibility, that is, governments' capacity and willingness to adopt, implement, and sustain mitigation policy over time. We review the political feasibility of climate mitigation along four dimensions: stakeholder interests and influence, public opinion and social norms, political-economic context and institutional constraints, and distributional effects and inequality. Using the policymaking process as our analytical lens, we trace how these dimensions hinder or enable mitigation at successive stages of agenda-setting, formulation, and implementation. We synthesise this literature into a practical framework for making mitigation politically feasible, identify a set of persistent misconceptions that obstruct progress, and outline near-term actions that the evidence supports. The central challenge of mitigation is no longer identifying effective instruments, but making them politically feasible under real-world conditions.

Leveraging LLMs to assist tourists at destinations – The opportunities and risks of deploying open-source humanoid robots

Yash Pandey, Spencer Kayser, Marius Portmann, Siamak Layeghy, Sara Dolnicar

Full text
LLMs are transforming tourist search behaviour. Tourists currently access LLMs on phones, tablets, and laptops. Open-source humanoid robots change this: the LLM becomes embodied and physically situated at the destination. This conceptual paper develops a framework for understanding the consequences of deploying open-source humanoid robots in tourism. The framework rests on two constructs. Embodied authority transfer describes how tourists reattribute LLM output from a private planning tool to the destination's official service infrastructure when delivered by a robot hosted in a tourism setting. The article discusses deployment scenarios and risk, and offers a detailed research agenda.

Corruption in Nepal's Local Governments: A Case Study of Institutional Weaknesses and Citizen Outcomes

Nirmal Gurung

Full text
This case study deals with corruption in public procurement at the local government level in Nepal. Nepal established 753 local governments with substantial responsibility for public spending, including procurement of goods, works and services, following the adoption of the Constitution in 2015. Mostly today, public spending is the responsibility of local governments, especially on capital investment in infrastructure, such as roads, schools, health facilities and water supply systems. But the speed of devolution of responsibilities has not been matched by adequate systems of transparency, accountability and integrity. This paper explores the nature and extent of corruption in local government procurement in Nepal, drawing on publicly available data from the Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority, Transparency International, the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank and government audit reports. The analysis shows that procurement is a high-risk area for corruption at the local level. Corrupt practices are many and include bid-rigging, inflated costs, shoddy work, kickbacks, collusion among bidders, political interference with contract awards, etc. The data show that many local governments do not follow procurement rules well, publish limited information and have weak oversight. Audit reports frequently find irregularities in procurement by local government. The paper investigates factors leading to procurement corruption including weak institutional capacity, political interference, lack of transparency, limited oversight by citizens and poor enforcement. The report ends with recommendations for improving procurement integrity, including better procurement rules, more transparency, capacity building, stronger oversight, more citizen participation, and accountability enforcement.

Beyond Blame: School Violence, Youth Despair, and the Failure of Containment in Recent Philippine Shootings

Rhine Lesther Tague

Full text
Recent school-related shootings in the Philippines, especially the June 2026 attack at San Jose National High School in Tacloban and the August 2026 shooting at Ateneo de Zamboanga University, generated public pressure to locate a single cause: a teacher, a game, a security failure, a disturbed child, a family failure. This paper argues that such explanations are emotionally understandable but causally insufficient. Drawing on incident reporting, official and reported-official statements, verified WHO Global School-based Student Health Survey data, DepEd Region VIII memoranda, public-health and threat-assessment literature, and C. G. Jung's Modern Man in Search of a Soul, the paper proposes a different representation: recent Philippine school violence should be understood as a failed ecology of containment. In this frame, adolescent grievance, despair, humiliation, or rupture become dangerous when they converge with firearm access, missed warning signs, weak disclosure pathways, digital scripts or grooming environments, livestream/notoriety affordances, and institutions that are not yet able to metabolize distress before it turns into public violence. The argument does not claim that Jungian "spiritual crisis" is a forensic motive. Rather, Jung helps name the meaning-layer of youth collapse: psychic pain, futurelessness, shame, and disconnection are real in their effects even when they cannot be reduced to one external cause. The paper concludes that prevention must move beyond blame toward containment: firearm accountability, threat assessment, confidential reporting, anti-bullying systems, psychosocial support, digital-platform safeguards, and living forms of youth meaning and belonging.

Staying Long Enough to Stop Seeing: Autoethnography, Long-Term Immersion, and Tourism Research

Rob Burton

Full text
Autoethnographic tourism research has typically been based on discrete journeys or bounded periods of fieldwork, leaving the epistemological implications of long-term residential immersion comparatively underexamined. This article argues that sustained residence produces a distinctive form of knowledge grounded not simply in prolonged observation but in the transformation of perception over time. Drawing on more than a decade of living and working across Nanjing, Hangzhou, and Wenzhou, China, it proposes a three-stage framework of estrangement, normalisation, and critical re-estrangement. Initial unfamiliarity heightens attention; repeated exposure subsequently renders once-remarkable features ordinary and increasingly invisible; disciplined analytical attention can then return the researcher to these normalised environments with a capacity to recognise recurrence, pattern, and institutional continuity. Through examples of everyday urban landscapes and the seasonal infrastructure underlying Hangzhou's elevated-highway flower displays, the article demonstrates how long-term immersion makes visible processes that shorter encounters may register only as isolated events or spectacles. Critical re-estrangement therefore offers a methodological means of converting habituation from an apparent limitation of long-term residence into an analytical resource.

Detention as a disabling institution: Age-related health disparities in the US immigration detention system

Caitlin Patler

Full text
From 2008 to 2018, US Immigration and Customs Enforcement apprehended over two million noncitizens in the US interior. However, data on detained immigrants' health is limited, with a particular lack of research on older detained immigrants, despite the distinct vulnerabilities aging individuals confront within and outside carceral settings. Immigrants face diverse structural challenges as they age, and incarcerated individuals experience accelerated aging and have worse health relative to non-incarcerated individuals, due to exposure to structural disadvantage, coupled with the health harms of incarceration itself. Using data from the Immigrant Detention Survey (n=495) and the National Health Interview Survey (n=8,383), and drawing on theories of aging in carceral settings, immigrant health and aging, and immigration policies as structural determinants of health, I address two critical and unresolved questions: What is the health profile of older detained adults, relative to younger adults? How do age-related health disparities among detained immigrants compare to those of non-detained immigrants? My findings reveal age-related health disparities in detained and non-detained populations. I also find much higher rates of poor health among detained immigrants than in the non-detained population, regardless of age. Results suggest immigration detention may be considered a mass disabling institution linked to poor health across the life course and may exacerbate age-related disparities, highlighting the need for more humane and equitable immigration and health policies.

A Foot in the Door: Ethnic Employment Quotas and Democratic Attitudes in Uruguay

Jorge Soler-Lopez

Full text
Does bringing a marginalized group into the state raise its members' trust in democratic institutions? Uruguay's 2013 Ley 19.122, reserving eight percent of public-sector hires for Afro-descendant citizens, offers a rare test on the dimension representation theory names. Using the Uruguayan household survey and eight waves of the Latinobarómetro, we document a genuine but small first stage, Afro-Uruguayan public employment rose about 1.3 percentage points, a composition shift rather than a net gain, against a target the state met only fractionally. In addition, as second stage delivered a precise attitudinal null, with equivalence tests rejecting effects larger than roughly 0.15–0.20 standard deviations. The result establishes a scope condition rather than a refutation: at this level of exposure, bureaucratic inclusion produces no measurable attitudinal return. How much inclusion is required before the representation mechanism fires remains an open and newly quantifiable question.

The development of patriotism among youth in the EU

Vladislav Medintsev

Full text
The article separates patriotism from nationalism and blind allegiance while examining the developmental trajectory of patriotism among young people in the European Union. Integrating identity status theory, social identity theory, and political socialization models, it constructs a developmental account of how patriotism matures from childhood through emerging adulthood. The review defines mature patriotism through four properties: differentiation, integration, criticality, and self-authorship. It identifies a shift from uncritical, affective attachment in childhood to a potential for “constructive patriotism”—characterized by critical loyalty and dual national-European identification—in later stages. Key mechanisms facilitating this maturation include open classroom climates, cross-border mobility programs like Erasmus, and digital engagement, though the article notes these opportunities are often stratified by class and education. Conversely, significant obstacles are analyzed, including populist mobilization, economic precarity, and East-West asymmetries in historical memory. The article concludes by proposing an integrative developmental-contextual model comprising three levels (substrate, elaboration, consolidation) and four moderating conditions. It argues that while a historically novel, reflexive form of patriotism is emerging among European youth, its full realization remains contingent on specific ecological conditions and faces ongoing threats from social stratification and political polarization.

Ambiguity-Robust Dictionaries using Contextualized Embeddings

Jamil Civitarese, Sylvan Zheng

Full text
Keyword dictionaries remain widely used in text analysis for their simplicity, transparency, and replicability. However, sensitivity to keyword selection and inability to account for context introduce measurement error that attenuates estimates and obscures real relationships. We introduce ambiguity-robust dictionaries, a text-as-data method that leverages contextual word embeddings to produce more precise measures while preserving the key advantages of dictionary approaches. Our method generates contextualized embeddings for all keyword instances, then applies constrained fuzzy clustering to determine the degree of each instance's membership in a target cluster defined by a small set of researcher-identified anchor words. This approach allows the construction of ambiguity-robust document level word counts, increasing precision and reducing sensitivity to keyword selection. We demonstrate the method using three applications: UN environmental discourse, ethnic bias in Kenyan judicial politics, and moral language in US Congressional speech. In each application, our method yields substantively more robust and precise estimates than conventional approaches.

Generational Warfare Online? Social Media Use and Intergroup Attitudes in a National Survey

Andrew M. Lindner

Full text
Content analyses document vitriolic generational discourse across social media platforms, yet surveys reveal moderate attitudes among the public. Does social media exposure create extreme generational attitudes? Using nationally representative survey data (n=1,221), this study examines whether total social media use and platform-specific usage patterns correlate with generational self-identification and intergroup attitudes among American adults. Results reveal limited associations with even frequent users not exhibiting greater generational antagonism. Platform-specific analyses reveal few significant effects, though YouTube use is associated with more favorable attitudes toward other generations. These largely null findings suggest extreme online discourse does not reflect typical users’ attitudes, implying that reasons for extreme discourse are connected to visibility mechanisms, such as algorithmic amplification and creators’ strategic performance. The study also demonstrates the risks of inferring population attitudes from content analysis.

The Social in International Relations: Mapping IR Theory through Social Theory

Audrey Alejandro, Gustav Meibauer

Full text
What conceptions of ‘the social’ underpin International Relations (IR) theories? Despite the growing engagement of IR scholarship with ‘the social’ dimensions of international politics, the concept remains surprisingly under-specified. Existing debates are theoretically rich but fragmented, offering limited guidance for understanding how different traditions conceptualise the social or how these assumptions are transmitted through the pedagogical accounts of the discipline encountered by students. This article argues that IR theories are underpinned by distinct yet often implicit conceptions of the social that function as unacknowledged ontological commitments. We develop a typology of six conceptions of the social grounded in social theory—functionalist, Marxist, rational choice, systems-theoretical, structuralist, and poststructuralist—and use it to re-read the traditions through which students most commonly encounter the discipline, from liberalism and neorealism to constructivism and critical approaches. We then trace how these conceptions have been reproduced and transformed through the discipline’s pedagogical canon taking widely assigned IR textbooks as our pedagogical point of departure. We show that the coexistence of heterogeneous conceptions renders the social both ubiquitous and under-specified, generating analytical and pedagogical ambiguities. By making these assumptions explicit, the article offers students, teachers, and scholars a clearer vocabulary for analysing, teaching, and debating IR theory.

The Fruits of Finitude: Reflexive Minimalism in Epistemology and Ethics

Max Sedlmair

Full text
Reflexive Minimalism is a foundational paper proposing a minimal criterion for epistemic and normative stability: reflexive stability. A claim or norm is admissible only if it can survive its own application and does not undermine the conditions of reasoning, justification, or disagreement. The paper begins from epistemic finitude: finite agents cannot certify possession of final, unquestionable truth about ultimate reality. This does not imply relativism. Rather, it motivates reflexive humility: truth may be sought seriously, but no finite agent may treat their grasp of it as absolute possession. Applied to ethics, the framework rejects coercion grounded in uncertifiable ultimate claims and protects the shared space in which reasons can be given, challenged, and revised. This text serves as the programmatic foundation for a broader research project. A shorter core statement and further focused spin-offs will develop selected implications of the framework in epistemology, ethics, and related fields.

Canvassing Mobilizes and Increases Vote Share: Evidence from a Local Election in Seattle

Nicholas Ottone

Full text
A long line of research has shown that door-to-door canvassing is a potent campaign tactic for increasing turnout and, in some cases, vote share. More recent work has theorized, however, that canvassing effects are largest in low-salience elections without party cues and concentrated among occasional voters canvassed closer to Election Day. In this research note, I test these theoretical expectations by examining the effect of canvassing in a February local election in Seattle for a housing-related ballot measure. Using a novel geo-coded dataset of seventy doorknocking campaigns over three months, I find that voters more proximate to canvassing operations were more likely to vote and that proximate precincts had a slightly higher vote share for the targeted ballot measure. Moreover, consistent with recent theorizing, I find that turnout effects were concentrated among targeted Democrats canvassed soon before the election.

The Quest for Peace in the Islamic Tradition

Abbas Aroua

Full text
The Muslim world is one of the regions with the highest densities of conflict in the world (intra- and interstate). Yet it is one of the poorest ones in terms of conflict transformation resources both theoretical and practical. In chapter 5, the value of work in the Islamic tradition and the requirements for decent work are then discussed, work conditions and environment being crucial for social justice and social peace. In chapter 6, the focus is put on the work of goodness, a concept that in the Islamic tradition goes beyond predication and humanitarian work and represents a holistic approach to human security. Chapter 7 addresses some issues related to Islam-West relations and the tensions that may arise between Muslims and Westerners, as well as the appropriate way to deal with them.

Uncovering the Hidden Magnitude of Within-Race/Ethnicity Skin Tone Inequality

Luyin Zhang, Varsha Menon, Sam Trejo

Full text
Decades of research have linked skin tone to meaningful inequalities in health and social outcomes among individuals in the same self-identified racial/ethnic group. Yet efforts to document and understand within-race/ethnicity skin tone stratification have been hampered by a fundamental data problem: in most social surveys and biomedical databases, skin tone is either not collected or measured imprecisely using coarse categories based on a single observer’s judgment. In this study, we begin by conducting an online survey to quantify the reliability of existing interviewer-rated skin tone measures. Then, using elastic net regression, we build a genomic prediction model for skin tone among Black and Hispanic participants in the Add Health study, with the goal of improving the accuracy and availability of skin tone measurement in admixed populations. Finally, we use these DNA-based skin tone measures in two longitudinal studies to explore within-race/ethnicity skin tone inequality in contemporary America. Predicted skin tone yields substantially steeper gradients in socioeconomic status and racial classification, indicating that interviewer-rated measures meaningfully understate the degree of social stratification operating within racialized groups. Our results suggest that genetically derived skin tone predictions represent a promising new tool for advancing research on colorism – and, via the ancestry disequilibrium regression framework we introduce, a path toward identifying the causal effects of skin tone.

American Sociological Review

How Norms Depend on the Presence and Status of Observers: The Relational Contingency of Everyday Behaviors in 90 Societies

Brent Simpson, Kimmo Eriksson, Irina Vartanova, Pontus Strimling, J. Paola Merchan Tamayo, Khalid Ahmed Abdi, Neta Ad, Alisher Aldashev, Habib Mohammad Ali, Maurizio Alì, ... (+148 authors)

Full text
Norms are fundamental to social life, but basic questions about how they are affected by social context remain. Specifically, how do normative constraints depend on the presence and status of observers, and how do such relational contingencies vary across cultures? Using data from more than 25,000 individuals across 90 societies, we test several hypotheses linking normative constraints on everyday behaviors (like arguing, laughing, cursing, kissing, and crying), to the presence and status of observers. We find that behaviors widely viewed as appropriate in private become more constrained in the presence of others (an observer effect), and even more so when those others are higher in status (a status effect). Both observer and status effects are culturally universal, but the observer effect shows more variation across cultures. In particular, we find that observer effects are larger in societies that emphasize individualizing moral foundations, that is, avoiding harm and unfairness to others, because these societies are more permissive of actions when others are not around. The relative stability of status effects across cultural contexts suggests a universal tendency to defer in social hierarchies. These findings shed light on the relational contingency of everyday norms and underscore the role of such norms in the maintenance of social order and reproduction of inequality.

Sociological Theory

Settler-Colonial Structure and Indigenous Agency

Dean Ray

Full text
Settler-colonial theory has become an influential framework in sociology. Yet its central claim—that settler-colonialism is an enduring structure organized by a “logic of elimination”—generates a theoretical paradox. If settler-colonialism is total, how can Indigenous agency be theorized without reproducing the structure it seeks to transcend? I address this tension by bringing sociological theories of structure and agency into dialogue with settler-colonial studies and Indigenous theory. Drawing on William Sewell Jr.’s conception of structure as the coupling of cultural schemas and material resources, I reinterpret two foundational concepts: the logic of elimination and the politics of recognition. Yet Indigenous theorists highlight forms of agency that exceed sociological accounts of action within a shared structural field. I therefore distinguish between interior agency, exercised within settler structures, and exterior agency, which sustains Indigenous worlds beyond them.

Childcare in Transition: Evidence That Patterns of Childcare Differ by Degree of Market Integration in Northwestern Tanzania

Anushé Hassan, David W. Lawson, Susan B. Schaffnit, Mark Urassa, Rebecca Sear

Full text
Highlights This study uses novel data on multiple childcare types from both kin and nonkin in two culturally similar communities with varying market integration. It tests the hypothesis that market integration is associated with reduced kin support. Who provides support and the type of support vary by level of market integration. Market integration disrupts childcare support from paternal but not maternal kin. Distant kin and nonkin substitute for reduced support from close kin in the study's town.

Reproduction, rejection, or adoption of indigeneity? Intergenerational patterns of ethnic classification in contemporary Mexico

Braulio Güémez

Full text
This paper examines the intergenerational transmission of Indigenous classification in Mexico, focusing on how respondents reproduce, reject, or adopt Indigenous identification in relation to how they classify their parents. Drawing on data from a novel, nationally representative survey, I analyze factors associated with two key trajectories: de-indigenization, in which respondents who classify at least one parent as Indigenous do not self-classify as Indigenous, and indigenization, in which individuals who claim no Indigenous parentage come to identify as Indigenous. The findings show that roughly 16 percent of the population differs from how they report their parents’ Indigenous status. Reports of the loss of Indigenous language across generations are primarily associated with de-indigenization. In contrast, perceived phenotypical features commonly linked to Indigenous belonging and reports of Indigenous language proficiency are more strongly associated with indigenization. Additionally, a notable subset (10 percent) identifies as both Indigenous and mestizo, a classification path correlated with higher levels of education. These patterns reveal the fluidity of ethnoracial boundaries in Mexico and illustrate how processes of cultural transmission, racialization, and stratification shape the social production of Indigenous identity through different pathways.

Sex differences in academic achievement in US adolescents: the role of maturation and impulse control

Liza Reisel, Atika Khurana

Full text
This study investigates mechanisms underlying girls’ academic advantage over boys by examining the role of physical and pubertal maturation. Using longitudinal data from the Add Health study, we analyzed whether sex differences in maturation explain girls’ higher grade 10 GPAs, and whether these associations are mediated by planfulness or impulse control. Path analysis revealed that females’ higher GPAs were partially explained by their advanced physical maturation as rated by the interviewer (explaining 2.4 percent) and greater impulse control (explaining 7.7 percent), controlling for cognitive ability and other potential confounders. Still, a significant direct effect of biological sex on GPA remained, suggesting additional unexplained mechanisms. Planfulness predicted GPA but did not vary by sex, and neither planfulness nor impulse control were associated with perceived relative maturation.

Beyond hiring: the dynamics of ethnic disparities in unemployment

Raffaele Grotti, Siddartha Aradhya, Juho Härkönen

Full text
Many ethnic and racial minorities experience higher unemployment rates than majority populations. Recent research in this area has largely focused on inequalities in getting a job—through employer discrimination in hiring—using field experiments. While these studies typically identify significant levels of discrimination, they rarely examine or discuss how hiring discrimination contributes to ethnic employment penalties at the aggregate level. Our study draws attention to the role of ethnic and racial inequalities in getting a job but also in remaining employed and avoiding unemployment as factors contributing to aggregate ethnic employment penalties. Our analysis employs population register data from Sweden to estimate unemployment entry and exit rates for the majority and ten groups of children of immigrants (the second generation, G2). Based on counterfactual scenarios we conclude that the substantially higher unemployment rates among the G2 are driven by their higher risk of becoming unemployed rather than their lower chances of becoming employed. Our findings indicate that hiring discrimination and other reasons for inequalities in getting a job play a less significant role than sometimes assumed in explaining ethnic employment penalties—at least in the context of Sweden.

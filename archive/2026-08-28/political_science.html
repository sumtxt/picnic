American Journal of Political Science

Partisan bias in a sorted party system: A problem of epistemic inequality

Justin Pottle

Full text
Normative and empirical scholars typically treat partisans’ distrust of their political rivals as a product of individual cognitive biases. But even if bias is inevitable, it is parties’ coalition‐building and messaging strategies that determine who bias is directed toward. This paper argues that the social sorting of party conflict exacerbates the epistemic and democratic harm of partisan distrust by generating distinctive patterns of epistemic inequality. I show that when voters associate parties with particular social groups, members of those groups suffer greater risk of testimonial injustice and related credibility deficits owing to their perceived partisanship. These dynamics are most damaging for members of marginalized groups, as party–group overlap can reinforce the prejudices they already face. Addressing these harms, I argue, requires looking beyond individual partisans to the background features of party systems that shape how elites mobilize group identities and animus in the struggle for office.

European Journal of Political Research

Populism, radicalism, and protest mobilization by parties in Europe

Leonhard Schmidt, Bruno Castanho Silva

Full text
As European democracies struggle with a ‘crisis of representation’, populist parties appear to be instrumental in channeling popular discontent with governments across the continent, including through protests. While contemporary theories propose a strong connection between populism and protest mobilization, this has seldom been tested in a comparative perspective. At the same time, research has found that radical parties are more likely to mobilize for protests, and those parties are often also populist. We empirically disentangle these relationships with a comprehensive dataset of 4.8 million Facebook posts by all active sitting MPs in all EU27 national parliaments plus the UK between 2018 and 2023, using large language models to identify protest-related posts and those that announce protest events. Findings show that it is particularly radical parties mobilizing their followers, with populism itself having little additional explanatory power. This is the first cross-national, longitudinal evaluation of a much-touted theoretical connection between populist parties and protest mobilization, finding that it appears smaller and more conditional than prior research proposed.

Quarterly Journal of Political Science

Checks, Balances, and Presidential Ambitions

Christian Fong, Kenneth Lowande, Ayse Eldes

Full text
This study investigates the relationship between running for president and members’ contributions to the core legislative functions that impact Congress’s standing relative to the presidency. This study finds some evidence that ambitious legislators attend roll call votes and oversight hearings at lower rates, but at the same time, they are more legislatively productive, conduct just as much informal oversight and are equally informational in their public oversight. When Congress votes on an issue that affects the balance of power between Congress and the president, they are more likely to side with Congress than legislators without presidential ambitions are. These behavioral differences persist even after their presidential runs, which suggests they are not driven by a strategic effort to audition for the presidency.

Social learning behind closed doors: evidence from seniority voting in the US Supreme Court

Gabriel Lopez-Moctezuma, Benjamin Johnson

Full text
We analyze the implications of seniority voting on Supreme Court decision-making. We provide reduced-form evidence consistent with justices learning from the votes of their senior colleagues as determined by the voting mechanism. To explain this evidence, we develop and estimate a model of sequential voting with social learning. In the model, justices weigh spatial payoffs against the appropriateness of their disposition and consider their preferences, private information and the choices of senior justices. We recover estimates of judicial preferences, expertise and learning and use them to assess the quality of Supreme Court dispositions relative to alternative voting mechanisms. We find that the quality of the Court’s dispositions would have improved under an anti-seniority rule, in which junior justices are allowed to vote first.

Does disability trump family background? Determinants of electoral participation among young individuals with intellectual disability

Björn Boman, Jonas Hultin Rosenberg, Johan Wejryd

Full text
Systematic differences of electoral participation between various social groups indicate crucial constraints of comprehensive democratic inclusion. Yet, there is a dearth of research on electoral participation among individuals with intellectual disability (ID), whose participation rates are considerably lower than among non‐ID citizens. This study examines determinants of electoral participation among individuals with ID based on administrative records covering five cohorts of individuals from the Swedish upper‐secondary school for pupils with ID ( N = 8438), and their parents. Our main results indicate that the severity of the disability and parents who have voted are the strongest predictors of voting among individuals with ID. The latter suggests that family background is vital. However, the socioeconomic status of the families, such as parents' level of education, income, and occupation, are less pivotal factors.

Not‐so‐great expectations: Outgroup indifference facilitates conflict resolution in negatively interdependent contexts

Smadar Cohen‐Chen, Garrett L. Brady, Gerben A. van Kleef, Eran Halperin

Full text
We examined how responses to outgroup indifference in conflict are shaped by the perceived nature of interdependence, which changes expectations of outgroup emotions toward the ingroup. Unlike conflicts perceived as positively interdependent, we hypothesized that in conflicts perceived as negatively interdependent, outgroup indifference would constitute a positive expectancy violation, thereby inviting favorable responses. Moreover, we examined affective and inferential processes as mediators across four studies ( N = 2521). In Study 1, outgroup indifference (vs. anger and hatred but not hope) reduced negative emotions (but did not affect expected collaboration), subsequently increasing support for cooperation. Study 2 added qualitative measures and showed that outgroup indifference (vs. anger) increased support for cooperation through decreased negative emotions and increased expected collaboration. Study 3 provided correlational evidence that perceptions of interdependence predict participants' expectations of outgroup emotions. Lastly, in Study 4, outgroup indifference increased expected collaboration and emotions when interdependence was perceived as negative.

Politics & Gender

Contesting Norms and Challenging Order: Uganda’s Anti-Homosexuality Act 2023 and the Geopolitics of LGBTIQ+ Human Rights

Haley McEwen

Full text
Uganda’s adoption of the Anti-Homosexuality Act in 2023 reveals significant contestation among domestic and external actors regarding LGBTIQ+ human rights norms. This article argues that opposition to LGBTIQ+ rights has become central to contemporary challenges to the liberal international order by contesting the authority of liberal human rights norms as universal benchmarks of state legitimacy. Drawing on critical IR theories of norm violation, it analyzes how external actors responded to the Act and how Ugandan state actors defended the legislation. The article concludes that political opposition against LGBTIQ+ human rights in Uganda, akin to anti-LGBTIQ+ politics observed elsewhere in the world, has become central to political critique of “the West” and the existing international order.

Law and Political Violence against Women in Central America

Pamela Neumann, Cecilia Menjívar

Full text
Cristiana Chamorro Barrios is a Nicaraguan journalist and founder of the Nicaraguan Foundation for Freedom of the Press, a nonprofit focused on promoting freedom of expression and public access to information, as well as providing training and support to local journalists. Cristiana was arrested on June 2, 2021, in Managua, Nicaragua, on accusations of tax evasion (International Society for Human Rights 2025). At the time of her arrest, Cristiana, a member of a prominent Nicaraguan family, had recently announced her intention to challenge Daniel Ortega in the November 2021 presidential election and was widely considered the favorite in the race. She was later tried in sham proceedings and sentenced to eight years in prison. When opposition leaders speak: personalisation and question time in Australia

Daniel Casey, Thiago N. Silva, Pandanus Petter

Full text
Question Time is a highly visible and adversarial parliamentary arena central to executive accountability, yet increasing personalisation may redirect scrutiny from collective party actors to individual leaders. This article analyses personalisation in Australian Question Time using more than 21,500 opposition questions asked between 1981 and 2025. We examine two dimensions of centralised personalisation: who asks opposition questions and whom those questions target, theorising variation through party structure (single-party vs coalition opposition) and institutional reforms to leadership selection rules. The findings challenge claims of a uniform rise in elite behavioural personalisation. While opposition questioning has become increasingly Prime Minister-centred over time, who asks questions follows a distinct pattern. Leader dominance varies across parties and institutional periods, rising in single-party oppositions but remaining lower and declining in coalitions, and falling further after leadership reforms. These results show that parliamentary personalisation is multi-dimensional and contingent on party organisation and institutional rules.

Different baselines, different outcomes: Reassessing the representative promise of within-party sortition

Robert Rosenberg

Full text
Cowley, Webb and Bale’s critique of within-party sortition argues that lotteries among party members cannot produce a socially representative UK Parliament because party memberships are unrepresentative. Drawing on Party Members Project data, they show that random selection would produce only mixed improvements in descriptive representation. While their empirical contribution is significant, this article argues that the broader sceptical conclusion rests on a narrow modelling baseline. Cowley et al. effectively test only one institutional configuration—unstratified random selection from static party memberships—while extrapolating broader claims about sortition as a reform. This article develops three lines of critique. First, their simulations treat party membership as exogenous and unaffected by institutional change, ignoring evidence that candidate selection rules shape recruitment and participation. Second, their analysis evaluates only a single design variant, despite evidence that modern lottery-based institutions employ stratification and corrective mechanisms. Third, their evaluation relies on implicit ‘mirror of society’ standards without specifying formal metrics for aggregating representational trade-offs. Drawing on scholarship on sortition, deliberative democracy, and party organisation, the article proposes a dynamic and design-sensitive framework for evaluating within-party lotteries. Once alternative institutional baselines are considered, the representational potential of within-party sortition appears considerably less constrained than Cowley et al.’s analysis suggests.

Political Research Quarterly

Beyond the Average: Revisiting How District Magnitude Shapes Party Systems

Ignacio Lago, Santiago Lago-Peñas

Full text
We argue that district magnitude shapes the number of electoral parties through two mechanisms: district permissiveness, which influences incentives for electoral coordination within districts, and variation in district magnitude across districts within a country. When district magnitude varies within countries, larger districts tend to support a greater number of parties. Small parties that succeed in these districts are subsequently incentivized to compete in smaller districts, thereby weakening overall electoral coordination. Using cross-sectional data from 54 democracies and longitudinal evidence from 30 European countries between 1945 and 2019, we find that within-country variation in district magnitude increases the number of electoral parties in permissive electoral systems and erodes electoral coordination over time. We further examine the contamination mechanism from larger to smaller districts using district-level data from legislative elections in Portugal and Spain, both of which employ simple Proportional Representation systems with varying district magnitudes.

Legislative Studies Quarterly

Federalism, Geography, and the Representation of Cities in the US Congress

Leah Rosenstiel

Full text
How elections impact the behavior of legislators is one of the most fundamental questions in the study of representation, yet little is known about the role of electoral geography. In a federal system like the United States, national resources often flow to cities. However, members of Congress represent congressional districts, which do not align with city boundaries. Using spending data from 1967 to 2019 and a within‐city research design, I show that the same city is represented differently depending on how it aligns with electoral maps. Cities benefit from having more representatives provided city residents still make up a substantial portion of each congressional district. These findings contribute to our understanding of representation within a federal system and illustrate how electoral geography can create inequalities in representation and the distribution of resources.

European Union Politics

Disentangling public opinion on European integration: On the multidimensionality of integration attitudes

Lukas Lauener

Full text
This study explores citizens’ attitudes towards European integration, moving beyond a simplistic ‘yes’ or ‘no’ perspective. Focusing on the EU–Swiss relationship, it examines a variety of integration questions using 2019 Swiss Election Study data. Latent profile analysis identifies five citizen groups that share similarities in support and opposition patterns regarding the studied questions: ‘pure sovereignists’, ‘neutrals’, ‘pure integrationists’, ‘cultural sovereignists’ and ‘economic integrationists’. The latter two groups are both comparatively more favourable of economic than cultural European integration, substantiating the claim that integration attitudes are multidimensional. Investigating the predictors of adherence to these two groups, the study finds education, income and party vote intentions to have a differentiated effect. Relevant group comparisons show that education and radical right vote intentions differentiate positions along the cultural, but not the economic, European integration dimension, whereas income, radical left and liberal vote intentions differentiate positions along the economic, but not the cultural, dimension.

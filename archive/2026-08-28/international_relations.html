Review of International Organizations

The institutional politics of development finance: ODA’s role in shaping subnational DFI investments

Nicolas Bau, Simone Dietrich, Jing Qian, Duy Trinh

Full text
Development finance institutions (DFIs), operated by donor governments to support private sector development in the Global South, often face informational barriers when deciding where to invest abroad. We argue that traditional Official Development Assistance (ODA), delivered through bilateral aid agencies, can guide these investment decisions. Specifically, aid agencies generate valuable, location-specific, investment-relevant knowledge through their ODA-funded activities across regions of the Global South. When shared with DFIs, this information reduces uncertainty and increases the likelihood of DFI investment in those same regions. To test this expectation, we leverage an original geocoded dataset comprising investments by 12 DFIs from 12 OECD-DAC donor countries, matched with subnational ODA data from the GODAD dataset for the period 2000–2020. Our quantitative analyses reveal a robust and consistent association between ODA activity and DFI entry at the subnational level. To probe the mechanism, we conduct qualitative case studies of Germany, France, and the United States, illustrating how aid agencies and DFIs exchange information and coordinate investment decisions. Our findings contribute to a growing literature on the spatial allocation of development finance and provide new insight into how traditional ODA and national DFIs interact as part of an evolving development cooperation bureaucracy.

Journal of Peace Research

From state heroes to social pariahs? Civilian hiring and voluntary service in Russia’s war in Ukraine

Sergey Alexeev

Full text
Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine has produced a politically charged cohort of veterans whom the state publicly celebrates as heroes. Yet in a context where even the word “war” can be criminalised, how civilians actually regard different categories of service is difficult to observe through conventional measures. This research note uses labour-market behaviour as a window into those politically sensitive distinctions. Combining a correspondence audit—a standard tool in discrimination research—with a Bayesian sequential design that learns efficiently under real-world constraints, I sent 60 matched pairs of fictitious résumés to vacancies on major Russian job platforms in 2025. Within each pair, one applicant signalled voluntary contract service in the war, the other only routine conscription. Applicants foregrounding voluntary service received roughly a third fewer callbacks (Δ=0.18, 95% credible interval above zero), with consistent results across Bayesian and frequentist specifications. The design cannot identify the mechanism behind this penalty—employers may be reacting to perceived trauma, discipline concerns, reputational risk, or quieter political distancing from the war. But the finding is clear: in this platform-based segment of Russia’s civilian labour market, signalling voluntary participation in the war reduced employer interest rather than enhancing it. If the state’s heroic narrative translated straightforwardly into civilian life, the opposite should hold.

Purging the police in nascent democracies

Monika Nalepa, Barbara Maria Piotrowska

Full text
How do new democracies reckon with inherited authoritarian enforcement agencies? Obviously, they can disband these agencies and build new ones (thorough purge) or make former agents work for the new regime (no purge). A third way is to purge selectively: evaluate each agent, case by case. We argue that, even accounting for self-selection, such selective purges are most likely to succeed when agents are moderate in competence and ideology. When the pool of agents is less competent or more loyal to the ancien regime, selective purges give way to thorough purges; when competence and loyalty are high, “no purges” are preferred. This theory is supported with data on the operation of vetting commissions in 49 sub-national regions of Poland. We analyze these data after developing a systematic approach to case selection that builds on the Most Similar Systems Design method, traditionally used for theory building.

Prior government experience of rebel leaders and civil war duration

Emre Cebeci

Full text
Why does conflict termination occur during some rebel leaders’ tenures but not others? Under similar structural and organizational conditions, can rebel leadership help explain this variation? Although recent research has increasingly examined the role of rebel leaders, explanations of civil war duration have more often emphasized conflict-level and organizational conditions. This study builds on the emerging leader-level perspective by examining whether rebel leaders’ prior government experience affects the likelihood of conflict termination during their tenure. It argues that leaders with this experience may have capabilities that shorten conflict. Prior government experience can give future rebel leaders direct familiarity with state institutions and high-level political decision-making. This experience may help them build more effective rebel organizations and better assess opportunities for conflict termination once war begins. The argument is tested with data from the ROLE and UCDP Conflict Termination datasets for the period 1980–2011. Using Cox proportional hazards models, the study analyses the duration of civil war under individual rebel leaders. The results provide strong and consistent evidence that prior government experience significantly increases the likelihood of conflict termination during a leader’s tenure. Additional analyses are consistent with the article’s capability framework, showing that government experience is associated with stronger rebel organizational development and wartime performance. These results underscore the importance of distinguishing among leadership backgrounds and suggest that prior government experience can significantly shape civil war duration.

Whataboutism in International Diplomacy: Shaping Third-Party Public Attitudes Toward U.S. Foreign Policy

Wilfred M. Chow, Dov H. Levin, Atsushi Tago

Full text
How does whataboutism influence the attitudes of third-party and allied audiences during international diplomatic exchanges? States openly accused of misconduct often employ whataboutism, deflecting criticism by highlighting the accuser’s own transgressions. Although policymakers frequently discuss whataboutism, research on its effects—especially on third-party publics—is limited. This study addresses the gap by examining via survey experiments a “hard case”: the impact of whataboutism targeting the U.S. on Japanese and the British publics, two steadfast U.S. allies. Our findings reveal that whataboutism significantly undermines support for the critic’s actions and diminishes the willingness of these populations to back sanctions led by the critic against states engaging in whataboutism. These results, the first empirical evidence of whataboutism’s effects on third-party allied publics, highlight whataboutism’s potency in shaping international public opinion, even among audiences from nations closely aligned with the critic country, potentially constraining U.S. foreign policy options.

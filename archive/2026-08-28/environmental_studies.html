Annual Review of Environment and Resources

Innovative Finance for Urban Climate Action: From Financial Instruments to Governance Conditions

Pourya Salehi, Theo Kötter, Amy Jones, David Corbett, Jihyeon Jeong, Asad Asadzadeh

Full text
Climate goals cannot be met without significant investment in cities, yet major gaps in climate finance, technical capacity, and access to capital markets persist. In response, a growing range of innovative finance methods have appeared. This article reviews the existing research on blended finance, green and sustainability-linked bonds, catastrophe and resilience bonds, land value capture, debt-for-nature swaps, and digital finance. The review finds that innovative finance should not be framed as a collection of instruments but as a governance framework that translates climate priorities into financial risk–return considerations, with effectiveness depending on supportive regulatory, fiscal, and institutional systems. Across instruments, financial innovation redistributes risk, aligns incentives, and reshapes public–private relationships. Effective strategies must therefore integrate instrument design with coordination, accountability, long-term value creation, and equitable climate outcomes across rapidly growing cities.

Auditing urban nature access claims for sustainable city governance

Kexin Zhou, Liuna Geng

Full text
Urban governments use nature-access indicators to assess biodiversity, health, equity and regenerative-planning goals, yet policy conclusions can depend on how the claim is constructed. We audit six specifications—inventory, access geometry, distance model, threshold, population allocation and vulnerability definition—using three purposive core cities, transfer checks, a 30-city data-readiness screen and an entrance-aware London validation. Plausible alternatives changed access levels by up to 48.82 percentage points, reversed some environmental-justice classifications and substantially altered underserved target populations. A matched London diagnostic showed that network penalties narrowed from 36.95 points at 300 m to 0.52 points at 1000 m. We therefore propose a decision-stability envelope that reports a preferred specification alongside ranges, equity-direction changes, target-set reclassification and missing operational data. The cases are not globally representative; they demonstrate an auditable workflow for testing whether urban nature claims are sufficiently stable to guide regenerative and equitable planning.

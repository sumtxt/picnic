Journal of Peace Research

Develop the countryside to stabilize the cities: the downstream effects of rural development assistance in Afghanistan

Andrew J Glubzinski

Full text
Does development assistance reduce violence in conflict zones? The case of Afghanistan strongly informs the scholarly literature on this. However, existing empirical frameworks are not grounded in theories of insurgent mobility and, as a result, fail to examine the downstream effects of development assistance projects. I propose that Afghanistan's environment created incentives for insurgents to project violence from rural areas into nearby urban centers, necessitating rural development assistance to disrupt urban violence. Using Asia Foundation Afghanistan survey data from districts with both urban and rural populations, aggregated to the province-year level, I find that projects targeting rural areas were associated with reduced violence downstream in nearby urban areas, whereas urban development assistance did not significantly impact levels of urban violence. These findings suggest development assistance may only reduce violence when projects are situated near areas of insurgent support, and the reduction in violence may only be evident when empirical models enable estimating downstream effects.

Fading voices: explaining the termination of organizations in dissent

Johannes Vüllers, Ingrid Vik Bakken

Full text
Why do organizations cease to exist? We focus on organizations in Africa and Central America and the Caribbean that have protested against the central government from 1990 to 2017. These organizations can play a crucial role in democratic development by mobilizing social groups. However, a significant number (up to 26%) of organizations actually disband over time. To understand the reasons for this, we focus on factors related to organizational activities and state responses. Using the Anatomy of Resistance Campaigns (version 2.0) dataset, we are able to track the evolution of such organizations from their founding to their eventual termination. Our results show that the use of nonviolent means and maximalist demands make the dissolution of an organization less likely. Whereas lethal repression has no effect, legal restrictions influence the demise of the organization. Understanding the conditions under which organizations survive or terminate is crucial for assessing the long-term potential of contentious politics. These insights are particularly relevant in contexts where civil society is under pressure and democratic institutions are fragile.

Falling on fertile ground? Examining the impact of sanctions on opposition movement coalitions

Finn L Klebe, Nils W Metternich

Full text
How do sanctions against the government impact opposition movement coalitions? While existing research has linked sanctions to increased legitimacy for the opposition’s cause as well as higher levels of state violence, we argue that the imposition of broad international sanctions sends a signal of a resolved government to opposition organizations. Consequently, opposition organizations will form coalitions conditional on this signal of resolve. We argue that violent organizations, which are willing to bear higher costs, become more central in opposition coalitions during broad sanctions against the government. We also expect the composition of alliances to change, bringing about alliances between ideologically more heterogeneous organizations in the face of a more resolved government. Using a multilayer network analytic framework, we test our theory by analyzing opposition movements on the African continent from 1990 to 2015. A within-case analysis of the anti-apartheid movement in South Africa further illustrates the causal mechanism. We find considerable empirical support for our main argument, which contributes to our understanding of how sanctions can promote or hinder the prospects of opposition movements.

Journal of Conflict Resolution

Unfulfilled Aspirations: Autonomy, Regional Boundaries, and Self-Determination Conflicts

Andreas Juon

Full text
Administrative boundaries play a crucial but understudied role in managing separatist conflict in federal and decentralized states. I argue that closely aligning these boundaries with ethnic settlement patterns helps sustainably defuse separatist conflicts. When minorities control unified ethnic regions, autonomy enables them to pursue their policy goals, reducing grievances. Conversely, when they are fragmented across regions or remain minorities at the regional level, autonomy is more likely to violate their aspirations and fuel continued separatist mobilization. I test this argument using new spatial data on regional boundaries in 132 multi-ethnic countries (1946–2017). A difference-in-differences design exploiting within-group variation over time reveals that administrative boundaries critically shape separatist mobilization. The step-wise implications of my hypothesized mechanisms are reflected in analyses of intermediate outcomes and in influential case accounts. These findings underscore the importance of regional boundary design in managing ethnic conflict and offer insights for institutional responses to separatist pressures.

Political Studies

Measuring Changes in Corruption Over Time

Maya Dalton, Justin Esarey

Full text
How confident should we be in the answers we get when using existing measures of corruption? Scholars routinely rely on country-level corruption indices to study democratic accountability, representation, development, and institutional reform. Yet a great deal of research argues that country-level corruption indicators are incomparable to each other and over time. We present evidence that these indicators all track a shared component identifiable as a change within country in overall corruption, but despite this, they remain problematically inconsistent with each other. As a result, we argue that empirical findings about corruption based on these designs will be particularly sensitive to the choice of measure. To address this problem, we introduce a new synthetic measure of within-country change in corruption that is robust to bias and measurement error that may be present in those existing measures. Our new measure allows researchers to explain changes in corruption that are common to all measures, rather than privileging any single index. We employ our new measure to revisit a published analysis of the effect of women’s representation on corruption, finding a more consistent relationship and more balanced dynamics using our measure than with others’.

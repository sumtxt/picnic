AI Translation, Culture Formation, and the Risk of Cultural Separation: A Conceptual Framework with Propositions

Matthias Huemmer

Full text
Language is foundational to culture formation, yet the consequences of AI-mediated translation for intercultural understanding remain insufficiently theorized. This conceptual paper examines whether AI-based translation connects cultures or, under specific conditions, contributes to their separation. Drawing on functionalist, conflict, symbolic interactionist, cultural transmission, and cultural evolution perspectives, the paper reconstructs culture as an emergent social process constituted through co-presence, repeated interaction, norm formation, identity construction, and temporal transmission. On this basis, it develops a five-step model of culture formation and derives five propositions concerning AI translation as socio-technical mediation. The central argument is that AI translation can connect cultures at the level of informational access while, under substitute-use conditions, weakening deeper meaning-based understanding that depends on interpretive effort and linguistic engagement. To capture this tension, the paper introduces the concept of cultural thinning, defined as broader cross-linguistic access combined with reduced depth and quality of shared meaning formation. The analysis further argues that translation systems are not neutral intermediaries: structural biases relating to gender, low-resource languages, and cultural context may reshape intercultural perception in systematically distorted ways, producing cultural misrecognition rather than mere neutral error. Twitter/X is discussed as an illustrative case of platform-mediated intercultural communication under compressed, rapid, and context-sensitive conditions. The paper concludes by outlining an empirical research agenda for examining overconfidence, interpretive loss, bias, and platform effects in AI-mediated intercultural communication.

Temporal dimensions of audiobook listening among older adults with visual impairment

Signe Lund Mathiesen, Amanda Grenier, Walter Wittich, Bjorn Herrmann

Full text
Reading is a recurring and immersive temporal practice that shapes daily rhythms and identity across the life course. When visual impairment disrupts access to print, established reading routines and familiar temporal structures can be unsettled. This study examines how audiobook listening (re)shapes the temporal organization of everyday life among older adults with visual impairment. Drawing on in-depth interviews with eleven participants, we identify three interrelated temporal dimensions of audiobook listening. Day- and life-course time captures how listening structures daily routines and sustains reading identity across life transitions. Situational time represents how participants negotiate listening by managing attention and affect within daily contexts. Momentary time captures immersive listening as a transportation into story worlds and a suspension of the present. Together, these findings conceptualize audiobook listening as a multi-scalar temporal practice that supports adaptation, reorganizes everyday rhythms, and contributes to well-being after visual impairment.

Mental Health First Aid in the workplace: a reflexive thematic analysis of UK workers’ experiences.

Opeyemi Atanda, Eleni Vangeli, Steven D. Brown, patrick callaghan, Graham Durcan, Kerry V Wood, Tim Carter, Nick O'Shea, Sarah White, Paula Reavey

Full text
Background Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) is widely used in workplace settings to improve mental health literacy, reduce stigma, and encourage help-seeking among employees experiencing distress. This paper explores how employees perceive and engage with MHFA within workplace contexts. Methods This qualitative study was embedded as part of a larger clustered randomised controlled trial (Atanda et al, 2020). Qualitative interviews with twenty-four participants purposefully selected from a list of trial survey respondents were conducted. Participants interviewed included employees who were MHFA recipients, mental health first aiders, employees of the organisation where MHFA was introduced, and senior managers involved in implementing MHFA. Reflexive thematic analysis was used to explore and understand employees ‘perceptions of MHFA. Findings: Three main themes and two subthemes were observed: a) Ambiguity concerning the purpose of MHFA, b) Employees’ reluctance to engage due to ambivalent feelings about MHFA support (subthemes: Anticipated Stigma, MHFA not addressing core concerns affecting staff well-being) and c) Risks of disclosure’s interplay with work-based interpersonal relationships. Employees discussed their doubts about the purpose of MHFA in supporting their mental health concerns. Doubts raised by employees appear to have impacted engagement levels. The employees also described concerns about anticipated negative reactions after disclosing their mental health concerns. Good interpersonal relationships among colleagues contributed to disclosure attempts, but participants appeared concerned about the anticipated impact of the disclosure on their careers and other job opportunities. Discussion: This study highlights challenges to integrating Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) into workplaces, noting that prevailing cultures hinder its effectiveness without addressing stigma and work-related mental health issues. It recommends a strategic approach to introduce MHFA, emphasising workplace culture change for better engagement. The findings clarify MHFA's scope, and future research should test its impact against other workplace mental health initiatives.

Housing Supply and Racial Integration: Evidence from Building Permits in U.S. Metropolitan Areas

Aaron Fernandez

Full text
Debates over housing supply, from NIMBY opposition to YIMBY advocacy, have focused largely on the economic consequences of permitting new housing, but their broader social effects remain underexplored. This article examines whether housing development shapes racial residential segregation. Using panel data on U.S. metropolitan areas from 1990 to 2020, I show that metros permitting more housing experienced larger declines in Black–White segregation. An instrumental variable strategy exploiting geologic constraints on buildability strengthens causal interpretation, and place-level analyses demonstrate that this relationship also holds for towns within metropolitan areas. I further assess several proposed mechanisms, including housing costs, residential mobility, and the location and type of new development, finding no single pathway fully explains the association. Together, these results suggest that housing supply operates as a macro-institutional condition that structures residential sorting. The findings position housing development not only as an economic outcome, but as a consequential social process shaping patterns of racial inequality.

Road Violence and Perceptions of Safety among Pedestrians and Cyclists

Jose Pina-Sánchez, Alexander Trinidad, Ian Loader

Full text
This registered report investigates whether involvement in road traffic collisions meaningfully shapes pedestrians’ and cyclists’ perceptions of safety. Drawing on criminological insights - particularly the fear‑victimisation paradox - the study explores the common policy assumption that reducing collisions alone improves subjective safety. Using ESRA2 survey data from 24 European countries, the analysis estimates both average and conditional treatment effects of collision experience on perceived safety, accounting for vulnerability (age, gender) and exposure (active travel frequency). The study employs equivalence testing and counterfactual modelling to assess whether victims’ perceptions differ from non‑victims’. Findings will clarify whether collision reduction strategies are sufficient to encourage active travel or whether broader interventions addressing vulnerability and environmental cues are required.

Social Media Addiction and Sleep Duration in Young Adults: Evidence From a Large Public Dataset

Brian M. Cooney

Full text
Social media use has become pervasive among young adults, which raises concerns about the impact on mental and physical health. Using a public dataset with a sample of 705 students aged 16–25, containing participants’ age, average hours of sleep per night, and an ordinal 1-10 social media addiction score (derived from the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale), this study examined three research questions. First, is there a relationship between social media addiction and hours of sleep per night? A linear regression showed that each one-point increase in social media addiction score was associated with a 0.54 hour decrease in average sleep duration, with 59% of the variance accounted for by the model. Second, do suspected addiction levels (Not Likely Addicted, Possibly Addicted, or Likely Addicted) differ significantly in mean sleep duration? A one-way between-subjects ANOVA (and subsequent pairwise comparisons) indicated significant differences in average sleep duration across all three addiction levels. Third, is there a relationship between addiction levels and adequate sleep (as recommended by the Mayo Clinic)? A chi-square test also revealed a strong association between addiction level and sleep adequacy, with the Not Likely Addicted group showing significantly higher rates of adequate sleep and the Likely Addicted group showing significantly higher rates of inadequate sleep. Keywords: social media addiction, behavioral addiction, sleep duration, BSMAS

From Awareness to Application: Ethical Understanding Among Interdisciplinary Undergraduate Students

Indhumathi selvalumar

Full text
The rapid advancement of modern science and technology has intensified the need for ethical awareness and responsible decision-making among future professionals. While undergraduate students are often exposed to foundational ethical concepts, the depth of their understanding and their ability to critically evaluate ethical challenges remain unclear. This study presents an exploratory analysis of undergraduate students’ awareness, perceptions, and interpretative understanding of ethical issues in modern science. A structured online survey was conducted with 50 participants from diverse academic backgrounds, examining not only awareness levels but also the perceived adequacy of existing ethical frameworks and the relationship between ethical understanding and trust in scientific practices. The findings indicate that while a majority of students demonstrate baseline awareness of ethical issues, significant uncertainty persists regarding the effectiveness and applicability of current ethical frameworks. Notably, the results reveal a gap between ethical awareness and deeper critical engagement, particularly in relation to emerging domains such as data privacy and technological accountability. This study contributes to the existing literature by highlighting the distinction between awareness and applied ethical understanding among undergraduate students. It underscores the need for more structured, application-oriented ethics education that moves beyond theoretical exposure to practical engagement with real-world ethical dilemmas. As a preliminary investigation, the study provides a foundation for future research on strengthening ethical competencies in scientific education. This study is novel in distinguishing between ethical awareness and applied ethical understanding among undergraduate students, highlighting a critical gap that is often overlooked in existing research.

From land prices to construction deficits: Understanding housing overappreciation in Santiago

Ernesto Lopez-Morales

Full text
During the 2010s, Chile experienced significant housing overvaluation and unaffordability. This chapter challenges the prevailing belief that land costs for developers are the primary cause of this issue. This study analyzes official data from Santiago, the capital city, showing that, on average, land costs for developers did not increase but slightly decreased between 2010 and 2019 due to fewer public restrictions on residential density. This indicates that land costs alone cannot explain the substantial rise in housing prices. This study investigates the impact of other economic and political factors on housing overappreciation, finding a surge in housing demand due to immigration and a shortage of new housing constructed since 2000 as a more probable cause. This supply shortage led to higher housing prices and rents, while housing demand became increasingly inelastic to absorb them. This study explores potential reasons for the lack of new housing construction and highlights the possibility of developers’ collusion, being a shortcoming of Chile’s neoliberal housing market. Finally, this study offers suggestions for improving market competitiveness, boosting new housing construction, and thus improving housing affordability in Chile.

An energetic theory of urbanization in ancient societies

David S. Sandeford

Full text
Urbanization is one of the most consequential and best-documented processes in the archaeological and historical record, yet we lack a general quantitative theory to explain it in ancient societies. I address this gap by developing a model of urbanization that integrates the comparative insights of archaeology and history with settlement scaling theory and human macroecology. The model proposes that urbanization---measured as the population size of a polity's largest settlement relative to polity population---is promoted by the scale of political organization and the economic opportunities created by a polity's largest settlement but constrained by agricultural productivity and the costs of transportation. I formalize this model as a system of interrelated scaling relationships that measure these variables and their interactions in terms of the general properties of human social networks and the energetic constraints on all social systems embedded in space. The model predicts that the population size of a polity's largest settlement scales as the two-thirds power of polity population, a prediction that is confirmed in hundreds of ancient societies from across the globe, spanning the last 10,000 years.

Gender Differences in Religious Participation during Adolescence: Exploring longitudinal development using genetically informed data

Christoph Spörlein, Elmar Schlueter, Josue Teran Linarte

Full text
Gender differences in religious participation are well-documented in Western societies, yet the mechanisms underlying these disparities remain a subject of debate. While psychological accounts emphasize heritable personality traits, sociological perspectives highlight gendered socialization and social control. This study bridges these perspectives by utilizing a genetically informed design to examine how genetic and environmental influences structure religious participation throughout adolescence. Drawing on longitudinal data from the TwinLife panel study (approx. 4,000 twin pairs in Germany), we employ genetic simplex, age-moderated, and bivariate ACE models to track the development of religious participation from age 10 into early adulthood. Our findings reveal that while mean gender differences in participation are modest in the German context, the underlying sources of variation are differ substantially by gender. For girls, shared environmental influences (the family context) remain the primary driver of participation well into the mid-20s, suggesting a prolonged influence of familial social regulation and constrained individual autonomy. In contrast, boys exhibit an earlier shift from shared environmental to genetic and non-shared environmental influences, reflecting a faster transition toward individual religious autonomy. Furthermore, bivariate analyses indicate that the genetic overlap between personality traits (e.g., risk-taking, agreeableness) and religious participation is specific to females, suggesting that personality-based accounts are insufficient to explain gender differences more broadly. These results are consistent with the idea that social environments may moderate the expression of genetic propensities in a gendered manner.

Using Layered Scaffolding to Protect the Writing Process in the Age of AI

Kayla Marie Sarte

Full text
Generative AI tools are increasingly present in second language (L2) writing environments, offering learners rapid feedback, revision support, and even the ability to generate extended texts. While these systems can help students produce linguistically polished writing, they also raise developmental concerns when key composing processes such as generating ideas, structuring arguments, and revising language are carried out by the system rather than the learner. This paper conceptualizes this shift as writing-process conflation: the merging of composing activities that have traditionally unfolded over time into a single AI-assisted interaction. In response, I propose the Sequenced–Segmented Layered Scaffolding (SSLS) framework, an instructional architecture designed to preserve the developmental value of writing in AI-assisted contexts. The framework structures writing instruction through three principles: sequencing, which encourages learners to develop reasoning before drafting; segmentation, which makes rhetorical work visible through move-level composing; and layered revision, which stages feedback across linguistic levels to support noticing and active engagement with language. Drawing on research in second language writing, genre pedagogy, scaffolding, and second language acquisition, the paper outlines how SSLS can guide the design of AI-assisted writing instruction that supports learner agency while using AI as a constrained dialogic partner. The framework contributes to ongoing discussions in L2 writing research about how emerging technologies can be integrated without bypassing the cognitive and linguistic processes through which academic writing ability develops.

Does Mandatory Military Service Shape Men's Occupational Choices? Evidence from the Suspension of Conscription Across Europe

Maik Hamjediers, Leandro Ivan Canzio

Full text
Recent geopolitical tensions have renewed debates on conscription and mandatory military service. While a substantial literature examines its consequences for education, employment, and earnings, less is known about whether military service shapes subsequent occupational placement. This study investigates whether conscription influenced men's early-career placement in the armed forces, in skill- and license-related civilian occupations, and in male-dominated occupations, and additionally explores whether such effects vary across different forms of institutionalized conscription. Drawing on repeated cross-sectional data from the European Labour Force Survey (1995--2024), we exploit the suspension of conscription across 18 European countries to estimate cohort-level effects in a staggered difference-in-differences design. Consistent with descriptive individual-level associations in European SHARE data, the estimates suggest that cohorts no longer exposed to conscription are less likely to work in all three types of occupations. However, the effects are modest in magnitude and statistically imprecise, and analyses of heterogeneity by conscription selectivity and service duration yield no consistent patterns. Overall, the findings contribute to understanding how conscription can shape subsequent socio-economic attainment and provide a first step toward a comparative perspective on how these effects may vary across conscription regimes.

From Additive Effects to Household Configurations: Reinterpreting Parental Influence in Intergenerational Occupational Transmission

Tamilka Bonjeer

Full text
Research on intergenerational occupational transmission typically models parental influences as additive and independent, treating maternal and paternal occupations as separable inputs. This study shows that such additive models are algebraically equivalent to configurational specifications that represent parental occupations as joint household configurations. Despite this equivalence, the two approaches are not interpretively equivalent. Drawing on Bourdieu and Passeron’s concept of the household as an “ensemble,” the study advances a configurational perspective in which dual-parent exposure serves as a baseline of joint occupational presence, and mother-only and father-only configurations are deviations from this baseline. Using data from students in 79 countries, the analysis examines occupational alignment in Health and Technical STEM fields. While both specifications yield identical fitted values, the configurational interpretation reveals systematic asymmetries across household configurations that are not directly visible in additive parameterisations. Differences commonly read as parental effects can be re-expressed as contrasts corresponding to the absence of the other parent relative to the dual-parent baseline, varying by gender and field. More broadly, the findings highlight how statistically equivalent models can support substantively different interpretations when the underlying comparison between household configurations is made explicit.

Aporofobia Indígena no Brasil

Jorge Luis Tonetto, Flavio Comim

Full text
A situação dos indígenas brasileiros é de exclusão, onde a imensa maioria reside nas reservas a eles destinadas. Este artigo objetiva explorar o conceito de aporofobia em relação ao indígena brasileiro. Os resultados demonstram que a aporofobia indígena emerge como um campo discursivo mensurável, com similaridade de 0,61 entre phobia e poverty no FastText e acima de 0,85 no BERT, revelando uma sobreposição semântica entre índio, pobreza e discriminação. A pesquisa analisou 676 notícias online publicadas ao longo de 12 meses em quatro veículos de comunicação, e utilizou análise léxica e de contexto com métodos FastText e BERT. Os resultados apontam ainda que 76% das notícias tratam dos direitos indígenas, porém foi detectado que a aversão e a pobreza têm comportamentos de grande similaridade. Encontramos ainda que o indígena é mais relacionado aos termos de pobreza que os de hostilidade e aversão. Este artigo contribui para preencher a lacuna sobre estudos de aporophobia no Brasil, e avança no entendimento da percepção de conceitos que moldam a sociedade brasileira contemporânea. Os resultados indicam que a aporofobia indígena emerge como uma região semântica concreta — um subconjunto do discurso social onde pobreza e rejeição se cruzam no campo indígena— oferecendo evidências empíricas que podem orientar políticas públicas voltadas aos povos indígenas e ao enfrentamento do preconceito.

When Social Democrats Move Right on Migration, Who Responds? Lessons from Germany's Social Democrats

Johanna M. Schulte, Daniel Bischof

Full text
Social democratic parties increasingly adopt more restrictive positions on migration in response to radical right challengers, yet we know little about whether such moves actually shift public opinion. We examine this mechanism in Germany, where form chancellor Scholz's October 2023 call to deport people ``more often and faster'' marked a visible break with the SPD's earlier migration profile. Using the fact that the statement occurred during ongoing European Social Survey fieldwork, we compare respondents interviewed just before and just after the intervention. We show that migration quickly became more salient in public debate, but attitudinal effects were limited in scope and duration. Left-leaning respondents and likely SPD supporters remained largely unaffected, while only voters in the political center briefly moved toward more restrictive views, primarily on the economic dimension. These shifts faded quickly and did not translate into greater closeness to the SPD or into sustained changes in government satisfaction overall.

Pairing In Situ Surveys and Traces: A Typology for Mobile Linkage Designs

Yifei Lu, Razieh Pourafshari, Lukas Otto, Mareike Wieland, Yung-Ju Chang, Joseph Bayer

Full text
The proliferation of methods for collecting digital traces through mobile devices has produced an increasing range of ways to capture daily behaviors and contexts. Digital traces, and mobile sensors especially, also offer new opportunities for augmenting mobile self-report methods (i.e., ecological momentary assessment; EMA). In this paper, we map the landscape of emergent methods to guide the design of studies that link in analysis – or directly pair in design – mobile traces and intensive surveys (i.e., mobile linkage). We propose a typology for categorizing mobile linkage designs according to two dimensions: timeliness and adaptiveness, which denote the ability of mobile sensing to shape when and what to trigger (in) an EMA survey, respectively. This results in four types of designs: informing, triggering, forming, and adaptive triggering. We then walk through key design considerations, including survey display (e.g., with or without traces), survey length (e.g., single-item vs. 10 items), and survey wording (e.g., past vs. present reference). We conclude by discussing the generalizability and applicability of our typology, as well as the challenges of leveraging the growing toolkit of mobile methods in communication and beyond.

Circulatory Propaganda, Narrative Media, and the Rhythms of Digital Conflict

Rob Burton

Full text
This paper examines a cluster of AI-generated, Lego-style videos that circulated widely during the March–April 2026 U.S.–Iran conflict and argues that they illuminate an emerging transformation in propaganda under platform conditions. Produced rapidly in response to unfolding events and distributed across X, Telegram, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube, these short-form videos combined stylised animation, rhythmic music, ridicule, and geopolitical commentary in highly shareable formats. Rather than understanding them primarily as attempts at persuasion or misinformation, the paper proposes the concept of circulatory propaganda to describe political media whose force derives less from belief change than from visibility, repetition, and persistence across digital platforms. Drawing on McLuhan, Baudrillard, and Virilio, it argues that meaning is increasingly stabilised through movement rather than verification, and through recurrence rather than narrative coherence. The paper also examines producer self-descriptions such as Narrative Media and Narratainment, terms offered by representatives associated with Explosive Media who rejected the label of propaganda, suggesting that contemporary influence operations increasingly frame themselves as hybrid forms of entertainment-driven storytelling. Finally, it argues that the metaphor of meme war may now be insufficient, as today’s information environment resembles a synthetic media tsunami: a continuous flood of AI songs, fake battlefield footage, parody leaders, animated conspiracies, and instant meme responses that together reshape how politics is encountered.

Actor-network storytelling: Narrative-as-a-network and the gacha game model in Genshin Impact

Yaeri Kim

Full text
This article proposes the actor-network theory (ANT) as a framework to analyze the narrative experience of games-as-a-service (GaaS) with a focus on gacha games. Although narrative is central to both player enjoyment and marketing strategies in gacha games, it remains theoretically neglected because its dispersed and amorphous narrative experience is incompatible with traditional models that posit the narrative text as a unified, self-contained entity. Drawing on Law’s conceptualization of ANT and Latour’s exploration of “beings of fiction,” this article develops an alternative model of ‘actor-network storytelling.’ This model approaches the video game narrative as a network produced by complex interplays among heterogeneous human and non-human actors both within and outside the game world, including players’ interpretations and the developer’s policy. Applying this framework to a case study of Genshin Impact (2020), the article demonstrates how the game provides a fragmentary and porous network of narrative information to enroll individual players and collective fandom into the process of completing, personalizing, and enhancing the story. While this strategy enables a diverse and enriched narrative network, it simultaneously renders the storytelling volatile and fragile, threatened by discursive friction and a lack of interiority. By introducing this relational framework, the article contributes to the academic pursuit of alternative storytelling models for interactive media and offers new insights into emerging forms of digital narrative.

The Morality of Petty Corruption in the UK

Viviana Baraybar Hidalgo

Full text
Why do citizens engage with and tolerate petty bribery and favouritism so differently? Research on petty corruption has focused on developing countries and treated different practices as equivalent. Drawing on moral and social psychology to complement existing accounts centred on social norms and institutional constraints, this paper argues that variation in citizen engagement can also be explained by the activation of distinct moral domains: both types of corruption violate fairness norms, but favouritism additionally invokes loyalty and kinship obligations, making it morally defensible. Full text

Feet without stress: High vowel deletion in Québec French

Natália Brambatti Guzzo, Heather Goad, Guilherme Duarte Garcia

Full text
This paper investigates whether high vowel deletion (HVD) in Québec French is conditioned by prosodic structure. In Prosodic Phonology, prosodic constituents are typically assumed to be universal. However, the universality of certain constituents, such as the foot, has been questioned for languages like French, where prominence is only obligatory at the phrase level. We probe this issue by analysing HVD, a lenition process that is sensitive to prominence. We conducted two experiments. Experiment 1 examined the role of phrasal tone profile in HVD, while Experiment 2 examined whether word-internal constituency affects HVD. The results show that, while tone profile does not affect HVD, the process is preferred in dependent positions of iambic feet in even-parity forms. Odd-parity forms show no preference regarding HVD, suggesting that evenand odd-parity forms are parsed differently in Québec French.

Intersecting inequalities in later life: work-family trajectories and old age poverty among natives and immigrants in Finland

Maria Vaalavuo, Aart-Jan Riekhoff, Aleksi Karhula

Full text
Concurrently to population ageing, the number of older people with immigrant background is rising. Immigrant background in conjunction with family and labour market trajectories during working age will increasingly shape inequality in late life. In this study, we ask: 1) What kinds of work-family trajectories can be identified among immigrant and native men and women in the years preceding retirement? 2) How do clusters of these trajectories differ by immigration background and gender? 3) How are these clusters linked to socioeconomic outcomes at age 65 and throughout working age? We use Finnish full-population register data from 1987-2022. Our analysis sample consists of individuals observed in the data for the entire observation period from age 45 to 65. Using multichannel sequence analysis and cluster analysis, we identified seven clusters. High-income earner with family was the most common among natives (21%), while low-income earner with family was the most common among immigrants (26%). The identified clusters correlate with differences in earnings development from age 45 to 65 and poverty status at age 65. Poverty at age 65 is higher among immigrants in all clusters, while being single is an important predictor of poverty regardless of the cluster. Gender appeared to affect poverty and earnings development much less than immigrant background when work-life trajectory was taken into account. Our study provides novel evidence on older immigrants and illustrates great heterogeneity regarding work-family trajectories, highlighting the importance of analysing determinants of poverty and inequality across the life course in a heterogenous population.

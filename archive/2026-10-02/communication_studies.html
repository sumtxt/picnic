Inoculating against fatalistic beliefs: a comparative test of narrative and non-narrative strategies

Minyi Chen, Hye Kyung Kim

Full text
This study compares the effectiveness of narrative and non-narrative inoculation messages in reducing fatalistic beliefs and fostering resistance to conflicting cancer information. A two-phase randomized experiment (N = 572 in Phase 1; n = 332 in Phase 2) tested their effects on counterarguing against attack, cancer fatalism, perceived efficacy, and intentions to adopt cancer risk-reduction behaviors. Compared with control, both inoculation messages reduced cancer fatalism, enhanced perceived efficacy, and increased certain intentions to adopt certain risk-reduction behaviors. Narrative inoculation elicited more counterarguing against attack than the non-narrative counterpart. Inoculation effects also varied by baseline fatalism, functioning therapeutically by immediately reducing initially high fatalism and, more modestly, prophylactically by maintaining initially low fatalism. These findings extend inoculation theory by elucidating the role of message format in sustaining resistance to negative media effects, and by demonstrating the theory’s potential dual prophylactic–therapeutic functionality in a fatalistic belief context.

Skin Tone as a Visual Cue in Political Campaigns: Evidence from Elections in Mexico

Silvia Téliz, Sandra González-Bailón

Full text
Research has long established that physical appearance and phenotypical traits influence how people evaluate political candidates. Skin color is one of the visual cues the electorate perceives during campaigns and media coverage, and a possible source of prejudice and bias. Here we use computational visual analysis to investigate the association between skin tone and electoral success during the 2024 Mexican Federal Elections. Our analyses incorporate important correlates not considered in prior research, most prominently news coverage, political ads, and social media use. We also account for geographic differences in average skin tone, explicitly modeling a source of variation that is obscured when skin tone is analyzed disregarding contextual baselines. We use fixed effects generalized linear models to control for state and party variation as well as candidate-level demographics, political experience, incumbency status, and media presence (news coverage, political ads, and social media use). Consistent with past work, we find that as candidates’ skin tone becomes darker, their predicted vote share declines. However, news coverage, political ads, and social media use are, together, more important predictors of electoral success than skin tone, indicating that this phenotypical trait is not as prominent a visual cue as earlier research suggested.

Affective Authority: Understanding Health and Wellness Influence on Social Media

Deanna Holroyd, Mariah L. Wellman

Full text
Health and wellness content has become increasingly popular on social media. When examining why this content resonates with certain viewers, existing research often turns to concepts of authenticity, credibility, trust, expertise, and authority. In this article, we map out existing work on these concepts across an extensive body of interdisciplinary scholarship within media, communication, and cultural studies, and the anthropology of health, media, and medicine. By analyzing how these theories relate to one another, we offer synthesized definitions of them within the context of digital health content. We then make the case that this existing work too often fails to acknowledge the relationality, mediatedness, and affective qualities associated with health and wellness influencers and their content. To combat this, we offer the theoretical intervention of “affective authority,” which encourages an attunement to how affect, emotions, and feelings emerge through the production and consumption of social media content. We argue that this concept of “affective authority” better illuminates how and why wellness influencers and their content are deemed authoritative arbiters of health-related knowledge.

Wise Empathy Improves Well-Being on Instagram

Gregory John Depow, Christopher Oveis, Emily Zohar, Michael Inzlicht

Full text
Two-thirds of the global population spends significant time browsing social media, making it critical to understand how to improve online experiences to support well-being. Social media use is often linked to both positive and negative psychological outcomes, but interventions to enhance well-being during social media use remain limited. Here, we test whether an intervention embedded in daily life that trains individuals to use wise empathy on Instagram can improve users’ well-being. Drawing from strategic regulation of empathy and wisdom, we used an ecological momentary intervention to train half our sample ( N = 260) to wisely regulate empathy for positive and negative emotions on their own Instagram feeds. Using experience sampling ( N obs . = 5,902), we tracked empathy and well-being in daily life. The intervention increased wise empathy online – boosting appreciative joy, positive emotion sharing and compassion for negative emotions – while reducing negative emotion sharing, upward social comparisons and personal distress. On surveys where participants had recently browsed Instagram, those in the wise empathy condition reported higher well-being than controls, an effect mediated by changes in wise empathy. These findings position wise empathy as a scalable, trainable skill that can enhance well-being on social media by helping users engage more adaptively with emotional content. This work extends our understanding of how empathy predicts well-being and elucidates the relationship between social media use and well-being, with broad societal implications for our increasingly digital society.

Like Walking a Tightrope: User-Centric Perspectives on Content in Need of Moderation

Anna Maria Planitzer, Svenja Schäfer, Pia Pachinger, Sophie Lecheler

Full text
Norm-violating content on social media platforms is on the rise, fueling prejudice and polarization. To counter such developments in a timely manner, platforms rely on automated content moderation. The issue, however, is that current understandings of automated moderation are mostly elite-driven, neglecting individual user perspectives, introducing biases and discrimination, and overlooking that users’ perspectives on what constitutes a norm violation in the first place may differ substantially from those of experts and platforms. Therefore, this study uses focus groups to explore what type of content users themselves think is in need of moderation. Findings suggest that users, while acknowledging the importance of moderating polarized topics (e.g., migration) and uncivil discourse styles (e.g., insults), are more concerned with the motivations of perpetrators and the vulnerability of targets. Our findings show that, if platforms want to incorporate user perspectives when designing automated moderation systems, they must move beyond merely detecting problematic message characteristics and focus on more tailored approaches, alongside accountability measures for perpetrators and safety measures for victims.

Scrolling for the Truth: Source Factors, Persuasion, and a Test of Misinformation Interventions on TikTok

Brittany N. Shivers Weinstein, Bartosz W. Wojdynski

Full text
The popularity of the video-based social media platform TikTok has rapidly grown in recent years, and with it its role in rapidly disseminating misinformation to its relatively young user base. However, little is known to date about how users perceive misinformation or about how platform interventions could be deployed to stop its spread. Two experiments examined users’ perceptions of TikTok videos making claims about a current health issue (monkeypox). Study 1 tested the impact of information type (true vs. false) and source (journalist vs. influencer) on users’ perceptions of TikTok videos, with party identification as a moderator. Participants rated message credibility and source quality higher for true videos and videos with a journalistic source. Study 2 tested the efficacy of three potential misinformation interventions (a banner label, pre-roll video, and click-through warning) and the role of psychological reactance. There was a main effect of the banner label and public service announcement pre-roll video on perceived message credibility. The interventions had no main effect on source quality, but there was a mediation of the PSA pre-roll and banner label on credibility and quality perceptions through reactance. In both studies, participants had difficulty discerning between true and false claims. Implications for journalists and social media companies are discussed, along with suggestions for future research.

Predicting Irritation Disclosures in Romantic Relationships: Testing and Extending the Revelation Risk Model

Rudy C. Pett, Anita L. Vangelisti

Full text
Feelings of irritation often emerge in romantic relationships. However, the theoretical mechanisms predicting whether individuals disclose these feelings to their romantic partner remain limited and largely reliant on examining indicators of future behavior rather than actual future behavior. Using a two-month, longitudinal survey design involving 448 participants, the current study tested the Revelation Risk Model (RRM) as a mechanism for predicting whether individuals would disclose or conceal feelings of irritation about their romantic partner. Relationship power was also incorporated as an additional predictor. The original RRM components (i.e., closeness, risk, communication efficacy, willingness to disclose) collectively predicted whether individuals disclosed or concealed feelings of irritation about their romantic partner. Relationship power predicted individuals’ communication efficacy, as well as perceived risk associated with disclosing their feelings of irritation. The findings are discussed in terms of their contributions to existing research on disclosure, irritations, and power in romantic relationships.

Media Use for Strong and Weak Ties: A Configural Examination of Personal Media System and Well-Being

Ceciley X. Zhang, Ronald E. Rice

Full text
Taking a configural approach to personal media use, this study examined how strong- and weak-tie media configuration relate to digital stress and well-being. Using a sample of 1,197 U.S. adults (analytic N = 1,143), we explored how media configuration attributes (size, density, and centralization by tie strength, and homogeneity) are associated with well-being, directly and indirectly through five dimensions of digital stress (overconnectivity, communication overload, deficient self-regulation, self-presentation pressure, and technological frustration). Our results highlight the importance of maintaining a larger, more interconnected, and centralized weak-tie configuration, and overall, a more uniform configuration, for well-being benefits (direct and indirect). Notably, larger weak-tie configuration is indirectly associated with lower well-being through greater technological frustration, while larger strong-tie configuration is indirectly associated with higher well-being through lower technological frustration. These findings emphasize the relational context of communication media use and suggest tie-specific strategies for managing communication flows and supporting well-being.

From Hate Speech to Collective Action Intentions: The Role of Counter-Speech and Accuracy Cues Among Targeted Minorities

Desiree Schmuck, Magdalena Obermaier, Nancy Molina-Rogers, Muniba Saleem

Full text
Online hate speech targeting minority groups poses a significant social identity threat to those affected, often prompting coping strategies like out-group avoidance or derogation, which can harm intergroup relations in diverse societies. However, collective action as an empowering response has received limited attention in this context. Drawing on the social identity model of collective action (SIMCA), this study examines both the mechanisms and boundary conditions of online hate speech’s impact on minority groups. Across two experiments ( N Study1 = 328 Muslims; N Study2 = 583 Latinos), we find that online hate speech increases group-based injustice, which in turn drives collective action intentions, supporting the SIMCA. This effect is amplified when information embedded in the online hate speech is perceived as inaccurate or flagged for inaccuracy. In Study 2, we further demonstrate that counter-speech can reduce group-based injustice and, when paired with low-accuracy hate speech, enhance collective efficacy beliefs.

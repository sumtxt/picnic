Selfish and prosocial individuals both distort their actions’ impact, but selfish distortions can be more contagious

Marius C. Vollberg, Jörg Gross

Full text
People often act without knowing exactly how their actions impact others. Here, across nine preregistered online experiments ( N = 8,769 adults, predominantly US residents), we tested whether people exploit this ambiguity to make themselves look good. On average, selfish individuals inflated the social benefit of their choice while downplaying that of the alternative, reporting the two options as more similar than prosocial individuals did (experiment 1a: b = 0.24, 95% confidence interval 0.14–0.34). Prosocial individuals portrayed their choice as comparatively more socially beneficial; they also inflated their implied relative sacrifice, although less consistently. We observed distortions even before people made a choice and when choices were forced, consistent with distortions reflecting social preferences rather than mere justification. While people differed in whether and how they distorted, distortions favouring selfishness showed evidence of contagion: others adopted them and became more selfish (experiment 6, selfish-serving versus no-distortion: b = 0.49, 95% confidence interval 0.10–0.88). These findings offer a psychological foundation for why polarized positions about the social impact of one’s actions may emerge. When social consequences are open to interpretation, people may predictably form divergent interpretations.

Environmental stochasticity reduces human planning effort

Jordan Lei, Jeroen Olieslagers, Nastaran Arfaei, Daisy Xinlei Lin, Wei Ji Ma

Full text
The world is stochastic, making planning difficult. Despite the ubiquity of stochasticity in real-world environments, it remains an open question how people effectively balance the cognitive costs of planning against its potential benefits when faced with stochasticity. To study this, we designed a planning task where participants face one of three forms of stochasticity commonly encountered in the real world: reliability, volatility, and controllability. We find a robust pattern across all three manipulations: as stochasticity increases, people reduce their planning effort as measured by first-choice response times. To understand the processes underlying this effect, we developed several computational cognitive models to account for participants’ choices. We find that rather than calculating expected values optimally, people choose a simpler strategy, acting as if the world were deterministic, a phenomenon known as determinizing. Consistent with our response time findings, we find that people reduce their effort by decreasing their sensitivity to values with increasing stochasticity, a signature of policy compression. Our work highlights the often overlooked role of stochasticity in human planning and its impact on planning strategy and effort. Moreover, it reveals the limitations of studying stochasticity solely through single-shot decisions.

Religious celibacy is linked to marriage and wealth transmission practices across different world religions

Gabriel Šaffa, Sara Sherbaji, Ruth Mace

Full text
Lifelong religious celibacy may seem to be an evolutionary puzzle. However, it is possible that where siblings compete for parental resources, families can benefit from having some offspring become celibate monks or nuns. Here, we test whether celibacy is more likely to be found in societies with substantial marriage transactions, lower levels of polygyny, and heritable wealth, especially land. Additionally, we predict male celibacy to be linked to polygyny and bride-wealth, and female celibacy to monogamy or polyandry and to dowry. Using Bayesian phylogenetic multilevel models on a cross-cultural sample of 352 societies, we find that celibacy occurs mainly in monogamous societies with heritable wealth, and male and female celibacy are more common where marriage payments are higher for the celibate sex. Buddhism and Christianity are especially likely to institutionalize celibacy, suggesting that the emergence of celibate religious institutions reflects common responses to similar socio-ecological conditions across cultures.

Classifying strategy use in multiattribute subjective choice: Application to conjoint experiments in political science

Nidhi V. Banavar, Kirk Bansak

Full text
Conjoint experiments are extensively used across multiple disciplines to make inferences about preferences over multiattribute alternatives. Much previous research has analyzed the importance of attributes at the aggregate level. Here, we focus on understanding individual-level behavior and, specifically, on strategy use. We present a Bayesian cognitive model of strategy use that performs attribute weight inference (how important an attribute is), attribute level inference (which value of the attribute is favored), and latent-mixture inference to classify strategy use among well-established strategies in cognitive science. We then validate and apply our model to two conjoint experiments ( N = 3,762) focusing on three political domains (attitudes toward immigrants, political candidates, and climate policy). We find that the simplest heuristic strategy is the highest probability in all conditions and that approximately 67–76% of subjects are classified as using different strategies depending on the political domain. Our model additionally highlights how aggregate-level inference on the weight of an attribute can be meaningfully decomposed by individual differences in strategy use and preferences over attributes. In addition, our experiments include a set of experimental design manipulations that increase different dimensions of complexity and show their impact on strategy classification.

The economic returns to citizenship: Evidence from a meta-analysis

Jens Hainmueller, David D. Laitin

Full text
Citizenship is widely summarized as producing a “naturalization premium” for immigrants, but naturalization is selected: immigrants who naturalize differ in language, residence, employment, and motivation. We provide a quantitative meta-analysis of citizenship effects on immigrant earnings, synthesizing 60 preferred estimates from 31 studies. How a study separates the effect of citizenship from selection into citizenship—its identification strategy—explains more variation in estimates than destination country, citizenship regime, or any other study feature we code. Averaging published estimates with a multilevel random-effects model yields a pooled premium of 9.9% (95% CI 5.1 to 14.9), and 11.0% (95% CI 5.7 to 16.6) when pooling within study-country cells. But this average conceals sharp differences across research designs. The randomized encouragement study relying on a lottery to overcome selection implies a near-zero effect of 1.9% (95% CI −1.3 to 5.3); the regression-discontinuity study yields 11.3% (95% CI 5.2 to 17.8); estimates from policy reforms and within-unit panels are positive on average but too imprecise to distinguish from either zero or the pooled average; and observational instrumental-variable estimates average 53.0% (95% CI 27.6 to 83.5). Excluding observational instrumental-variable estimates reduces the pooled premium to 6.0% (95% CI 3.8 to 8.2). Reanalyzing the randomized trial data shows the same pattern: Experimental estimates are near zero, while an observational difference-in-differences comparison based on self-selected citizenship uptake implies a 9.6% gain (95% CI 4.0 to 15.5) four years post lottery, a bias standard pretrend tests do not detect. The published citizenship premium is positive, but its size and causal interpretation depend on research design.

A harmonized European database of education, telework, labour market and earnings from three Eurostat surveys

Amado Alarcón Alarcón, Jordi Pascual-Fontanilles

Full text
VSET is a harmonized cross-survey profile-space resource documenting the alignment of education, occupation, labour-market, telework, establishment size, and earnings variables from three Eurostat surveys. It combines three Eurostat microdata sources accessed under an approved research proposal: the Adult Education Survey (AES) 2022, the Labour Force Survey (LFS) 2023, and the Structure of Earnings Survey (SES) 2018. The final harmonized database contains data for 22 countries, 1,440,134 unique demographic profile cells, 39 variables, and a summed profile multiplicity of 3,249,634. Occupations are standardized using ISCO-08 at the 2-digit level, educational attainment is harmonized into four ISCED-based groupings, and SES earnings are converted to euros before aggregation. The integration workflow uses sequential outer joins and deterministic coalescing of overlapping variables. The final rows describe harmonized profiles rather than linked individuals. The N_instances variable records the multiplicity of identical rows combined during final profile compression. The release has no integrated survey weight. Its rows represent harmonized profile combinations rather than linked respondents or population units, and the dataset is intended for inspecting category mappings, profile coverage, merge compatibility, and reproducible cross-survey processing rather than for population inference.

Natural disasters and the two dimensions of redistributive demands: evidence from a major earthquake

Irene Pañeda-Fernández

Full text
Whether natural hazards raise redistributive demands remains poorly understood. I argue that the distinction between taking from the rich and giving to the poor is essential to understand the impact of disasters. I expect natural disasters to not spark demands to “soak the rich” because their unfair advantage is less visible than is the case after other economic shocks. I theorize natural hazards raise demands to redistribute to the poor because these events force close experiences with socio-economic others. Combining variation in exposure to Japan’s 2011 devastating earthquake with representative panel data, results are consistent with my propositions. Even though beliefs in the role of luck increased across the board, demands to redistribute from the rich decreased after the disaster and demands for pro-poor redistribution increased in affected areas. These findings set natural hazards apart from other types of economic shocks such as job loss, financial crises, or mass warfare.

A name too big or too small: status-quality discrepancy and aspirational pricing

Pengfei Wang

Full text
Economic sociologists have long emphasized a positive but loose coupling between status and quality, prompting scholars to rule out quality in the status research. However, limited attention is paid to the implications of their loose coupling per se. We underscore that the loose coupling implies a status-quality discrepancy: some actors’ status positions are over-ranked relative to their quality (status inflation), while others are under-ranked (status deflation). The status-quality discrepancy warrants attention because it can place important constraints on actors’ behavior. Specifically, we maintain that status-inflated actors may avoid unconventional, aspirational behavior to shield themselves from potential scrutiny and loss, whereas status-deflated actors embrace it to seek market recognition. Supporting this, our study of fine wineries in Bordeaux shows that wineries with deflated status engage more in aspirational pricing behavior, while those with inflated status tend to shy away from it. Our emphasis on the status-quality discrepancy makes important contributions to the literature on status and aspirational behavior.

Rising wealth concentration and democratic backsliding across U.S. states

Manuel Schechtl

Full text
Wealth concentration in the United States has reached heights not seen since the Gilded Age, renewing fears that concentrated economic power may undermine democracy. Yet empirical research linking inequality to democratic erosion remains inconclusive, partly because most studies focus on cross-country comparison and examine income rather than wealth. This study exploits variation across fifty U.S. states; each embedded in a common federal framework but differing in political and economic dynamics. To estimate the exogenous effect of rising wealth concentration on democratic backsliding, I develop a shift-share instrument of state-level millionaire wealth shares. Results reveal a clear, negative effect on democratic performance. Causal mediation analysis demonstrates that the effect is mediated by the share of seats held by Republicans in state legislatures, pointing to a mechanism of elite influence through partisan channels. These findings demonstrate that subnational wealth disparities, and their convergence with partisan politics, actively erode American democratic institutions.

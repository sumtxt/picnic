American Political Science Review

Structural Injustice as Emergent Disorder

HOCHAN KIM

Full text
Structural injustice remains a puzzling concept despite its increasing prominence. I distill recent theoretical challenges into a dilemma: either structural injustice lacks the normative credentials of injustice given that wrongdoing is inessential, or it is redundant given existing theories that trace injustice to culpable state and other institutional failures. I then develop a novel account of structural injustice, the Complexity View, that unravels this dilemma. The emergent properties of human actions in a complex society explain what is “structural” about structural injustice, specifically how it can arise without (culpable) wrongdoing. Structural injustice is unjust because it is wrong to contribute to social processes that cause harm and to passively accept preventable forms of vulnerability and deprivation as tragic misfortunes. But this account of structural injustice has a sobering implication: while complexity explains what distinguishes structural injustice from other wrongs, it also sets significant epistemic limits on social reform.

Marriage as Slavery: Oroonoko, the Slave Trade, and the Origins of English Feminism

MARY JO MACDONALD

Full text
This article investigates Mary Astell’s controversial comparison of marriage to slavery. While existing scholarship situates this analogy within the neo-Roman republican framework, which defined slavery as subjection to absolute rule, I investigate another paradigm: the transatlantic slave trade. To make this case, the article traces the influence of Aphra Behn’s Oroonoko (1688) on early feminist thought. By foregrounding the market logic underpinning chattel slavery, Behn popularized the idea that slavery’s harms were rooted, not in absolute power, but in the commodification of human beings. Astell appropriated this insight, showing that marriage resembled slavery not because husbands wielded power over wives but because the institution had been commodified: women were treated as things to be bought and sold. In uncovering this genealogy of feminist thought, the article highlights the enduring contributions of the “first” English feminists—insights that are entangled with, and shaped by, the development of the slave trade.

Democratic Accountability with Citizen Coproduction

JIE MA, KEITH E. SCHNAKENBERG, IAN R. TURNER

Full text
Many policies require citizens to actively participate in their implementation in order to be successful, a situation referred to as citizen coproduction. Though citizen coproduction is necessary in many policy areas, it could create difficulties for democratic accountability because citizens and politicians share responsibility for policy failures and attribution of blame may be difficult. We analyze the problem of democratic accountability with citizen coproduction in the context of a model in which politicians, who vary with respect to their competence at selecting policies, choose whether to implement policies whose success depends on citizen coproduction choices. For citizens, the problem is one of attribution: when coproduction is low, it may be hard to select good incumbents because policy failures are frequent even when policy choices are good. Incumbents anticipate these effects, which sometimes implies that they are less likely to choose reforms when they are more likely to be successful.

What Would It Mean to Democratize Finance? An Evaluation of Divergent Conceptions of Democracy and Finance

LEAH DOWNEY, STEFAN EICH

Full text
Calls to “democratize finance” abound today. Yet this apparent consensus obscures sharply divergent conceptions of both democracy and finance. This article maps these demands along two axes, distinguishing between access- and power-based conceptions of democracy, and between three dimensions of finance: payment, capital markets, and banking. We then develop three normative claims. First, we argue that conceptions that reduce democratization to expanded market access are not merely insufficient but, when applied to speculative capital markets, amount to predatory inclusion. Second, thicker accounts of democratization as equal inclusion are normatively more promising but internally unstable, as their democratic commitments generate pressures toward power sharing. Third, the most plausible conceptions of democratizing finance are power-centered and involve greater public control over credit creation and allocation. Rather than defending a single institutional solution, the article assesses a range of reform proposals while specifying a normative threshold for evaluating what it would mean to democratize finance.

British Journal of Political Science

When, Where, and Why Might Elected Political Elites Reach for Democratic Innovations?

Şule Yaylacı, Mark Warren, Edana Beauvais

Full text
Electoral democracies are under strain. Elections and conventional relations of representation between elected politicians and citizens do not seem to be providing sufficient legitimacy for democratic governance. In principle, democratic innovations such as deliberative minipublics or participatory budgeting could supplement electoral systems so that they function more democratically. But elected representatives may resist these innovations due to unfamiliarity, perceived threats to their power, and the belief that traditional electoral methods suffice for democratic legitimacy. This paper theorizes the potential incentives for elected officials to adopt democratic innovations, examining the conditions under which innovations might align with their interests. Democratic deficits may, and often do, present electoral risks and challenges. By mapping democratic deficits onto elite incentives, we suggest when, where, and why the strategic motivations of elected officials might align with democratic innovations. We illustrate the theory with examples of democratic innovations drawn primarily from the Participedia database. We conclude that strategies for deepening democracy should include understanding how democratic innovations relate to incentives structured by electoral institutions, so that innovations might supplement rather than compete with electoral democracy.

Critical Ties: How NGOs Become Reliable Information Suppliers to International Organizations

Florencia Montal, Gino Pauselli, María-José Urzúa

Full text
International organizations (IOs) monitor compliance with international norms but public criticism of member states remains discretionary. We argue that such criticism depends not only on mandates or political incentives, but on whether IOs can access information they deem credible. This credibility is socially produced. Through repeated interaction, IO staff develop trust in specific local non-governmental organizations (NGOs), which become reliable conduits of mandate-aligned and verifiable information. These trust-based ties reduce uncertainty and reputational risk, enabling public criticism. We test this argument using original data on press releases and NGO participation in public hearings at the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights from 1996 to 2020. Combining statistical analysis, an instrumental variable strategy, text analysis, and interview evidence, we show that the Commission is more likely to issue and to sharpen critical press releases when it has established ties with local NGOs focused on the relevant country and issue.

The Legacy of Mexico’s Drug War on Youth Political Attitudes

Omar García-Ponce, Isabel Laterzo-Tingley

Full text
How does childhood exposure to large-scale criminal violence and militarized law enforcement shape political attitudes and behavior? We examine the case of Mexico’s drug war, where a generation has been raised amidst the country’s most violent conflict in over a century. Surveying nearly 3,000 urban youth to measure their political and interpersonal trust, we matched respondents to historical data on criminal violence and military confrontations and operations in their childhood municipalities. Disentangling the effects of criminal violence and military interventions, we find that exposure to homicidal violence during childhood erodes interpersonal trust and trust in law enforcement, while military interventions – especially those involving violent confrontations – broadly undermine trust in federal security institutions and generate political backlash against the parties that governed Mexico during the exposure period. These findings have important implications for young democracies affected by high crime and violence and advance our understanding of how criminal wars influence political socialization.

The Threshold Effect: Preference Falsification Versus Weak Preferences in Russian War Support

Isabelle DeSisto, Grigore Pop-Eleches, Jacob R. Tucker

Full text
How reliable are surveys in autocracies? Using data from wartime Russia, we show why list experiments – commonly used to detect preference falsification – do not always reveal hidden dissent. We identify and test what we call the ‘threshold effect’: respondents may report support for a sensitive item in a direct question but omit it in a list experiment, not because they are lying, but because the list format can create a higher threshold for expressing support. We provide direct evidence of this effect and use two approaches to distinguish preference falsification from weak preferences. Both suggest that outright lying in Russia is limited, but many attitudes are weak and easily shaped by question framing. We conclude that while many Russians genuinely support the war against Ukraine, this support is shallow. Our findings suggest that researchers should take care when designing and interpreting survey questions that measure sensitive attitudes using indirect methods.

The Disappearance of Political Moderates Online: How the Privacy Function of Social Media Amplifies Observed Political Polarization

Fuhai Hong, Shouzhi Xia, Chen Zhang

Full text
This article contends that when the privacy function of social media is used for politically relevant audience curation, users may take more partisan stances than they would otherwise, which therefore amplifies the observed political polarization. The privacy function allows users to selectively disclose their content to like-minded people and hide it from out-groups. We argue that, in a safe space created by using the privacy function, people tend to express more partisan stances to impress their in-groups. Through an experiment conducted in Hong Kong, where political polarization between pro-democracy and pro-establishment camps has intensified over the years, we created a social media-like environment for participants and demonstrated that those who were provided with the privacy function were more likely to disclose content to in-groups than to out-groups and, more importantly, exhibited stronger stances on polarizing issues. This article contributes to the understanding of the relationship between social media and political polarization and emphasizes the need to reassess the robustness of the observed polarization level on social media in various societies.

Closing the Gap: Gender, Race, and Welfare-State Knowledge

Jasmine English

Full text
Decades of research document persistent gender and racial gaps in political knowledge. However, a growing literature shows that these gaps depend on the content of political knowledge batteries. Building on these insights, I introduce a measure of welfare-state knowledge that captures understanding of what welfare programs do, whom they serve, and how political authority is organized. Using an original survey in the United States, I compare welfare-state knowledge with canonical political knowledge and find pronounced shifts in group-knowledge patterns. Women score lower on canonical items but outperform men on welfare-state knowledge, thereby reversing the gender gap. The racial gap narrows substantially in the welfare domain, with racial minorities scoring indistinguishably from white respondents. These findings challenge prevailing narratives about group-knowledge deficits by revealing domains of political expertise that canonical measures overlook and underscoring how measurement choices shape whose political experiences are represented in the study of public opinion.

Think Globally, Act Locally: The Determinants of Local Policy Makers’ Support for Climate Policy

Sabrina B. Arias, Joshua A. Schwartz

Full text
Although much progress to combat climate change has occurred subnationally, little research examines the policy preferences of local policy makers themselves. To address this gap, we conduct a conjoint experiment on over five hundred elected local policy makers across the United States, including mayors, county executives, and council members. Per our theoretical expectations, we demonstrate that the probability of policy adoption can be increased by strategic design. Most notably, climate-related subsidies and regulations are preferred over taxes and penalties, suggesting efforts to put a price on carbon may not be the most effective political strategy in terms of obtaining support. Partisan endorsements of climate plans also have a large effect, greater in some cases than substantive policy design, consistent with increasing polarization. Most interestingly, there is a significant backlash effect—even among Democratic policy makers—when climate plans are endorsed by only the Democratic Party rather than by neither party or by both parties. While direct economic relief to the public is popular among policy makers, it does not increase support for bolder climate policies. We also uncover salient differences between Democratic and Republican policy makers. For example, Republicans are more likely than Democrats to support delayed implementation and are less likely to support plans with high short-term costs even if the long-term benefits are high. Finally, we conducted an identical replication of our elite experiment among the general public and found policy maker preferences are highly compatible with those of average citizens. Our study provides one of the few causally identified tests of local policy makers’ climate policy preferences.

Toward Solidarity in Democratic Theory and Practice

John N. Medearis

Full text
I seek to demonstrate that solidarity should be understood as a vital good of democracy, one that is internally, inextricably related to the more familiar democratic values of equality and freedom. Acknowledging solidarity as a vital democratic good, I argue, involves recognizing that democracy entails political agents continually creating for each other the conditions to act as free equals. I build my democratic case for solidarity on Jürgen Habermas’s argument for the co-originality of solidarity with freedom and equality. In particular, I show how a concept crucial to Habermas’s argument, interdependence, can be translated from its original context—discourse ethics and moral agency—to the context of politics and political agency. In doing so, I posit three essential forms of political interdependence: practical, skillful, and ideological interdependence. Drawing on this translation of Habermas’s theory, I explore an immanent movement toward solidarity in three distinct new literatures in political theory that make a place for contentious, extra-institutional practices in democracy. This exploration sets the stage for elaborating a new conception of solidarity highlighting political interdependence.

American Public Support for Higher Education in the Trump Era

Leonie Huddy, Seden Yilmazturk

Full text
Political science researchers have devoted little attention to studying public opinion toward higher education, an oversight we address in this review of recent opinion trends. We demonstrate the complexity of public attitudes toward higher education, which reflect a paradox: declining confidence and negative assessments of the sector have combined with positive ratings for college degrees and for the medical and scientific breakthroughs that occur on college campuses. Partisans are polarized in their broad assessment of higher education and the presence of political bias in the classroom, but they are far less divided on other aspects of higher education, indicating that only some attitudes toward higher education have become politicized in the Trump era. The study of public opinion toward higher education sheds light on a policy area of growing political importance and underscores its potential as a rich topic for political behavior researchers.

From Courtiers to Courtship: Tory Feminism and the Political Dangers of Flattery

Geertje Jeanne Bol, Mary Jo MacDonald

Full text
Flattery appears to be a trivial vice, yet it receives a curious amount of attention in contemporary political analysis. Scholars often view flattery as a symptom of autocratic regimes, an association with a much longer history. In seventeenth-century England, republicans likewise understood the courtier’s flattery as a humiliating sign of slavish dependence on the ruler. This article complicates this position by turning to an understudied perspective in the history of political thought: Tory feminism. We show how two Tory feminists, Mary Astell and Judith Drake, challenged the standard republican narrative by drawing on a broader set of experiences—namely, the dynamics of flattery in courtship. We argue that they used the example of courtship to isolate the true wrong of flattery: its employment to subordinate the flattered. Astell and Drake applied this insight to the political realm, contending that the most morally troubling forms of flattery emerge not in monarchical courts, but in republican political spaces. Recovering the perspectives of Astell and Drake is not merely a matter of diversifying the canon; it also shows how gendered assumptions have shaped our conceptual history of flattery. A more complete account of flattery—its value, dangers, and the political environments in which it flourishes—requires attention to the insights of these Tory women.

The political class in citizens’ minds: Mapping public perceptions of politicians

Mads Nørgaard

Full text
Politicians are often portrayed as a socially distinct and internally homogeneous elite – a narrative central to populist rhetoric and scholarly debates on political discontent. Yet we know little about how voters mentally represent politicians. Using survey data from Denmark ( N = 1302), combining free association tasks, open-ended descriptions, and closed-ended items, I demonstrate that most citizens view politicians as both internally homogeneous and socially distinct from the general population. These perceptions correlate with negative characterisations and significantly lower political trust, external efficacy, and democratic satisfaction. Crucially, perceived social distinctiveness matters more than perceived homogeneity: citizens who view politicians as different from ordinary people show substantially diminished political support, regardless of whether they see politicians as similar to one another. By shifting the analytical focus to citizens’ mental representations, this study highlights the importance of not only who politicians are but also how they are imagined by the public.

Do voting advice applications have durable causal effects? Evidence from concurrent survey and field experiments

Micha Germann, Iulia Cioroianu, Fernando Mendez, Constantinos Djouvas

Full text
Voting advice applications (VAAs) are used by millions of voters, yet their causal effects on political knowledge, preferences, and behavior remain unclear. We present new evidence from two experiments conducted during the 2024 UK General Election: (1) a panel survey experiment that provides access to participants’ VAA advice, achieves high compliance with treatment assignment, and allows us to track outcomes over time; and (2) a concurrent field experiment embedded in a real-world VAA that allows us to assess the ecological validity of our findings. We find that VAA usage leads to durable changes in voters’ perception of how close they are to parties on policy issues, shifts party preferences in line with the advice received, and affects the vote choices of undecided voters. These results suggest that VAAs play an important role in election campaigns. However, we find no evidence that VAAs increase electoral participation.

Diaspora diplomacy in the making: The Case of Latvian diaspora professionals working in international organisations

Inta Mieriņa, Baiba Bela

Full text
Nationals living abroad are increasingly recognised as important, yet often overlooked, actors in international diplomacy. Unlike previous research – which has focused mainly on states and diasporas as singular entities – this study highlights the pivotal contributions of individuals. It introduces a new theoretical framework that emphasises the multifaceted roles of professionals in international organisations as resources for strengthening their home state’s development, influence, and global visibility. The study examines the actors involved in diaspora diplomacy, how diplomatic work is conducted by, for, and through diasporas, and what motivates citizen diplomats. The analysis draws on a survey ( N = 150), six focus groups ( N = 18), and four interviews with highly skilled Latvian professionals in international organisations. Findings reveal the decentralised, networked nature of diaspora diplomacy, challenging the notion of the state or diaspora as a singular actor. Individual diaspora members play vital roles, advancing their home country’s goals and visibility through their professional activities. All core functions of public diplomacy – communication, representation, listening, advocacy, and nation branding – are actively performed at the individual level by citizen diplomats. However, the degree to which states benefit from this resource depends on having a coordinated strategy for diaspora engagement.

Terrorist threat decreases citizens’ relative preferences for women candidates: Insights from a Danish conjoint experiment

Brian Littmann, Nadia Natasha James Nielsen, Lasse Laustsen

Full text
For several decades, democracies across the Globe have faced the persistent threat from terrorist attacks. A possible consequence of terrorist threat is that citizens come to find men candidates relatively more appealing compared to women candidates. However, recent meta-analyses reveal that citizens generally prefer women over men candidates when terrorist threat is absent. Here, we report the results from a pre-registered conjoint experiment testing whether the terrorist risk assessment from the official Danish intelligence authority (Politiets Efterretningstjeneste, PET) affects candidate gender preferences among a sample of 1,034 representative Danes. Results reveal an average marginal component effect of 8.6 percentage points favoring women candidates in the absence of terrorist threat. However, when the risk of terrorism against Denmark is made salient, this effect is significantly reduced to 5.3 percentage points. Follow-up analyses reveal that left-wing respondents change their candidate gender preferences most when terrorist threat becomes salient. Implications for challenges to political representation are discussed.

How left-wing parties shape the voting gender gap across Western Europe

Davide Angelucci, Ester Flumeri

Full text
Empirical evidence suggests that the traditional gender gap in voting behaviour has been reversed in many advanced democracies. Cultural and structural factors have led the younger generations of women to shift from voting more conservatively than men to being more likely to support left-wing parties. Although these transformations have affected virtually all advanced democracies, empirical evidence shows that this gap is not uniform across time and space; there is significant variation in both the presence and strength of the association. In this paper, we argue that this variability can be partly explained by the role of left-wing political parties. In particular, we hypothesise that the modern gender gap is stronger when left-wing parties adopt progressive positions on both the socio-cultural and economic dimensions of politics. To test these hypotheses, 19 Western European countries are analysed using a dataset that integrates individual-level data from the European Social Survey (Waves 1–10) with party-level data from the Comparative Manifesto Project, covering 85 elections between 1999 and 2021. The results reveal that the modern gender gap is shaped by party positioning, with left-wing parties’ positions on both the economic and, most relevantly, the socio-cultural dimension playing a key role in reinforcing or mitigating gendered voting patterns.

Online privacy protection: How the varieties of democracy matter

Ahmed Maati, Stefan Wurster

Full text
Digital technologies have enabled the gathering, storing, and processing of large amounts of data in increasing and unprecedented ways. This has triggered concerns regarding governments’ ability to harvest vast amounts of information on citizens and the consequences of this surveillance on democracy. The reliance of many democracies on digital (surveillance) technologies in response to the 2019 Coronavirus pandemic has amplified these concerns. While most studies focus on how privacy violations affect democracy, this study analyzes how variations in democracy affect the legal protection of citizens’ rights. We rely on data from the varieties of democracy to capture this variation and on data from the Digital Society Project to measure variations in online privacy protection laws. Our results point to a complex and partially unexpected relationship between democracy, its different dimensions, and online privacy protection laws in democratic regimes. The quality of democracy is positively associated with protective online privacy laws in typical times, but changes in democratic quality in the years preceding the pandemic did not affect changes in online privacy laws during the pandemic. However, the relationship between the different dimensions of democracy and privacy laws is more complex, and our results show that egalitarianism and participation are more important than liberalism in protecting online privacy laws. We conclude with a discussion of the relevance of these results to policy.

Pocketbook politics and climate action: How economic strain reduces support for green parties

Frederik Thieme

Full text
Why do green parties struggle electorally despite overwhelming public support for climate action? While macro-level studies suggest that green party performance is shaped by economic conditions, the micro-level mechanisms driving this relationship remain unclear. This paper investigates whether personal economic strain reduces support for green parties and reshapes climate-related political attitudes. Leveraging rich panel data from Germany (2016–2024), I combine long-term correlational analysis with a difference-in-differences design exploiting the war-related 2022 surge in consumer prices. Voters facing financial hardship consistently evaluate the Greens more negatively and shift their political attention toward inflation and economic growth, while deprioritizing climate protection. However, evidence on vote intentions is mixed. These findings contribute to debates on green party support and the distributive politics of climate action, highlighting the relevance of voters’ pocketbooks and the need for green parties to convincingly address material concerns to ensure electoral success.

The making of strange bedfellows: spatial diffusion in unorthodox coalition building

Gissur Ó Erlingsson, Emanuel Wittberg, Hanna Emilia Bäck, Assar Wixe

Full text
Why do ideologically distant political adversaries sometimes choose to govern together? This article advances research on coalition formation in parliamentary democracies by introducing a diffusion-of-innovation perspective. We argue that cooperation between political archenemies can spread spatially as parties learn from nearby cases. When sister parties in adjacent jurisdictions successfully form such coalitions without electoral punishment or sanction from national leaderships, the normative barriers to similar cooperation are weakened. In this way, unorthodox coalitions become politically imaginable and strategically viable through processes of observation, emulation, and learning. We test this argument in the context of Swedish local government, where an extraordinary transformation has occurred over the past few decades. Historically, cooperation between the Social Democrats and the Moderates – the two dominant rivals for national power – has been virtually unthinkable. Today, however, these former adversaries co-govern in nearly one-fifth of Sweden’s 290 municipalities. This shift in coalition behaviour challenges core expectations in coalition theory, which predict alliances between ideologically proximate parties. Drawing on a unique panel dataset covering eight local election cycles (1994–2022), we employ complementary log-log regression models to test whether exposure to unorthodox coalitions in the surrounding regional environment increases the likelihood of similar coalition formation. The analysis reveals clear evidence of hierarchical regional contagion: Social Democrat–Moderate coalitions are significantly more likely to emerge in administrative regions where similar arrangements existed in the previous term. These findings suggest that coalition formation is a socially embedded process of spatial learning, offering a new account of how political cooperation evolves across space.

Quarterly Journal of Political Science

Coloring in the lines: the role of segregation and majority-minority districts in local descriptive representation

Gustavo F. Novoa

Full text
The urban politics literature finds municipal elections are highly racialized, but it is unclear how this is reflected in redistricting. I analyze the implementation of majority-minority districts in 133 US cities during the 2010 redistricting cycle. I find that in cities where Black or Hispanic citizens are minorities, they are proportionally underrepresented in city council districts, but are overrepresented in cities where they make up the majority. To isolate the role of residential segregation, I use a Monte Carlo algorithm to sample from the underlying distribution of plausible maps. The proportion of Hispanic-majority districts implemented generally matches median simulated plans, but nearly one-third of cities implemented more Black-majority districts than the median simulation. The proportion of Black city council members positively predicts the gap between the median proportion of majority-minority districts simulated and the proportion implemented. I then link this data to the electoral outcomes of racially identified candidates. I find that majority-minority and majority-White districts elect co-racial or co-ethnic candidates in over 75% of elections (n = 2,434). Finally, I predict the number of Black and Hispanic councilors elected under simulated plans. Implemented plans led to an estimated 9.5% more Black councilors on average, but roughly the same number of Hispanic councilors.

Political Science Research and Methods

The effects of participating in government-led citizens’ assemblies

Mariana C. Duarte, Jorge M. Fernandes, Miguel M. Pereira

Full text
Contemporary democracies face growing public disaffection and mistrust. Governments have tried to revert these trends by promoting participatory governance. Prior studies established how deliberation can empower citizens. However, whether these effects hold in real-life initiatives initiated by partisan governments remains unclear. We test the ability of citizens’ assemblies to foster democratic attitudes in a natural experiment leveraging the random selection of participants to Lisbon’s citizens’ assemblies Participation led to an increase in issue knowledge and salience, external political efficacy and institutional trust. However, it did not impact internal political efficacy nor helped citizens better recognize the challenges of collective decision-making. The results hold for two assembly editions and reveal the potential of government-led participatory initiatives to reconnect citizens with democracy.

Contact as collective action: Positive and negative contact experiences of disadvantaged group members

Fiona Kazarovytska, Micha Hilbert, Dana Ionescu

Full text
Intergroup contact programs worldwide strive to combat prejudice by promoting encounters between socially advantaged and disadvantaged groups. Despite their prevalence, little is known about how disadvantaged group members experience participation in such programs. We present qualitative field data from the German contact program Meet a Jew . In this program, Jewish volunteers engage in dialogue with attendees and share insights into their Jewish life to address antisemitism. Drawing on N = 450 volunteer responses (age range: 16–77 years) collected over five years (2020–2025), we examine the positive and negative experiences of volunteers with the arranged encounters. A qualitative content analysis shows that volunteers experienced contact as positive when their engagement and perspectives were met with interest, their efforts were socially recognized, and they perceived their engagement as effective in contributing to social change. However, volunteers also reported negative experiences, including disinterest from attendees, interference by authorities, and various conflicts. By providing the perspective of Jewish individuals who are both targets of prejudice and actors of prejudice reduction, we address the understudied perspective of disadvantaged group members who engage in contact interventions as agents of collective action. We discuss how our work contributes to contact theory and advances research on social change.

A complex arc of engagement: Shifting psychological trajectories of international solidarity during sustained geopolitical conflict

Vivian P. Ta‐Johnson, A. Burcu Bayram

Full text
How do international audiences psychologically engage with prolonged geopolitical conflicts on social media? Drawing on 1.2+ million English‐language tweets posted during the first 10 months of the Russia–Ukraine war, this study examines temporal shifts in engagement, emotion, existential concern, cognition, and psychological distance reflected through language. Results identified a clear psychological arc across the conflict. Discourse in the earliest phase was characterized by strong active engagement, heightened negative emotion, increased focus on death, conflict, and morality, and greater cognitive effort aimed at making sense of unfolding events. As the war persisted, these indicators declined and were replaced by shorter posts, fewer retweets, reduced existential focus, and simpler emotional and cognitive expression, consistent with attention fatigue and chronic crisis adaptation. Unexpectedly, psychological distance decreased over time, suggesting that users expressed themselves in more personal, spontaneous ways as the conflict became an enduring part of the digital environment. This challenges the view that declining engagement signals general disengagement and instead reveals a shift toward more grounded, personally meaningful forms of involvement. Findings illuminate the dynamic psychological processes through which publics respond to sustained conflict and highlight implications for political communication, humanitarian outreach, and crisis messaging strategies across the lifespan of international crises.

Party Politics

Digitalisation without democratisation: Organisational conditions for deliberative override capacity in European parties

Gema Sánchez Medero

Full text
Contemporary political parties are digitalising their internal processes at an accelerating rate, yet higher levels of digitalisation do not consistently translate into deeper internal democracy. This article builds a diagnostic framework around deliberative override capacity (DOC), the organisational conditions under which members can access, contest, and reverse technically mediated decisions. Combining an exploratory analysis of the DIGIPART dataset (n = 76) with a theory-building statutory analysis of 18 parties across seven countries, we find that only two parties exhibit the full statutory configuration associated with robust DOC, while most combine formal participatory commitments with executive or proprietary control of the digital infrastructure. A DOC proxy applied to the seventy-six parties indicates that DOC captures variation existing indices do not: parties scoring highly on democratic digital-innovation indices may nonetheless exhibit governance indeterminacy, that is, a formal participatory commitment without any statutory specification of who controls the digital infrastructure. We develop a heuristic typology of override configurations across ideological families and draw out its implications for party-organisation research and party law. The design is configurational and cross-sectional; we identify co-occurrence patterns rather than causal effects.

Partisan differentiation in legislative texts in South Korea: Agenda selection, framing, and partisan classifiability in the 21st national assembly

Sangjoon Ka

Full text
How do parties translate their ideological identities into the language of legislation? This study analyzes 22,964 member-sponsored bills from the Democratic Party of Korea and the People Power Party in the 21st Korean National Assembly, decomposing partisan differences into three dimensions: agenda setting, framing, and partisan classifiability. The study finds significant divergence in topic distributions across parties, with the Democratic Party prioritizing judicial reform and labor rights while the People Power Party emphasizes real estate taxation and regional development. More substantively novel is the evidence for within-topic framing differences: even after agenda composition is held constant, keyword contrasts within shared policy domains reveal recurring asymmetries in problem diagnosis and justificatory language. A text-only classification model further confirms that partisan identity is consistently encoded in legislative texts. These findings indicate that agenda selection and within-topic framing combine to produce a consistent partisan signal. Party discipline, decentralized incentives for position-taking, and ideological polarization constitute plausible institutional and political conditions under which such a pattern may emerge, with broader implications for comparative research on party-centered democracies.

The party soldier, the young careerist, and the outsider: How distinctive career paths affect party dissent

Iakovos Makropoulos

Full text
Parties are not unified entities, yet research on intra-party dissent has focused on legislators while overlooking the broader pool of candidates and the distinct career paths that bring them into politics. Drawing on theories of careerism, representation, and spatial competition, I argue that pre-parliamentary careers shape both whether candidates dissent from their party and in which direction. Using the Comparative Candidates Survey across thirteen country-elections, I find that party soldiers with local roots are the most loyal, whereas young careerists who have never worked outside politics are the most likely to dissent. Crucially, when careerists break with their party, they move toward the centre rather than the extremes, and they do so most when their electoral chances are high. These findings link the professionalisation of political careers to party cohesion and candidate-voter congruence.

A Habit-Building View of Democracy: Mary Parker Follett and Motor Activities

Etienne Cardin-Trudeau

Full text
Citizens of democracies should live democratic lives and develop democratic habits. That is one of Mary Parker Follett’s core insights on democracy. A child of the Gilded Age, Follett experienced the erosion of communal relationships, the intensification of socio-economic inequalities, and the alienation of ordinary people from the complex political system that governed their lives. She saw democracy become remote and foreign to citizens who had little control over what happened to them. Her solution was simple: to reinject a dose of democracy into the everyday life of citizens. This paper retrieves Follett’s embodied and habitual vision of democracy for contemporary political theory. Follett claimed that citizens should participate actively in the democratic life of their society; however, she conceived of democratic participation as going beyond decision-making and deliberative activities. She thought that participation in simple forms of embodied joint actions, like recreation and collective chores, could also benefit democracy. Drawing from Follett, I argue that participation in a wide variety of embodied practices of collective action provides benefits to individuals and society by instilling democratic habits in citizens. Those habits incline those who develop them to enter and maintain relationships as free and equal peers with all sorts of people to reach common ends. When faced with collective problems, therefore, these individuals are more likely to choose democratic collective action and to do so more effectively.

The Perils of Improvement: Burke, Slavery, and Colonial Governance in the West Indies

Ross Carroll

Full text
Edmund Burke’s thoughts on plantation slavery have long been the subject of scholarly attention. Too often, however, discerning Burke’s long-term intention in relation to slavery has taken precedence over examining the specific administrative strategies he proposed for managing enslaved populations in the interim. Seeking to remedy this gap, the article reads Burke’s Sketch for a Negro Code as an exercise in colonial ameliorationism; that is, a reform programme that looked to ameliorate the institution of slavery as a way of strengthening British colonialism in the West Indies and civilising the African coast. Using colonial ameliorationism as an interpretive lens, I argue, allows us to reconstruct a consistent Burkean vision for colonial management in the West Indies, one that survived his vacillations on the question of abolition. It also throws into doubt the common argument that Burke saw imperial governance in negative terms; a matter of taming abuses of power and exercising restraint. In the West Indies, by contrast, Burke’s commitment to ameliorationism led him to embrace a productive form of colonial power aimed at enhancing the physical and mental capacities of the enslaved population through discipline, religious instruction, and the encouragement of biological reproduction. I conclude by showing how Burke’s faith in the improvability of the slave system is vulnerable to his own criticisms of other projects for political reform; namely that a certain guilelessness can blind reformers to the dangers inherent in their projects and reconcile them to what they purport to oppose.

European Union Politics

The disciplining effects of European fiscal governance on national pension policymaking

Igor Guardiancich, Igor Tkalec, Eugenio Borgognoni

Full text
While pensions are electorally risky to cut back, they are also the largest budget item in most European Union countries, making them central to fiscal stability in the Eurozone. Despite these tensions, member states have since the 1990s implemented convergent cost-containment reforms, showing how supranational pressures force decision-makers to retrench. But what happens when these pressures fade? Using an original dataset linking pension-related country-specific recommendations to reform events, we find that excessive deficit oversight and higher sovereign bond yields encouraged reforms strengthening long-term pension system sustainability. As these twin constraints weakened under the Juncker Commission and vanished during the pandemic-related suspension of the Stability and Growth Pact, policy reversals emerged, undermining the alignment between European Union recommendations and domestic implementation and leading to higher projected pension spending.

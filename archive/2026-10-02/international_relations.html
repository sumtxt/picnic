Review of International Organizations

Influence by omission: The IMF’s lending capacity and central bank design

Ana Carolina Garriga, Michael A. Gavin

Full text
A large literature explores how loan conditionalities and policy recommendations embedded in International Monetary Fund lending programs influence country behavior and policy choices. We argue that the IMF’s influence extends beyond these intentional efforts. This paper shows that the growth in the IMF’s lending capacity has failed to keep pace with financial globalization, and that this has incentivized emerging and developing economies to strengthen their domestic institutions for financial stability, particularly, their central bank’s capabilities to act as a lender of last resort. We conceptualize this as influence by omission , whereby the IMF shapes behavior not through direct engagement but through its declining ability to serve as an effective financial backstop. Using original data coding central bank lender of last resort powers for 60 developing countries between 1994 and 2020, we find that countries with relatively limited access to IMF resources are significantly more likely to strengthen their central banks’ lender of last resort authority. This finding is robust across a range of model specifications, instrumental variable analyses, and dynamic estimations. An event study of countries’ response to the Covid shock reveals that countries with stronger lending of last resort capabilities were much more likely to manage the crisis without drawing on IMF resources. Importantly, this effect is specific to lender of last resort powers and does not extend to other aspects of central bank governance such as independence or transparency, suggesting that distinct international and domestic incentives shape different reform trajectories.

Journal of Peace Research

Crises and regime instability: autocratization and democratization pathways across political regimes

Guido Panzano, Luca Tomini

Full text
To what extent are political regimes vulnerable to crises? Established research has explored in depth how crises can compromise regime stability, from Linz's foundational studies to contemporary investigations. Yet, such contributions remain scattered across unconnected concepts, methods, and applications, thus hindering cumulative knowledge. This article crafts a coherent examination of different crises and their impacts on regime instability, with a twofold contribution: first, it synthesizes the findings of such a large literature; second, it advances them through a single framework and an extensive empirical test of their external validity. Applying various indicators of economic, violent, and disaster-related crises, our global study implements a broad range of time-series cross-sectional regression models on direct and moderated effects on different types of regime instability (alteration in democracy levels, democratic upturns/downturns, democratization/autocratization episodes, and democratic breakdown/transition) in democracies and autocracies. The findings not only confirm but also unveil multiple crises-related pathways of regime change, especially for economic and violent crises, and a generally greater fragility of autocracies compared to democracies, both in the face of the impact of a single crisis and in the occurrence of simultaneous crises. This reassessment bridges previous examinations, offers important policy suggestions, and opens up ways for further inquiries.

Securing support, avoiding harm? Civilian support, community organization, and rebel landmine use in Colombia

Henrique Garbino

Full text
How do armed groups manage the trade-off between securing and accessing support from civilian populations and minimizing the risk of exposing them to violence? While rebels often depend on local communities, their proximity exposes civilians to significant risks. This article addresses this dilemma by examining the use of landmines by Colombian guerrilla groups. Although landmines are expected to offer an effective and low-cost means to offset asymmetries in civil wars, their indiscriminate nature threatens the very communities on which rebels rely. I argue that while rebels are more likely to lay minefields where they enjoy greater local support, they should also attempt to reduce risks to civilians in these areas, especially when faced with stronger resistance from local communities. Using cross-sectional, subnational data from Colombia's national mine action program, I show that guerrilla groups indeed laid more landmines in more supportive municipalities. However, while such support does not appear to correlate with restraint in landmine use, higher levels of community organization are associated with minefields being laid further from populated areas and with fewer civilian mine accidents. The findings contribute to research on civilian victimization, rebel governance, and restraint in wartime violence, with implications for mine clearance and civilian protection.

Guilty by association: patrons, purges, and promotions under personalism

Yumin Sheng

Full text
Mounting scholarship grapples with the causes and consequences of power personalization in autocracies. Prominent research suggests that as the personalist dictator amasses power, he tends to repress potential rivals from his inner circle to stave off mortal threats to his rule. Because military supporters of fallen elites are considered especially menacing to the dictator, they should be more susceptible to a similar fate than other officers, but little empirical work systematically studies authoritarian purges of secondary targets in the military. Drawing on monthly data from 1955–1976 on the leaders of the regional commands of the People's Liberation Army, I find China's regional military leaders hailing from patronage networks of disgraced top civilian and military leaders at the center were more likely to be purged or otherwise experienced more adverse career outcomes than their counterparts without such tainted associations amidst the consolidation and culmination of Mao's personalist rule.

From aggrieved to activist: group grievances, personal resources, and participation repertoires

Solveig Hillesund, Karin Dyrstad

Full text
From voting to violence, politicized ethnicities shape many aspects of politics. Yet, within-group variation in how individuals respond to ethnic inequalities remains understudied. This article examines how perceived group grievance and personal resources combine to shape political participation repertoires. We argue that perceived group grievance can facilitate both conventional and contentious participation, but that individuals' repertoires are conditioned by socioeconomic status: Only the poorest and least educated should be willing to join contentious activities when protests entail risk of repression. Using 2016 survey data from Nepal collected during a period of high contention, we show that while middle class group members respond to perceived group grievance primarily with conventional political activities, such as party work and contacting politicians, individuals from the lowest socioeconomic strata are more likely to respond by supplementing or replacing conventional participation with protests and strikes. Additional analyses suggest that variation in civic skills, measured as literacy, basic political knowledge, and proficiency in the official language, provides the most likely explanation for these differences. The combination of within- and between-group inequalities is commonly seen to increase the risk of recruitment to armed groups. Our findings suggest that they can also serve as a catalyst for nonviolent contentious action. Moreover, the socioeconomic composition of identity groups could help condition the mode of their political struggle.

Can they agree on what to disagree about? Patterns of issue agenda setting in the 2020 US presidential election

Mathias W. Tromborg, Carsten Jensen

Full text
American voters, politicians, and media are increasingly hostile toward out-partisans and frequently disagree about the content of policies. This development raises an even more fundamental question about the nature of politics in highly polarized societies: Can the political actors even agree on what issues to disagree about, and why? To answer this critical question, we generate a massive new dataset covering communication by key actors from the last 2 months of the 2020 US Presidential election, including the presidential candidates (all tweets by Donald Trump and Joe Biden), the main news organizations (CNN and Fox News), and a large and original voter survey (N ~ 24,000) with daily representative data. Surprisingly, we find that the patterns of agenda setting in the 2020 election reduced rather than fueled agenda diversity and that the major political actors generally agreed on what the most important issues were in this otherwise polarized election.

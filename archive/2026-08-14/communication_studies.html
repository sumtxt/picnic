Social Media + Society

Make Campaigns Dance Again: The Trumpdance and the Memeaesthetic Logic of TikTok

Cristopher Cepernich, Alice Fubini, Antonio Martella

Full text
TikTok’s mimesis-oriented affordances invite users to ‘perform’ politics through embodied, replicable templates. This article examines the Trumpdance – Donald Trump’s recurrent YMCA dance at campaign rallies – as a case of how offline events become a platform-native trend. Using the TikTok Research API, we collected 6799 Trumpdance-related videos posted during the final month of the 2024 U.S. presidential campaign. We extracted a stratified sample of the top 20% most-viewed videos per day and content-analyzed 1130 posts. Through Multiple Correspondence Analysis and hierarchical clustering, we identified six participation modes and modeled views and comments with negative binomial regression. Findings show that TikTok visibility concentrates on event-based remediation: clips of Trump dancing at rallies generate the highest expected views and comments, while supporter reenactments and playful appropriations circulate more narrowly. Discursive reframing and satirical remediation attract comparatively fewer views but can elicit intensive commenting, underscoring a split between algorithmic visibility and conversational engagement. We argue that ‘imitation publics’ support meaning-making and production but do not automatically translate into algorithmic visibility. Overall, the Trumpdance exemplifies what we label memeaesthetic: a communicative logic in which political performance becomes a shared aesthetic template for production and participation on TikTok.

Negotiating the Influencer Imaginary: How Everyday Professionals Construct Legitimacy on Social Media

Luciana Valerio-Alfaro, Ignacio Siles

Full text
This article examines how professionals in diverse fields create content on social media as part of their efforts to establish professional legitimacy. Existing scholarship has primarily focused on influencers and creators whose work relies financially on digital content production. Instead, we analyze the experiences of what we call everyday professionals: individuals who pursue full-time occupations while selectively adopting elements of the influencer “toolbox” to complement their practices. Drawing on 20 interviews conducted in Costa Rica, we show that performing legitimacy in the contemporary media environment requires professionals to increasingly grapple with the “influencer imaginary,” a framework that organizes ideas of subjectivity around notions of authenticity and commerciality. Our findings reveal that everyday professionals negotiate this imaginary in three interrelated ways: by engaging with the influencer identity itself, by reflecting on the motivations behind creating content, and by confronting the increased labor required for content production. This study highlights the normative weight of influencer culture across professional domains by extending the influencer imaginary beyond issues of subjectivity to encompass motivations and labor. We conclude by discussing the implications of these findings for understanding current transformations of professional legitimacy.

New Media & Society

Algorithmic imaginaries: Definition and critical questions

Andreas Schellewald, Ellenrose Firth, Hui Lin

Full text
Algorithmic systems play an increasingly central role across a variety of social domains. Over the last few years, various concepts have been developed in the interdisciplinary field of algorithm studies to understand how people make sense of these systems and interact with them – including mental models of algorithms, folk theories of algorithms, or algorithmic imaginaries. This theoretical article engages with the concept of the algorithmic imaginary in particular. It argues that the concept of the algorithmic imaginary occupies a unique position in literature, foregrounding questions of affect and emotion in human-algorithm interaction, and theorising the dynamics of sense-making about algorithms in such terms. Critically examining work that has explicitly applied and developed the concept, we synthesise the scholarship, offer an updated definition, and pose critical questions for future research, including on emergent artificial intelligence phenomena.

Posting under pressure: Politicians active on social media are more stressed

Majbritt K. Severin-Nielsen

Full text
While social media has become an indispensable tool for politicians’ communication with citizens and other stakeholders, emerging qualitative evidence suggests that its use may be associated with diminished well-being and reduced performance within the parliamentary arena – potentially undermining democratic processes. Combining insights from a representative survey study of politicians in Denmark ( n = 1233) with data on their observed social media usage (Facebook: 24,625 posts; Instagram: 13,019 posts), this study is the first to test the generalizability of this emerging evidence and advance our theoretical understanding of the conditions under which politicians’ social media usage is connected to stress. The study demonstrates a significant, positive correlation between politicians’ social media usage and self-reported stress symptoms and points to excessive usage as the primary explanation behind the association. Furthermore, exposure to online harassment is a crucial mediating factor in the association.

Rendering the whole world blank: Precarity, totalising fear and digital scrolling

Alex Paterson

Full text
This article explores the connection between structurally induced precarity and the material practice of digital scrolling in the global north. It argues in favour of an expanded definition of precarity and that precarity, as a generalised affective condition, results in a state of totalising fear (fear which relates, at once, to discrete objects as well as the world itself). In that context, this article advances the claim that scrolling can be understood as an affective attachment (of the kind outlined by Lauren Berlant), developed in response to totalising fear, and which enables the subject to suspend time and to escape from conscious experience of precarity. In establishing this conclusion, the article also explores how the form of scrolling engenders an absorbing, passive and essentially ‘blank’ experience of the world, and how the attainment of this state is an expression of a limited, precarious mode of agency.

Care, competence, or control? The evolution of parental supervision tools on social media platforms: A cross-platform comparison

Amanda Cipriani, Tama Leaver, Michele Willson

Full text
Interaction with social media constitutes a significant part of children’s online experiences, with the potential for both positive and negative experiences. Social media platforms provide parental “supervision tools” as a mechanism for balancing children’s autonomy with safeguards, promoting this function to parents to ensure safer digital practices for their children. This research adopts a constructivist framework to investigate the evolution and operationalisation of parental supervision across Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok. Utilising qualitative methodology, this study combines archival data from an ethnographic walkthrough conducted in 2024, simulating a child’s and parent’s account for each platform. The cross-platform comparison reveals how platforms construct parental responsibility through their discursive and interface supervision mechanisms, expressed through macro-narratives, meso-contexts, and micro-practices. In doing so, platforms shift responsibility for regulation to parents as the accountable supervisory actors, while ultimately also controlling the supervision practices of these parents through the affordances and constraints offered through supervision tools.

Policy and Internet

Navigating the Battlefield: How Social Media Platforms Respond to Governance Demands From Legitimacy Communities in Geopolitical Conflicts

LeTian Cheng, Diyi Liu, Le Yang

Full text
Social media platforms have become central actors in governing online speech during geopolitical conflicts, navigating complex governance demands from diverse stake‐holding communities including states, civil society, and users. This paper explores these dynamics through an analysis of Meta, X (formerly Twitter), and TikTokduring the recent Russia–Ukraine war and Israel–Palestine conflict. We develop a framework categorizing platform strategies along two dimensions: activeness and alignment, leading to five response types: proactive, accommodation, avoidance, defiance, and disregard. Through analysis of 126 interaction events, we found three platforms’ distinct strategic approaches. Meta adopted the most proactive measures with clear inclinations to the Western states and calculated defiance against others. X's strategy shifted dramatically after Musk's takeover: from accommodation to defiance and disregard, though it made pragmatic accommodations under severe pressure. TikTok, risk‐averse, and geopolitically vulnerable, frequently employs cautious and ambivalent avoidance and disregard strategies. The findings shed light on how corporate strategy, institutional background, and geopolitical positioning shape how platforms construct their legitimacy in content governance.

Annual Review of Resource Economics

The State of Environmental-Economic Statistics: Puzzle Pieces Waiting to Be Assembled

Eli P. Fenichel, Angel Hsu, Silvia Landa, Yating Ru, Mariana Camacho

Full text
Environmental changes and challenges, such as climate change and nature loss, are increasingly relevant to economic decisions. Evidence-based economic decisions require organized data. So, environmental data need to be organized in a way similar to, and connected with, existing economic data and statistics. Current approaches to environmental-economic statistics and data remain fragmented, despite a variety of efforts to unify them. We review environmental-economic concepts to provide a high-level framework for addressing the primary challenge of organizing existing environmental data within the economic statistical system. We connect environmental-economic statistics to a variety of use cases, focusing on uses aside from benefit-cost analysis and sustainability assessment. We then review and assess the state of existing efforts to organize environmental-economic data and statistics. We conclude with a discussion of emerging tools that can enable a next-generation approach to environmental-economic statistics and ideas about how to accelerate the process or organizing and integrating environmental data into economic statistics.

Innovation and the Governance of Outer Space as a Commons

Elliot Choi, Gordon Rausser

Full text
The commercialization of outer space has accelerated faster than any other global commons, yet governance remains tethered to static frameworks designed by twentieth-century global institutions and national governments. This article examines how technological innovation endogenously reshapes commons attributes at a pace that destabilizes existing institutional models. Through comparative analysis of six technological domains (space transportation, in-space manufacturing, bioproduction, agriculture, nuclear propulsion and disposal, and satellite services), we identify sector-specific dynamics of congestion, market concentration, and undersupplied public goods. Our framework demonstrates that governance challenges arise less from fixed resource characteristics than from feedback loops between innovation development and institutional design. The findings advance three contributions: a conceptual model of shifting commons attributes, sectoral diagnoses of emerging coordination failures, and prescriptions for adaptive governance architectures, including modular treaties, polycentric arrangements, and public-private partnerships.

Air Quality in Sub-Saharan Africa: Measurement Issues and Empirical Challenges

Emilia Tjernström

Full text
Air pollution imposes large and uneven health and productivity costs in sub-Saharan Africa, yet evidence remains limited by severe data constraints. This review synthesizes what economists know about particulate matter exposure in the region, with a focus on measurement, sources, and implications for applied research. I review ground-based, satellite, reanalysis, and hybrid data products, emphasizing how spatially structured measurement error shapes empirical identification. I then assess evidence on health, agriculture, labor, and human capital, highlighting both credible designs and remaining gaps. The review concludes by outlining research strategies that integrate exposure uncertainty into applied economic analysis.

Space for people, not cars: a large-scale visual choice experiment on urban street redesigns

Michael Wicki, Claudia Sinatra, Aura Huang, David Kaufmann

Full text
Urban street reallocations and redesigns are central to sustainable mobility transitions, yet public acceptance remains a major challenge. While previous research has examined general attitudes toward transport policy, little is known about how specific design features shape support across diverse user groups. We conducted a large-scale stated choice experiment ( N = 5991) embedded in a nationally representative mobility panel in Switzerland. Respondents evaluated navigable 360° and static visualizations of redesigned fictional urban streets, varying in infrastructure, greenery, and social space. We examine effects on three outcome dimensions: acceptance, willingness to live nearby, and willingness to cycle. Greenery consistently increases support across both formats. How the redesigns are visualized shapes which features become salient: the 360° condition increases sensitivity to social features, while the static condition increases sensitivity to environmental and infrastructural features. Subgroup analyses reveal substantial cleavages by gender, age, transport behavior, and political ideology. The results show that design-sensitive, visually supported planning strategies can build broader public coalitions for urban street transformations. Our findings inform participatory planning and policy design for more inclusive and context-sensitive urban transitions.

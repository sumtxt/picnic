International Security at Fifty Years

Stephen M. Walt

Full text
Fifty years ago, the founders of this journal hoped that its creation would help revitalize the academic study of international security. Their ambitions have been fulfilled: International Security is now one of the most widely cited journals in the field of international relations and there is a robust community of scholars studying different aspects of the subject. The contents of the journal constantly evolve in response to world events, new scholarly developments, and its contributors’ particular interests, but it remains a vital resource for scholars and students alike. By facilitating active debates on important scholarly questions, International Security and other academic journals help educate each new cohort and contribute to robust discussions of critical policy questions.

The Return of Nationalist Geopolitics: Liberal Backsliding and Armed Conflict

Lars-Erik Cederman, Paola Galano Toro, Luc Girardin, Andreas Juon, Julian Wucherpfennig

Full text
The post-Cold War decline in violent conflict has given way to rising instability both within and between states. While many attribute this shift to intensified great power competition, we argue that nationalist geopolitics, manifested through conflict driven by grievances reflecting the political exclusion of ethnic groups and their kin, offer a more accurate explanation. We explain how endogenous and exogenous factors weakened the liberal international order, which allowed ethnic nationalism to resurge in the early twenty-first century. This erosion of broader liberal norms, particularly what we call liberal backsliding, has started to undermine the inclusive policies that ultimately helped to stabilize the post-Cold War peace. Using newly updated global data on ethnic power relations (1946–2023), we document a reversal of inclusionary trends that began in the early 2010s. These findings challenge power-centric explanations of global instability and highlight how liberal institutional erosion, particularly in the domain of ethnic inclusion, creates fertile ground for identity-based violence. The return of nationalist geopolitics is thus rooted in not only shifting power relations but also retreating institutional safeguards.

The Iron Cage of International Anarchy

John J. Mearsheimer

Full text
Great power competition has been endemic to the modern system of industrialized nation-states that began emerging in Europe around 1800. This article seeks to describe and explain the principal features of that competition over the past 226 years, paying special attention to how it has evolved during the fifty-year history of International Security (1976–2026). The focus is on both security competition, which is a constant feature of great power politics, and war, which is not. The article shows that although security competition is pervasive, the shape of that competition has varied markedly over time, depending largely on the polarity of the system—the number of great powers—and the distribution of power among them. Those same two factors, coupled with the coming of nuclear weapons, have had a pronounced effect on the likelihood of great power war, including conflicts that involved all the great powers, such as the Napoleonic Wars, World War I, and World War II. Finally, the article considers the likely contours of great power competition in the next fifty years, focusing on determining which countries are likely to be great powers, what is the likely distribution of power among them, and what are the prospects for a future great power war.

Seeing Through “Transparent Oceans”: Emerging Technology, Submarine Vulnerability, and the Future of MAD

Eli Sanchez

Full text
Ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) are widely believed to be invulnerable to disarming strikes because of their weak detectable signatures and the large areas within which they can threaten an adversary. SSBNs are therefore considered secure second-strike forces and thus a strong deterrent against nuclear war and nuclear coercion. Many analysts now believe that various emerging technologies could render SSBNs vulnerable to disarming strikes. This study surveys key sensing and signal processing technologies featured in the “transparent oceans” debate (i.e., quantum sensors, artificial intelligence [AI], space-based sensors, and advanced acoustic sensors) and analyzes their potential to enhance SSBN detection. Findings indicate that states can enhance SSBN detection by applying AI algorithms to passive sonar signal processing. But the capabilities that have been demonstrated by the relevant AI algorithms in the unclassified literature suggest that these advances in SSBN detection are evolutionary rather than revolutionary. This study finds that the other technologies do not meaningfully improve SSBN detection; it is therefore highly unlikely that a threat to SSBNs that are presently survivable will materialize in the coming decades.

Journal of Peace Research

Landmark UN Security Council resolutions, agenda setting, and warring parties’ peacebuilding commitments

Cameron Mailhot

Full text
What is the relationship between United Nations Security Council (UNSC) resolutions and warring parties' peacebuilding commitments? The UNSC has adopted various landmark resolutions with the goal of codifying what it deems necessary for promoting lasting peace and security. However, little is known about whether there have been systemic shifts in the subsequential peacebuilding commitments that warring parties make as they transition out of armed conflict. I address this gap in two parts. First, I develop a theoretical framework to explain the agenda-setting role that landmark resolutions play in peace processes; in doing so, I explicate a mechanism through which agenda setting operates: the mandated activities of the United Nations’ (UN's) field missions. Second, I test this theory by leveraging insights from the PA-X Peace Agreement Database and the original Mandates of International Organizations' Missions dataset. In line with my framework, I find that warring parties incorporate different landmark resolutions' thematic issues into their peacebuilding commitments at higher rates following the resolutions' adoption as compared to time periods before their adoption, and UN missions' mandated promotion of the resolutions' thematic issues is consistently associated with higher rates of incorporation. Alternative models and placebo tests examining the mere presence of UN missions and the mandated activities of regional organizations' missions further substantiate these findings.

Representative-capacity-making: The strategic backstage work of political representation

Carolyn M. Hendriks, Michael Saward

Full text
The work of being an elected representative today is increasingly demanding as they seek to perform representation amid political distrust, polarised electorates, and rising populism. In response, elected representatives and their teams invest heavily in developing and showcasing their capacities to represent. This variously involves constructing representative personas, designing participatory events, curating diverse digital content, and communicating evidence on how and where they are representing. Extending Saward’s representative claim-making framework, this article develops the concept of representative-capacity-making to better understand this overlooked (‘backstage’) aspect of modern representation. The article explores – in concept only – the work involved in developing and demonstrating four key representative capacities. The normative implications of representative-capacity-making in an era of democratic strain are considered. While representative-capacity-making can be read as a response to the challenges of democratic representation today, the extent to which it can be a solution is mixed, in specified ways.

Government identifiability and the number of parties: No sweet spots, just a steady linear increase

Chris Hanretty

Full text
Government identifiability – the degree to which the party composition of the eventual government can be predicted given the election results – is a desirable characteristic of a political system. But the measurement of identifiability has remained impressionistic, limiting our ability to speak precisely about trade-offs between identifiability and other characteristics of the political system. I show that a recent measure of bargaining complexity by Cronert and Nyman acts as a ratio-level measure of government identifiability measured in bits – or equivalently, the number of yes/no questions needed to identify the composition of the government. I then show how identifiability is linearly related to the number of seat-winning parties in three different contexts: parliamentary governments, presidential cabinets, and municipal governments. Across all three contexts, an additional seat-winning party increases entropy of government formation by between 0.7 and 0.8 bits. Since identifiability is linearly related to the number of parties, any increase in the number of parties through electoral reform will worsen identifiability by the same amount. Conversely, there are no “sweet spots” where we can achieve high levels of identifiability at low cost in terms of the number of parties.

Two competing giants: An experimental test of spatial and valence models of voting

Dieter Stiers, Ann-Kristin Kölln

Full text
Rational choice theories offer one of the major explanations for citizens’ voting behavior. The most prominent models within the rational choice framework – spatial and valence voting – share the same roots but differ in central aspects, emphasizing ideological congruence and competence evaluations, respectively. In this note, we provide a direct test of the models’ relative strength. Does a party’s issue congruence with a voter (spatial) or its effectiveness in handling important issues (valence) have a larger effect on vote choice? Our results from lab-like conditions using a novel conjoint experiment show strong evidence for both models, but valence evaluations consistently trump spatial considerations. These results have important implications for research on electoral behavior, party politics, and democratic representation.

Rectifying injustices against humanity: communitarianism, Ubuntu, and restoration

Mohamed Sesay

Full text
How should we assess and judge the harm inflicted by egregious wrongdoing and how should we respond to such wrongdoings after they have occurred? These are the questions this contribution tackles from an African communitarian Ubuntu worldview that centres the humanity of everyone impacted in both its understanding of wrongdoing and remedy. It argues that restoration of shared humanity is regarded as the primary and ultimate function of justice in the African systems of political thought.

Pan-Africanism or Black internationalism? Evoking African political thought as political activism

W. R. Nadège Compaoré

Full text
The article examines the ideas of Pan-Africanism and Black internationalism as African political thought and core concepts in African International Relations. They acquire significant meaning through their framing as political activism or as theoretical frameworks aimed at advancing social and political change. Pan-Africanism has been understood as an idea and a movement for African peoples’ self-determination and solidarity worldwide and has been linked to the concept of internationalism, specifically Black internationalism. Both concepts call for solidarity between Africa and its diasporas worldwide, and they both evoke the ideals of self-determination and Black liberation. The article argues that the two concepts are deployed not as problem-solving theories but rather as intellectual activism. They are central pillars of African intellectual exercises that seek to change or transform the international system to reflect the experiences of Africans and Black people, who have historically been marginalized in both scholarship and practices of international politics.

International Relations in Africa: not without Indigenous knowledge systems

Evelyn Namakula-Mayanja, Afua Boatemaa Yakohene

Full text
The field of International Relations (IR) has come under increasing and sustained criticism for producing and disseminating a particular reading of European history, one rooted in violence, as if it were universal knowledge. Generations of students, academics, practitioners, and policymakers have been taught and socialized into accepting this violent historical experience as the norm and, implicitly, as a reflection of human nature. As a result, the dominant accounts of IR tend to foreground competitive, conflictual, domineering, power-seeking, and exploitative behaviours as defining features of international life. This understanding of IR marginalizes and often ignores Indigenous ways of knowing that emphasize relationality, solidarity, mutual care, and cooperative forms of social organization. In this article, we argue that centring Afrocentric Indigenous Knowledge Systems (AIKS), particularly their conceptualizations of persons in IR scholarship, has the potential to move the field beyond its bleak and violent roots towards a more humane and relational epistemology. The AIKS-centred approach to IR is better suited to capturing the nuances of contemporary global life while speaking to the lived experiences of Africans and potentially other Indigenous peoples around the world.

Strategic desecuritization under asymmetry: from security opportunity to security trap

Kai He, Huiyun Feng

Full text
Why do actors that use security framing to elevate an urgent issue later soften, narrow, or recalibrate that framing, even when the threat remains acute? This article develops a theory of strategic desecuritization under asymmetry. We argue that securitizing moves can generate both security opportunities and security traps. They create opportunities by raising salience, securing recognition, and attracting resources. Yet under power asymmetry and geopolitical competition, they may also draw in stronger external actors whose security logics reshape governance and erode initiators’ agenda control. Security opportunities can thereby become security traps. To mitigate these traps, weaker actors may pursue strategic desecuritization: a discursive and institutional strategy that preserves urgency while narrowing security framing, limiting external strategic intrusion, and reasserting authority over issue governance. The article illustrates this mechanism through Pacific climate diplomacy, tracing the trajectory from the 2018 Boe Declaration to the 2025 Blue Pacific Ocean of Peace Declaration and the Pacific Resilience Facility. The argument contributes to securitization theory by distinguishing securitizing moves, full securitization, and security effects; reconceptualizing desecuritization as a strategic response to security framing’s autonomy-eroding consequences; and showing how institutional substitution enables weaker actors to reassert agency under geopolitical competition.

African systems of thought and IR: the road to epistemic justice

Thomas Kwasi Tieku, J. Andrew Grant, Karolina Werner

Full text
International Relations (IR) has come under increasing and sustained criticism for marginalizing Africa’s intellectual traditions, rich history, Indigenous knowledge systems, and lived experiences. Several scholars of IR have argued that meaningful engagement with African contexts and thought would lead to better theory and pedagogy, more robust concepts, sharper analysis, and the study of issues that have greater relevance to contemporary global life. Despite the seriousness and widespread nature of the criticism, it is rare to find manuscripts in major IR journals that move beyond critique to demonstrate concretely how centring Africa in IR scholarship could transform the discipline or substantially enhance its analytical tools. This symposium takes up that difficult task by showing how a focus on African systems of thought can enrich IR’s conceptual vocabulary, reorient core assumptions, and expand the range of questions the discipline is equipped to address.

African feminisms in International Relations

Peace A. Medie

Full text
Scholars of Africa’s International Relations (IR) have criticized how the continent is studied in IR and its positioning on the margins of the field. Though a significant portion of the IR literature on Africa focuses on gender, and women, African feminist thought has not been centred in this literature. In this paper, I examine how African feminisms can contribute to IR. I show that by drawing on African feminist thought, IR can address weaknesses in how Africa is studied in the field. Specifically, I discuss how African feminists’ understanding of the complexity of context, and attention to history and intersectionality, provide direction on how to study African states and societies and their interaction with the international.

IR Resource Bank: a tool for diversifying and decolonizing the curriculum

Fikir Haile, Badriyya Yusuf

Full text
Spurred on in part by the large social justice mobilizations that began in 2020, recent years have seen growing calls for diversifying and decolonizing the disciplinary practices in the field of International Relations (IR). In response to these calls within the field and across academia more broadly, faculty and students in the Department of Political Studies at Queen’s University embarked on a project to produce a ‘Resource Bank’ highlighting and centring alternatives to Euro-American epistemologies, pedagogies, and ontologies that have thus far dominated the IR field. Drawing on our experiences as project managers of this Resource Bank project since its inception in 2021, we discuss some of the most salient issues and important challenges that emerged in the course of this work. We employ a postcolonial theoretical framework to tease out and problematize the Eurocentric foundations of the IR field and utilize the Resource Bank to reimagine research and teaching in the IR field.

Contrapuntal reading of ‘Africa rising’: seeing Africa in International Relations beyond the exceptional

Lina Benabdallah

Full text
How can we read International Relations from the perspective of Africa in a way that does not perpetually put the continent in a permanent state of exception vis-à-vis global politics? What strategies are available to study Africa without overhyping narratives of its rise or demise? This article contends, drawing on Edward Said, that adopting contrapuntal reading enables reading Africa’s place in international politics beyond narratives and counter-narratives of Africa moving from one exceptional state of despair to an exceptional state of prosperity with nothing in between. The article uses the ‘Africa Rising’ narrative and its counter-narrative of Africa as a place of perpetual lack, deviance, and demise to illustrate how to practically adopt contrapuntalism as a method and as an ethos when engaging African perspectives in IR. It argues that contrapuntalism is a commitment to practicing critical thinking and questioning Eurocentric modes of thinking when approaching mainstream accounts of the history, origin, or interpretations of any political event. Contrapuntalism acknowledges that dominant narratives need to be read side-by-side with narratives of resistance and that non-Western concepts do not always need to be translated or rendered legible by creating (forced) Eurocentric equivalents for them to be legitimate or valid.

British Journal of Politics & International Relations

The long shadow of the past: How Irish history shapes attitudes towards the Israel–Palestine conflict

Miceal Canavan, Liam Kneafsey

Full text
Why do some publics engage deeply with distant conflicts absent direct ethnic or strategic ties? Existing explanations emphasise geopolitical ties, demographics, or elite cues, but these cannot account for the Republic of Ireland’s consistent pro-Palestinian stance, exceptional in Western Europe and central in shaping Irish state behaviour, international coalitions, and bilateral relations. We argue that historical narratives – particularly those rooted in anticolonial struggle – provide cognitive and affective mechanisms that shape public attitudes to foreign conflict. Using novel survey measures and open questions on a representative sample of Irish respondents (1547), we find that sympathy for Palestinians is broad-based, deeply held, and closely tied to perceptions of Irish historical oppression and unity. These results hold after accounting for ideology and party cues. Given its prominence in public debate, we also test antisemitism as an explanation. We find low levels of antisemitism and higher levels of Islamophobia, indicating that general pro-Palestinian sympathy is driven neither by prejudice toward Jews nor particular affinity toward Muslims. Open-ended responses show Irish respondents spontaneously draw analogies between Ireland’s colonial past and the Palestinian experience. Our findings highlight the long shadow of national history in shaping contemporary foreign policy attitudes.

Representations of nuclear risk in the UK Parliament: Understanding the role of political discourse in the development of preparedness activity for nuclear accidents

Lianna Roast, Christopher Baker-Beall

Full text
This article argues that despite a prevailing national preparedness imperative, significant vulnerabilities persist in the UK’s emergency arrangements for a nuclear accident. While such an imperative may drive legislation and the legitimation of emergency planning, its integrity can be compromised by other institutional and discursive factors. We propose that social representations of nuclear risk in UK parliamentary debates play a key role in shaping the conditions for anticipatory action. Through a discourse analysis of debates following three major nuclear accidents, Windscale (1957), Chernobyl (1986) and Fukushima (2011), we identify the emergence of a dominant nuclear energy metanarrative, which frames nuclear power as safe, necessary and nationally beneficial. We argue this metanarrative has contributed to the marginalisation of central government preparedness by shifting responsibility to local authorities and regulators and fostering potential overconfidence in structural mitigations, thereby narrowing the conditions under which nationally coordinated anticipatory action becomes possible.

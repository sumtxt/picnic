American Journal of Political Science

Durable majority gerrymanders: Where partisan gerrymandering can displace democracy

Maxwell Palmer, Benjamin Schneer

Full text
We develop the concept of, and estimation tools for, durable majority gerrymanders : electoral district maps drawn to reduce an opposing political party's probability of winning a majority in a legislative chamber. Directly interpretable, forward‐looking, and motivated by the democratic principle that a party in power should have some chance of losing it, this measure provides new insights into the role of redistricting in state legislative elections. We show that, when map drawers are unconstrained, the ability to create durable majorities is so widespread that at least one party in every state can draw a map where a majority of legislative districts withstand almost any likely future electoral swing. Enacted maps are less durable due to a combination of underlying geography, voter partisanship, and state‐level guidelines. This paper provides the theoretical framework and empirical tools to understand which gerrymandered maps enable state‐level majorities to emerge and to endure.

Endogenous coalition formation in policy debates

Philip Leifeld, Laurence Brandenberger

Full text
Political actors form coalitions around their joint normative beliefs in order to influence the policy process on contentious issues such as climate change or population aging. Policy process theory maintains that learning within and across coalitions is a central predictor of policy change but has yet to explain how policy learning works. This article explains the formation and maintenance of coalitions by focusing on the ways actors adopt policy beliefs from other actors in policy debates. A policy debate is a complex social system in which temporal network dependence guides how actors contribute ideological statements. Belief adoption matters in three complementary ways: bonding , which exploits cues within coalitions; bridging , which explores new beliefs outside one's perimeter; and repulsion , which reinforces polarization between coalitions and cements their belief systems. We formalize this theory of endogenous coalition formation in policy debates and test it on a micro‐level empirical data set.

(Non)belonging and (un)freedom

Yuna Blajer de la Garza

Full text
This article centers on the concept of “belonging” to argue that belonging and freedom are fundamentally related, and that this relationship cannot be easily captured in formal terms alone. I contend that experiences of incomplete belonging among citizens deserve attention from political science because, in addition to their more obvious challenge to equality, they also erode freedom, even in the context of robust formal guarantees. Citizens perceived as not fully belonging are more likely to eschew behaviors or opinions they know can exacerbate the likelihood of being perceived as interlopers. But although this challenge to individual freedom may be by itself worrisome, I focus on the ways in which some citizens’ unequal belonging undermines collective freedom—what Hannah Arendt calls political freedom—by confining some people's sincere expressions to narrow public spaces, limiting pluralism, and depleting the public space of opportunities for deliberation and political action.

Global harms, local profits: How the uneven costs of natural disasters affect support for green political platforms

Silvia Pianta, Paula Rettl

Full text
Large‐scale fires are becoming increasingly common due to climate change. While conventional wisdom suggests that firsthand experiences with natural disasters foster green coalitions by raising awareness of environmental degradation, we propose an alternative view: Natural disasters can heighten the perceived trade‐off between economic development and environmental policies. This study examines the case of Brazil, where large‐scale fires, by clearing natural vegetation, create new opportunities for land grabbing and the expansion of agriculture and livestock production. Using satellite, administrative, and electoral data, we provide evidence that large‐scale fires increase support for green candidates only in municipalities that are unlikely to reap economic benefits from fires. Survey data further indicate that voters prioritize environmental protection over economic growth only when they stand to receive limited or no benefit from the newly cleared land. Our findings suggest that the potential for natural disasters to foster green coalitions is contingent upon distributive concerns.

Perspectives on Politics

Colorism, Descriptive Representation, and the Representation of African Americans in the United States House of Representatives

Danielle Casarez Lemi, Jennifer R. Garcia, Christopher T. Stout

Full text
Previous research overlooks how certain ascribed characteristics, such as skin tone, correlate with the representation of marginalized groups. Yet research also demonstrates how colorism shapes candidate evaluations and life experiences. We theorize that colorism, the systemic privileging of lighter skin, structures the motivations and electoral incentives of Black lawmakers. We hypothesize that darker-skinned Black members of Congress will better represent Black Americans on racialized issues than lighter-skinned Black members, depending on the form of representation: rhetorical or legislative. We test this hypothesis with a combination of human and computer-assisted content analyses of 204,806 press releases and 18,025 bill introductions from the 114th, 115th, and 116th Congresses and a crowdsourced measure of skin tones. We find that on average, darker-skinned Black members of Congress provide more representation in press releases, but not in legislation. Our findings highlight the need to examine diversity among descriptive representatives.

The War on Universities: Illiberal Tactics in Turkey, Hungary, and the United States

Sefa Secen, Ramazan Kılınç

Full text
Illiberal governments increasingly target universities as sites of political dissent and ideological opposition. This paper compares three illustrative cases—Boğaziçi University under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, Central European University under Viktor Orbán, and elite US universities under Donald Trump—to examine how illiberal governments deploy state power against autonomous, high-profile, liberal-aligned institutions of higher education, and how students and faculty respond. Although universities are not always bastions of pluralism, tolerance, and democracy, attacks on universities are often concurrent with illiberal governance. We argue that illiberal governments perceive universities as vanguards of hegemonic liberalism and as partisan opponents in certain contexts, and that state repression operates along a continuum of coercion, legal displacement, and discursive delegitimation. These attacks on academic autonomy trigger varied responses—from mobilization, to fragmentation, to withdrawal—shaped by domestic political opportunity structures, the intensity of state repression, and campus culture. By conceptualizing the university as a contested political institution rather than a neutral educational space, this article contributes to broader debates on democratic backsliding, populism, culture wars, and higher education.

Reconciliation, Decolonization of Relationships, and Egalitarian Bi-nationalism: A Praxis for the Israeli–Palestinian Impasse?

Basel Mansour

Full text
In this article, I interrogate the theoretical architecture that underpins three increasingly influential normative prescriptions for a just political configuration in Israel–Palestine: reconciliation, decolonization of relationships, and egalitarian bi-nationalism (RDB-N). Although recent scholarship has attempted to integrate them into a shared framework, they remain insufficiently articulated, delaying the emergence of a transformative praxis. Here, I argue that the core impasse lies in the dominant “top-down” conception of reconciliation, which, by presuming that a postconflict political order is a prerequisite for implementation, produces a dependency that obstructs the normative potential of RDB-N as a coherent framework for structural transformation. In response, I advance a “bottom-up” conception of reconciliation that is both theoretically plausible and normatively desirable. This reimagines reconciliation as an implementation-driven and grassroots dynamic capable of initiating decolonial transformation and prefiguring an egalitarian binational future, rather than a postconflict process led by the state and its institutions. In proposing this theoretical rearticulation, I set the groundwork for a new praxis of RDB-N grounded in localized political agency. The analysis culminates in the Palestinian Community of Israel (PCI), whose liminal position and situated political condition potentially make it a particularly fertile site for developing such a praxis. Although largely theoretical, this contribution has practical implications for conflict resolution, settler-colonial critique, and political transformation in asymmetric contexts. It also opens avenues for future empirical and normative research into both justice in non-ideal conditions and collective action.

I Read the News Today, but Why? Media Consumption, Socialization, and Autocracy

Samuel A. Greene

Full text
Dominant models of news consumption in political science treat citizens as rational individuals, gathering information to make materially or politically consequential choices. This assumption, developed largely in democratic contexts, risks obscuring other incentives that may be equally or more influential—particularly in authoritarian regimes, where the informational value of news is often constrained. This article advances the concept of social rationality: the incentive to consume news to sustain conversations, reinforce social bonds, and participate in communities of shared meaning. Drawing on original survey data from Belarus and Russia—two highly authoritarian states with divergent approaches to media control—the analysis shows that social motivations are powerful predictors of both the intensity and breadth of media consumption, while decision-rational considerations also help to explain breadth. Divergent interactions between social motivations and intrinsic prosocial orientations in the two cases further complicate theories of “informational autocracy.” These findings challenge classical models and underscore the need to place social incentives at the center of explanations of media use.

Grounding Hope in Trust: The Case of Climate Activism

Ilaria Cozzaglio, Lukas Sparenborg

Full text
According to the standard definition of hope, for one to be hopeful, the desired outcome must be considered at least possible. The literature on climate activism has incorporated this notion of hope, but we argue that this notion alone cannot fully account for hopeful attitudes in situations where collective action is demanded. For hope to be justified in the case of climate activism, it must be supported by some form of trust. We then outline different forms of trust that can be found in activist practices to ground hopeful attitudes, including procedural and moral trust in institutions and in fellow citizens or activists. We finally argue that mistrust represents a democratic equilibrium between the activists’ duty to exercise vigilance and critique toward institutions and the need for institutions to be perceived as trustworthy by activists and citizens more broadly.

The Victory Trap: Endogenous Opposition Coalition Weaknesses in Reversing Autocracy

Elvin Ong, Frances Cayton

Full text
After opposition parties and alliances defeat dominant autocratic incumbents, what challenges do they encounter in reversing autocracy and what are the consequences? We argue that long-neglected endogenous weaknesses in opposition coalitions undermine re-democratization efforts, easily opening the door to autocratic comebacks. First, coalition intra-elite conflicts post-victory result in fractured and ineffective governance, generating mass disappointment with the new political elites. Second, coalition intra-supporter divergence pressures the new coalition government into implementing pro-democratic reforms at haphazard speeds and fluctuating scopes. Swift actions in reversing autocracy alienate supporters of the ex-autocrat, elicit accusations of partisan revenge, and fuel counter-mobilization against the new government. A most-different case comparison between the recent autocratic reversal experiences of Poland and Malaysia demonstrates our argument. We show in both cases that ideological and distributional conflicts among diverse coalition elites undermined governance after victory, and different supporter bases prioritized different policy agendas. Counter-mobilization by the autocratic ex-incumbents was highly successful in undermining public support for the new government. These findings suggest that profound challenges in reversing autocracy and rebuilding democracy lie within opposition coalitions themselves, in addition to authoritarian legacies.

José Carlos Mariátegui’s Archaic Developmentalism: Utopia, the Incan Empire, and Peruvian Reality

Gabriel Vergara

Full text
“Developmentalism” refers to the idea that human societies progress through a series of historical stages, along one path or multiple paths, which allows for the comparison and hierarchization of different populations. As numerous works have shown, developmentalism was often called upon to justify Europe’s domination of non-European populations, which were figured as backward or barbaric. To complicate the commonplace equation of developmentalism and empire, I analyze José Carlos Mariátegui’s political thought as a stratigraphic history in which different temporalities—especially archaic temporality, or the remnants of Incan communalism, and developmental temporality—were vertically stacked and in tension, and I elaborate a conception of what I call “archaic developmentalism.” For Mariátegui, while the past temporality, institutions, and practices of the Incan Empire persisted into the present, he did not perceive them as hurdles to be cleared on the upward march of progress. Rather, as I argue, Mariátegui recognized the political potency of Incan communalism’s remnants—particularly, the ayllu , or Incan communal form—and he conjectured that the collision of temporal-historical layers could open an alternative developmental trajectory by which the ayllu would be reactivated as a stage in the process of creating modern communism, skipping over the developmental stage of mature capitalism that was required by Soviet orthodoxy. In other words, Mariátegui thought that the friction generated by the convergence of Peru’s different telluric strata opened a new developmental trajectory in which remnants of Incan archaism could spark socialist struggle and be reconfigured as an arsenal of possibilities in the present.

European Journal of Political Research

Does citizen deliberation inside representative institutions enhance legitimacy among non-participating publics?

Adele Webb, Max Grömping, Marlene Mauk

Full text
Claims that citizen deliberation can strengthen public confidence in democracy rest on the assumption that it enhances the perceived procedural legitimacy of democratic governance. We test this assumption by examining whether integrating deliberative constituent forums into electoral-representative institutions increases perceived legitimacy among non-participating publics and which specific features of such forums drive these diffuse effects. We extend existing experimental research on democratic innovations by theorising that the deliberative norms of consequentiality and listening shape legitimacy judgements. Drawing on a nationally representative survey vignette experiment in Australia, we show that even minimal, realistic exposure to information about a deliberative citizens’ panel informing a parliamentary committee produces a modest but statistically significant increase in perceived procedural legitimacy. These gains depend on signals that citizens’ input is taken seriously and that participants are sincerely listened to. While legitimacy declines when deliberative input is ignored, the strongest positive effects arise when processes visibly communicate high-quality listening by political representatives. Moreover, legitimacy gains are strongest among respondents with low institutional trust. The findings demonstrate that the legitimacy-enhancing potential of deliberative innovations hinges not only on institutional design but also on the perceived relational quality of engagement between constituents and decision-makers. This contributes to ongoing debates in democratic theory and political behaviour about when and why deliberative innovations can rejuvenate representative democracy.

The winner–loser gap in democratic attitudes among politicians: Individual and collective outcomes

Erick Stivens Padilla-Galviz, Álvaro Canalejo-Molero

Full text
Rooted in concerns about losers’ consent to electoral outcomes, a vast literature documents a winner–loser gap in democratic attitudes among voters, with winners displaying systematically more positive attitudes than losers across a range of indicators. Surprisingly, we know much less about whether this gap extends to politicians. This paper addresses this question both theoretically and empirically. Theoretically, we distinguish between politicians’ reactions to electoral outcomes at the individual level – whether candidates secure a seat in parliament – and the party level – whether their party enters government, and theorise about their interaction. Empirically, we present comprehensive observational evidence from 27 elections showing that losers systematically report lower satisfaction with democracy, weaker support for representative institutions, and lower perceptions of citizens’ trust. The gap is present at both levels, but the individual-level gap is conditioned by party outcomes: it is most pronounced within winning parties and disappears within losing parties. A complementary analysis leveraging close races and administrative data from Iceland provides plausibility that these associations are, at least in part, causal. Taken together, the findings reveal that democratic attitudes among rank-and-file politicians are, like those of voters, highly malleable and responsive to electoral outcomes. While politicians maintain overall positive attitudes towards democracy, this malleability raises questions about when and how such contingency might translate into anti-democratic behaviour.

PRRP identity and sense of understanding for citizens who engage in political violence

Fernando Feitosa, Bjarn Eck, Jean-Benoit Pilet

Full text
This research note extends existing knowledge on anti-pluralism among identifiers of a populist radical right party (PRRP) by investigating their sense of understanding for citizens who engage in two key forms of political violence: threatening and spreading false information about political opponents. Building on methodological advances in political science research, we estimate a series of Bayesian zero-inflated regression models using original Flemish data ( N = 2,963). Results reveal a positive correlation between PRRP identity and sense of understanding. Results further indicate that this correlation diminishes with higher levels of education in the case of spreading false information, but remains equally strong in the case of threatening. This research note offers new insights into anti-pluralism among PRRP identifiers.

Assessing Nonignorable Response: Sensitivity Analysis for Survey Weighting, with Applications to Survey Estimates of COVID-19 Vaccination Uptake

Melody Huang, Erin Hartman

Full text
Standard statistical uncertainty measures, like standard errors, are unable to quantify the uncertainty from an unrepresentative sampling process. Existing work has highlighted the potential risk in large datasets, in which larger surveys resulting in more precise estimates can misleadingly overstate the degree of confidence in biased survey results. In this research note, we introduce a sensitivity framework for researchers to transparently summarize their uncertainty to a biased sampling process. We illustrate the framework on a set of COVID-19 vaccine surveys, and show that had researchers conducted a sensitivity analysis, they would have found that despite substantially smaller standard errors, the larger surveys conducted on less representative samples were more susceptible to potential bias than smaller surveys conducted on more representative samples. We argue that these summary measures should be routinely reported to accurately quantify uncertainty to potential bias, allowing researchers to assess their confidence in results, in large and small datasets.

Political Science Research and Methods

Measuring racial attitudes pre-treatment in survey experiments

Leah Christiani, Nathan Kelly, Jana Morgan

Full text
Experimental researchers often need to assess the moderating effect of racial attitudes. However, it is unclear whether the racial attitude battery deployed may bias treatment effects. Less subtle batteries could bias treatment effects toward zero if measuring racial attitudes primes these attitudes for subjects assigned to all conditions, including the control condition. We assess whether bias is introduced across four conditions, pre-treatment: not measuring racial attitudes, using stereotype endorsement measures, using the American National Election Study racial resentment battery, or using the simplified Cooperative Election Study racial resentment battery. We do not uncover widespread instances of treatment bias. However, we do identify bias when the stereotype endorsement measures are used pre-treatment. We conclude with recommendations for researchers.

Debriefings in misinformation research should include fact-checks to correct misperceptions and increase perceived learning

Emma Hoes, Sacha Altay

Full text
Debriefings are a cornerstone of ethical research, especially when deception is used. In misinformation experiments, debriefings both disclose the deception and warn participants about misinformation. Unlike other misinformation interventions, they reveal that researchers deliberately introduced the falsehoods, raising concerns about trust and skepticism. In a preregistered U.S. experiment (N = 896), we compare no debriefing, a general debriefing, and a specific debriefing with fact-checks and source links. Only the specific debriefing reduced belief in false claims participants saw, not in novel ones. The general debriefing slightly reduced belief in true news, suggesting warnings may heighten skepticism. Both improved attitudes toward the study and perceived transparency, supporting the use of specific debriefings as an ethical and effective practice in misinformation research.

Right-wing terrorism and far-right support: evidence from anti-Roma attacks in Hungary

Gabor Simonovits, Attila Gaspar, Bekes Gabor, Marton Vegh

Full text
How do ethnically motivated terrorist attacks shape electoral support for the far right? We study a unique case: a coordinated series of anti-Roma murders in Hungary in 2008–2009, the most severe episode of anti-minority violence in the country since World War II. Combining difference-in-differences and synthetic control methods, we compare attacked settlements to multiple counterfactuals, including planned-but-unrealized targets. We find that Jobbik, Hungary’s radical right party, gained 11–14 percentage points more support in attacked villages than in comparable controls in the 2010 election—an increase 53–70% larger than baseline trends. The effect persisted for several years and spilled over to nearby settlements. In contrast to some research from Western Europe suggesting that right-wing terrorism can reduce far-right appeal, our findings highlight how deep-seated prejudice can reverse this pattern. The results underscore the importance of antecedent inter-group relations in conditioning political reactions to ethnic violence.

Political Psychology

Consistency, not waste aversion, explains apparent sunk‐cost effects in politics

Marco Mattei, John Michael

Full text
Do sunk costs shape how voters judge political leaders, or do reputational signals carried by current choices dominate? Across three preregistered experiments (total N = 1462) using vignettes about a national rail project, we orthogonally manipulated sunk costs (high vs. absent), leaders' decisions (continue vs. abandon), campaign commitments (pro, anti, none), and reminders of prior voting behavior. In Experiment 1 ( N = 346), Bayesian ANOVA showed that evaluations (vote likelihood, perceived honesty, competence) were reliably higher when leaders continued rather than abandoned a project, with no main effect of sunk costs and no interaction. Experiment 2 ( N = 719) replicated the null sunk‐cost effect and further found no effect of campaign commitments once the leader's current decision was known. Experiment 3 ( N = 397) used a structural equation model to test mediation: perceived commitment—but not perceived sunk costs—robustly predicted evaluations, and abandonment sharply reduced perceived commitment; prior‐vote cues were weak and did not mediate effects. Together, the studies indicate that people take continuation to signal commitment and view it positively, whereas abandonment stopping signals a breach of commitment and is penalized. Apparent sunk‐cost sensitivity in politics may often reflect a premium on consistency rather than waste aversion. We discuss implications for designing “face‐saving” off‐ramps and reframing evidence‐based reversals as fidelity to higher order commitments.

Philosophy & Public Affairs

Epistocracy and the Commitment Problem

Simon Goldstein

Full text
The epistocracy debate has turned largely on the character of the electorate—what voters know, whether competence filters can be designed well, and what filtered selection produces. Another strand has focused on the legitimacy of epistocratic justifications. I ask instead about the equilibrium effects of the institution. Epistocracy shifts some political authority from the median voter toward citizens selected on competence grounds, and existing critiques press legitimational, epistemic, abuse‐based, and demographic objections against such proposals. I develop a different objection, focusing on one especially plausible form of epistocracy: Jason Brennan's epistocratic veto , a competence‐selected council empowered to override legislation it judges incompetent. Drawing on Acemoglu and Robinson's account of democratization, I argue that the franchise solves a credible‐commitment problem between elites and the poor under conditions of revolutionary threat: universal suffrage puts redistributive authority in majority hands in a way elites cannot easily reverse, because reversing it requires revoking the franchise itself. The institutionally realistic veto hands some of that authority back to a body whose preferences systematically diverge from the median voter's, reintroducing the commitment problem suffrage solved. I give empirical and theoretical reasons to worry that the magnitude of the problem could potentially be significant, while its actual size (and its comparison with democracy's own commitment problems) remains an open empirical question. A formal appendix maps the veto to Acemoglu and Robinson's imperfect‐democracy parameter and shows that the range of conditions under which democratic institutions avert revolution strictly shrinks.

Politics and Justice: Aristotle, Rawls, and the Case for Realism in Political Theory

Paul Sagar

Full text
Aristotle's analysis of stasis in Book V of the Politics offers a striking example of “distinctively political thought,” as advocated by Bernard Williams's political realism, and in contrast to the moralist approach exemplified by John Rawls. Aristotle identifies disputes over justice, driven by partial and conflicting conceptions of equality, as a principal cause of civil conflict, advocating institutional measures to manage such disagreements rather than relying on citizens converging on a correct understanding. This focus on stability as the first function of politics, aimed at preventing constitutional breakdown, parallels Williams's “first political question” and emphasizes the pragmatic safeguarding of order to avert catastrophe. By contrast, Rawls treats politics primarily as a means to realizing justice, positing that stability depends on an overlapping consensus. The paper critiques Rawls's neglect of the historical‐institutional foundations of liberalism, his underestimation of the durability of modi vivendi, and his ideal‐theoretic abstraction from the circumstances of politics. Aristotle's realist alternative provides a more plausible framework for understanding the relationship between justice and politics—a necessary corrective to Rawlsian political moralism.

Voting for populism? How ‘populist’ labels affect support for candidates

Nicolas Bichay

Full text
Many consider the rise of populism to be a major threat to liberal democracy today. At the same time, voters have given considerable support to such parties and politicians, with their average vote-share quadrupling in the last thirty years. Thus, while research realizes the potential hostility populism poses to the polity at large, it is unclear what connotations of the ideology exist to voters. Do voters recognize they are voting for a politician considered to be averse to democracy? Do they simply not care? I field a randomized survey experiment of 2099 respondents in the United Kingdom to measure (1) the effect labeling a politician ‘populist’ may have on their level of support, and (2) voters’ perception of the link between populism and democracy. Results suggest voters’ perception of ‘populist’ labels are largely dependent on the candidate’s left-right ideology, as well as the level of populist ideology of the voter.

Affective, but not ideological, polarization increases tolerance for undemocratic behavior

Jordi Muñoz, Francesc Amat, Albert Falcó-Gimeno, Marc Guinjoan, Toni Rodon

Full text
Over the last years, there have been frequent claims that democracy may be endangered by polarization, which has the potential to push voters and elites to embrace undemocratic practices and hence pave the way for democratic backsliding. But what is more decisive to generate tolerance to such practices: ideological or affective proximity? This article examines the effect of ideological vis-à-vis affective polarization on support for undemocratic practices. By using a conjoint experiment fielded in Catalonia in a sample of citizens and politicians, we find that both voters and elites overall reject politicians with undemocratic attitudes in a sizable and consistent way. Among citizens, while ideological proximity does not mitigate punishment of undemocratic attitudes, affective polarization does: individuals with a more intense affection toward their candidate’s party are less prone to punishing him/her for eroding the losers’ consent. The article has important implications for our understanding of the relationship between affective and ideological polarization, and support for democratic undoing.

Macrodiscontent across countries

Haofeng Ma, Jeongho Choi, Yuehong Cassandra Tai, Yue Hu, Frederick Solt

Full text
Public discontent with the political system has become an increasingly salient concern in recent years, with the argument that it undermines democratic stability and effective governance. Nevertheless, the understanding of the nature, trends, and drivers of political discontent remain debated, largely reflecting the constraints from available survey data and items in the construction of measurement. This article takes advantage of state-of-the-art latent-variable modeling to aggregate survey responses and a comprehensive collection of survey data to generate dynamic comparative estimates of public political discontent (PPD) for 136 countries and regions over 56 years (1968–2023). These PPD scores are validated with responses to the individual source-data survey items that were used to generate them as well as the democratic evaluation survey item that was not used in our estimation. Next, a cross-national and longitudinal analysis of PPD in advanced democracies (i.e., OECD countries) highlights that PPD has been on a rising trend, rather than merely trendless fluctuations. Our results reveal that these increased discontents are largely attributable to worsening economic conditions, including low average income, slow growth, and high unemployment rates.

Shades of Negativity in European Parliament Debates: Exploring the Affective Dimension of Political Conflict in the EU’s Rule of Law Crisis

Lise Esther Herman, Julian Hoerner, Joseph Lacey

Full text
This article seeks to explore the nature and determinants of political conflict in European Parliament (EP) debates within the unique context of the Rule of Law Crisis (RoLC). While previous research on this topic has mainly studied the ideological dimension of this conflict, we shift focus to the affective dimension, analysing the roles of negative sentiment and motive-based criticism through a quantitative analysis of 53 hand-coded debates. We find that negativity dominates, and that this tone is adopted by Members of the EP (MEPs) irrespective of the ideological orientation or geographical origin of their national party. However, concerning levels of motive-based cynicism are primarily driven by MEPs from more right-wing, Eurosceptic and, to a lesser extent, Central and Eastern European parties. These findings suggest that the EP, an institution traditionally geared towards compromise-building, is becoming increasingly susceptible to the normative jeopardies of negative and attack politics when it comes to the RoLC.

Rethinking Pathways of Autocratisation and Democratic Resilience in Comparative Perspective

Nic Cheeseman, Marie-Eve Desrosiers, Edem E. Selormey

Full text
This review article examines the rise of democratic resilience as a central concept in the comparative study of regime stability and change. Over the past decade, resilience – understood not simply as institutional survival but as the capacity of democratic systems to adapt, regenerate and deepen democratic norms in the face of crises – has gained prominence in response to accelerating autocratisation. The article situates this shift within the diverse pathways through which democracies weaken and decline, including executive aggrandisement and growing political polarisation. While the existing literature has improved our understanding of autocratisation, much comparative research remains influenced by a relatively narrow set of Northern cases. As a result, it risks treating regional experiences as universal. Drawing on evidence from Africa, Asia and other parts of the Global South, the article argues for a comparative theory of democratic resilience that incorporates state capture, rapid authoritarian reversals and more diverse forms of aggrandisement.

Political Theory

Care and Contemporary Violence: Deconstructing the Monstrous in/as Cavarero’s Horrorism

Clare Woodford, Michelle Bentley

Full text
This article offers a feminist reworking of Adriana Cavarero’s conceptualization of horrorism as a form of extreme violence that targets and dehumanizes its victims through dismemberment and an ontological assault on human uniqueness. While Cavarero’s intervention is motivated by a commitment to resisting patriarchal logics of war and reorienting political thought toward care, we argue that her framework depends on an overly fixed opposition between the monstrous perpetrator and the defenseless victim, exemplified in her reading of Medusa in contrast with the Christian Madonna. The binary risks reproducing the very logics of exclusion and moral absolutism it seeks to disrupt. Demonstrating the point, we engage in a critical rereading of Medusa across classical and later interpretive traditions that demonstrates how she cannot be reduced to an emblem of absolute monstrosity, but instead appears as a plural figure: victim, goddess, protector, and generative presence—as well as a destructive monster. This reading destabilizes Cavarero’s conceptualization of both horrorism and victimhood, revealing the political and narrative instability of these categories. In reaction, we develop a feminist framework for responding to contemporary violence that shifts analysis away from moralized typologies toward a relational and narratively mediated understanding of violence. This approach retains Cavarero’s commitments to relationality and uniqueness, but rejects the closure produced by the neologism “horrorism,” instead foregrounding the entanglement of care and violence and the contested processes through which both are recognized and named.

Moderation or Amplification? Mapping Speech Distinctiveness Among Green Parties Using a Machine Learning Approach

Clint Claessen, Denise Traber, Martijn Schoonvelde

Full text
How do issue‐owning parties adapt their rhetoric when transitioning from opposition to government? Focusing on the Green parties in Germany and Ireland, we analyze legislative debates to trace how these parties seek to maintain ownership of environmental issues. Using more than 1.7 million parliamentary speeches from the German Bundestag (1991–2018) and the Irish Dáil (1992–2013), we apply a fictitious prediction problem approach implemented as a multiclass text‐classification task to measure distinctiveness in parties' legislative speeches. Our findings show that both parties increased their rhetorical distinctiveness on environmental issues during their first time in government, but with key differences: the Irish Greens maintained a distinct profile throughout their term, while the German Greens' distinctiveness increased as elections neared. No such patterns emerge for welfare, a non‐owned issue. Our study offers a framework and a transferable method for studying how smaller parties navigate between coalition influence and visibility through the issues they own.

The Legislative Effectiveness of Congressional Oversight Reports

Sean M. Theriault

Full text
This article assesses the linkage between the House and Senate oversight committees' reports and subsequent legislative efforts in those policy areas from the 106th to the 116th Congresses (1999–2020). The analysis finds support for the three proffered hypotheses. First, the policy topics covered in the reports spur the referral of bills on those topics to the oversight committees. Second, the oversight reports on topics that the majority staff releases are more reflected in downstream legislative activity than the topics covered in the minority staff reports. Finally, the majority's advantage in pushing legislation related to its oversight reports' topics is greater in the House than it is in the Senate. These initial findings substantiate the importance of gathering and coding congressional oversight reports for not only how Congress conducts oversight, but also how it legislates.

Success and failure in complex collective decision making: The Random Exchange Decide model

Frans N Stokman, Jelmer Draaijer, Jacob Dijkstra, Marcel ALM van Assen

Full text
We present the Random Exchange model, an extension of the bilateral exchange model of collective decision-making of Stokman and Van Oosten. Compared to more elementary models, the Random Exchange model generates predictions of the process leading to the outcome and enhances understanding of deadlock or difficulties in the negotiation process. After explaining its logic, the Random Exchange model is applied to collective decision-making in the international setting of two climate agreements, chaired by the European Union; the 2009 Climate Conference of Parties (COP) in Copenhagen, and the 2015 COP in Paris. The applications show how Random Exchange model not only may be used to predict collective decision-making outcomes but also deadlock when powerful actors attach high relative saliences to at least two issues on which they have opposite positions.

Contemplating Conquest: Public Opinion on the Forcible Seizure of Territory

Amnon Cavari, Asif Efrat, Omer Yair

Full text
The norm against conquest—a central tenet of the international order—is eroding. This study is among the first to examine public support for this norm, using a real-world case: Israel’s seizure of Syrian territory following the collapse of the Assad regime in December 2024. Our survey experiment in Israel reveals that citizens are sensitive to the prohibition on conquest. They demonstrate greater support for an occupation that involves only military control of the forcibly seized territory and weaker support for civilian settlement in or annexation of the foreign territory: policies that amount to conquest. Our finding—a stronger preference for legally acceptable occupation over illegal settlement or annexation—sends a positive signal for the constraining power of norms.

Museums of Eurowhiteness or museums of migration? Exploring the geographies of racialization through migration museums

Tina Magazzini

Full text
This article explores the opportunities and challenges of Migration Museums as sites of knowledge-production and representation. Building upon contemporary debates that connect migration studies with research on race and ethnicity, I first provide an overview of which museums exist worldwide that are explicitly framed as ‘museums of migration’, meaning that their mission and permanent expositions concern migration (be it emigration or immigration), and that receive some kind of public funding or support. After tracing the emergence of 15 museums that respond to these criteria, I look at how these institutes understand and narrate migration and migrants, and how such narrations have changed over time. I employ a global blackness framework as a tool to reflect on the entanglements of race, racism, colonialism and migration, and on the existing absences in the representation of migration narratives. One pattern that emerges is that most migration museums worldwide have so far mainly centered the experiences of white European migrants, leaving other migration narratives and experiences untold. Yet a second pattern testifies to a shift in focus over the past decades, with museums acknowledging that many communities have been traditionally invisibilized, and increasingly taking some remedial actions to incorporate the lived experiences of indigenous, racialized and migranticized populations in their permanent exhibitions.

Words with Consequences: The 2016 Election and International Student Visa Demand in the United States

Jamie Sharpe

Full text
This paper examines whether the 2016 US presidential election altered international student mobility by transforming campaign rhetoric into a credible signal of future policy and social risk. Using country–year data on F-1 visa issuances and active international student enrollments, the analysis studies how international students from countries explicitly targeted during the campaign responded to the election outcome. Two-way fixed effects models and event study specifications are used to trace both average post-election effects and their dynamic evolution. The results indicate a sharp post-election reversal in new international student inflows among targeted countries. Event study estimates show that, prior to 2016, F-1 visa issuances from Muslim-majority and Arab World countries grew faster than those from a plausibly less-affected comparison group. This upward trajectory reverses in FY2017, with visa issuances falling and remaining persistently lower in subsequent years. In contrast, active international student enrollments exhibit substantially more muted responses, reflecting the stock nature of enrollment outcomes and the continued presence of cohorts admitted prior to the election. Together, these findings indicate that the election primarily affected entry decisions rather than inducing immediate exits among enrolled students.

The Quest for Freedom and Migration Aspirations: Evidence from the MENA Region

Réda Marakbi, Hajare El Hadri

Full text
This article develops and empirically tests a theory of migration under repression. It argues that migration aspirations are shaped by perceived freedom repression and that such perceptions can make exit more attractive when voice appears blocked, risky, or ineffective. The theoretical framework yields four hypotheses, which we test using Arab Barometer data from 2021 to 2024 covering about 34,000 respondents in 12 Middle East and North Africa (MENA) countries. Empirically, we construct a Freedom Repression Index and examine how perceived repression shapes migration aspirations across different freedom dimensions and migration routes. Four findings stand out. First, perceived repression significantly increases migration aspirations, with substantive effects comparable to standard economic push factors. Second, the association is strongest for personal liberties, especially freedom of expression and freedom of religion, whereas restrictions on collective political rights such as protest are less strongly associated with migration aspirations. Third, repression shifts intentions toward irregular rather than regular migration routes. Fourth, this relationship is context-dependent; it is strongest in stable yet restrictive settings and weakens in conflict-affected contexts where insecurity dominates. By disaggregating freedom dimensions and distinguishing regular from irregular intentions, the article connects exit-voice theory to micro-level evidence and clarifies when, which, and how perceived repression shapes migration aspirations in the MENA region.

Journal of Refugee Studies

Love in a “hopeless” place? Courtship, intimacy, and romance in displacement

Philipp Schulz

Full text
Even in the midst of war and displacement, love persists. Yet, these lived realities remain mostly absent from refugee studies and from the vast body of literature on mass internal displacement and encampment in northern Uganda specifically. In this article, I document different narratives of romance, intimacy, love, and compassion in the context of Ugandan IDP camps and situate those accounts alongside a recognition of the harms caused by a decade of mass forced displacement, including their profound disruption of social-moral-intimate orders. Drawing on ethnographic insights, the article offers two key observations and arguments: I show that relations of love can be remarkably resilient, resistant, and adaptive, even in the face of adversity and despite divergences between lived realities and idealized notions. I also demonstrate how love and intimacy in the midst of violence can facilitate hope, perspective, and survival for displaced people, attesting to the power of love.

‘Duty-bearers’ in the governance of durable solutions for internally displaced persons in Ethiopia: an actor-oriented approach

Alemayehu B Hordofa

Full text
The notion of duty-bearers in forced displacement research is often confined to states. Yet, the responsibilities of humanitarian organizations in the governance of durable solutions for internally displaced persons remain scant in academic scholarship. This study goes beyond a formal and legalistic understanding of duty-bearers’ responsibilities and explores affected communities’ perspectives in the displacement context in the Tigray region, Ethiopia. Based on ethnographic interviews and focus group discussions conducted in Tigray and Addis Ababa in 2024 and 2025 and grounded in an actor-oriented approach, the study argues for moving from the top-down imposition of norms to value the affected community’s agency in shaping duty-bearer norms from below. This is in opposition to commonly held notions that duty-bearer norms are merely a top-down imposition by formal state-sanctioned rules and instead advances a multiple duty-bearer framework, with empirical evidence from the ground where accountability of duty-bearers is claimed and contested.

‘It felt wrong’: emotional labour and the everyday reconfiguration of refugee governance in Cameroon

Claire Lefort-Rieu

Full text
This article examines how emotional labour shapes the everyday implementation of forced migration governance. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork in Cameroon, it explores how refugee aid providers’ emotional responses—including frustration, empathy, and ethical discomfort—inform situated forms of judgement in their daily work. Building on the emotional turn in the social sciences, emotions are conceptualized as epistemic dispositions through which actors interpret institutional tensions and, in some cases, act upon them. The analysis identifies three pathways through which emotional labour contributes to reconfiguring refugee assistance from within: pragmatic adaptations that soften bureaucratic rigidity; improvised forms of inclusion and relational alliances that challenge categorical boundaries; and structural engagements that support alignment with state institutions. These findings show how emotional labour makes visible institutional contradictions and informs practical adjustments, while remaining shaped by organizational constraints and unequal power relations, offering a more situated and embodied understanding of refugee governance in practice.

Homes for Ukraine: Understanding the Geography of Ukrainian Refugees and Hosts in Great Britain

Hannah Slocombe, Kathy Burrell

Full text
The escalation of conflict in Ukraine in March 2022 triggered the mass displacement of Ukrainian people. In response, the UK government launched the Homes for Ukraine scheme, a private hosting initiative that urged members of the public to temporarily host refugees in their homes. As of December 2024, around 194,800 Ukrainian refugees had been issued visas through the scheme. Despite the significance of the scheme, little is known about the geography of refugees and hosts, factors that have influenced this geography, and how this geographical distribution shaped the temporary integration of Ukrainian refugees. This paper employs a mixed‐methods design, integrating qualitative interviews with Ukrainian refugees and those hosting them, with spatial mapping and regression modelling, to examine the geography of the Homes for Ukraine scheme. Our findings demonstrate that while hosting was geographically widespread, there were distinct regional trends, with more Ukrainian refugees being hosted in southern England. Various factors were associated with this distinct geography, including average income levels, as well as the availability of spare rooms. Finally, we show that many areas home to higher rates of Ukrainian refugees pose challenges for their temporary integration due to higher housing costs, as well as limited availability of social housing. These findings contribute to wider understandings of migration and refuge by demonstrating the significance of place‐based attributes in not only determining where displaced people are able to move but also in shaping their temporary integration into hosting areas.

From Integration Deficit to Imbalanced Integration: Digital–Social Mechanisms Across Places and Migrants' Mental Health

Hanbei Cheng

Full text
Migrants' mental health has become an increasing public health concern amid persistent social and cultural displacement. Although social integration is widely recognised as a key determinant of mental health, existing research has largely focused on integration deficits, while overlooking imbalanced forms of integration. We address this gap by examining superficial integration, in which frequent face‐to‐face interactions outpace migrants' sense of belonging. Using original survey data from Wuhan and multigroup structural equation modelling (MG‐SEM), we investigated how social integration influences migrants' mental health through digital‐social processes across generations. We found that the mental health consequences of social integration depend not on social interaction or belonging alone, but on their social asymmetries. Frequent interactions become psychologically detrimental when they are not accompanied by a corresponding sense of belonging, revealing the adverse effects of superficial integration. We further identified two complementary mechanisms underlying this relationship. First, social media engagement intensifies the health consequences of social integration by strengthening both the benefits of belonging and the psychological costs of interaction. Second, a stronger destination‐city belonging reduces perceived relative deprivation with respect to migrants' places of origin, thereby improving mental health, particularly among younger migrants. By moving beyond the deficit‐based view of migrant integration, this study highlights the importance of examining how integration unfolds unevenly across social, digital, and spatial contexts. Our findings call for policies that go beyond increasing social contact and instead promote meaningful, emotionally supportive, and locally embedded forms of integration.

A Biographical Approach to Understand Long‐Term Occupational Trajectories of Immigrants in the UK Labour Market

Michael Mahony, Francisco Rowe

Full text
This study adopts a biographical approach to examine migrants' integration experiences within the UK labour market. It employs monthly retrospective data from the UK Household Longitudinal Study (UKHLS) and the British Household Panel Survey (BHPS) to identify distinct occupational trajectories experienced by migrants who arrived in the UK between 1927 and 2016. We identified five representative trajectories capturing labour market stability (i.e. stable paid employment, stable self‐employment, and stable family care), precarity (transitional unemployment) and enhancement (investing in education). Overall, these trajectories evidence that a high proportion of UK migrants experienced successful integration, characterised by fast and sustainable labour market entry, while only a small percentage (2.36%) experienced transitional unemployment. EU nationals were substantially more likely than non‐EU migrants to experience this success, despite being less educated on average and entering without prior job offers. Our analysis also revealed that gender, education, age at arrival, and ethnicity all significantly influence the integration process. They interact in unique ways to influence the probability of immigrants embarking on one of the identified trajectories. High national unemployment during migrants' year of arrival was identified as a consistent factor of labour market exclusion, indicating that initial inactivity significantly increased migrants' vulnerability to future economic scarring.

Imagined Majorities: Perceived Group Size and Population Replacement Beliefs in Turkey

Evren Balta, Ezgi Elçi, Deniz Sert

Full text
Population‐replacement beliefs are often treated as an intensified form of demographic misperception. This article instead argues that they comprise three distinct layers: perceptions of the current migrant population size, expectations of future growth and the attribution of deliberate political agency. Using an original nationally representative survey in Turkey ( N = 1136), we test whether these layers form a single attitude or operate independently. Respondents overestimate the migrant population and expect further growth, yet current misperception does not independently predict belief in deliberate replacement. Projected growth is more strongly associated with replacement belief, while partisanship structures the attribution of hostile intent. Overestimation is concentrated among far‐right voters, whereas replacement belief extends more broadly across opposition constituencies. Socio‐demographic effects are weaker and less consistent. The findings show that replacement belief is not simply a mistaken assessment of present demography. It is a future‐oriented political interpretation that links anticipated group change to intentional agency. By demonstrating this layered structure in a majority‐Muslim refugee host, the article also shows that replacement belief is not confined to Western white‐majority settings.
